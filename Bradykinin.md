# Involvement of Bradykinin (BK) and Kinin Receptors (B1/B2) in Neuropathic Pain

Bradykinin (BK) is a nine‑amino‑acid endogenous peptide (Arg‑Pro‑Pro‑Gly‑Phe‑Ser‑Pro‑Phe‑Arg) that belongs to the kinin family of vasoactive peptides. It is generated from high‑molecular‑weight kininogen (HK) through the sequential action of plasma kallikrein or tissue kallikrein, which are themselves activated by the contact system or by proteases released during tissue injury. Bradykinin was originally identified as a potent vasodilator and mediator of acute inflammation, but it is now recognised as one of the most powerful endogenous **algogenic** (pain‑producing) substances known. In neuropathic pain, the kallikrein–kinin system (KKS) is pathologically upregulated at the site of peripheral nerve injury, in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), and in the spinal dorsal horn, where bradykinin engages two distinct G‑protein‑coupled receptors—the constitutively expressed **B2 receptor (B2R)** and the injury‑inducible **B1 receptor (B1R)** —to drive nociceptor sensitisation, central sensitisation, neuroinflammation, and the transition from acute to chronic pain. Pharmacological blockade of B1 and/or B2 receptors consistently attenuates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and cold allodynia in a wide range of preclinical neuropathic pain models. However, the clinical translation of kinin receptor antagonists has been challenging: a Phase 2a clinical trial of the B1 receptor antagonist **fulmetibant** in diabetic neuropathic pain failed to demonstrate significant analgesic efficacy despite robust preclinical data. These findings underscore both the promise and the complexity of the kinin system as a therapeutic target for chronic pain.

## 🧬 The Kallikrein–Kinin System: Peptides, Receptors, and Normal Functions

*   **Gene and peptide family**: The kinin family comprises the nonapeptide **bradykinin (BK)** and the decapeptide **kallidin (Lys‑BK)** . Both are derived from kininogens, which are large plasma proteins synthesised predominantly by the liver. Bradykinin is generated by the action of plasma kallikrein on high‑molecular‑weight kininogen (HK), while kallidin is produced by tissue kallikrein acting on low‑molecular‑weight kininogen (LK). Kallidin can be further processed by aminopeptidases to yield bradykinin. The biologically active kinins have extremely short half‑lives (seconds) in the circulation due to rapid degradation by kininases, primarily **angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE)** and neutral endopeptidase (neprilysin).
*   **Biosynthesis and activation in the nervous system**: In the context of nerve injury, the KKS is locally activated at multiple levels. Damaged cells release proteases that convert prekallikrein to kallikrein, which then cleaves kininogen to generate bradykinin. Additionally, bradykinin itself can be generated within the spinal cord in response to nociceptor inputs, where it acts as a **synaptic neuromodulator**. The production of bradykinin in the spinal cord following intense nociceptive stimulation represents a feed‑forward mechanism that amplifies pain signals.
*   **Receptors**:
    *   **B2 receptor (B2R)** : A Gαq/11‑coupled receptor (also coupling to Gαi and Gα12/13) that is **constitutively expressed** in most tissues under physiological conditions. B2R has high affinity for intact bradykinin and kallidin. It mediates the acute, short‑lasting effects of kinins, including vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and the initial excitation of nociceptors. B2R undergoes rapid desensitisation and internalisation upon agonist binding, a feature that limits the duration of its signalling. B2Rs are expressed on:
        *   **Primary sensory neurons** of the DRG and trigeminal ganglia, particularly in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. B2R is the predominant functional bradykinin receptor subtype in these sensory neurons.
        *   **Spinal dorsal horn neurons**, where they are present both presynaptically (on primary afferent terminals) and postsynaptically.
        *   **Satellite glial cells** in the DRG and **Schwann cells** in the peripheral nerve.
        *   **Endothelial cells**, where they mediate vasodilation via nitric oxide and prostacyclin release.
    *   **B1 receptor (B1R)** : A Gαq/11‑coupled receptor that is **absent or expressed at very low levels in healthy tissues** but is rapidly and robustly upregulated following tissue injury, inflammation, and nerve damage. B1R is selectively activated by the des‑Arg⁹ metabolites of bradykinin (des‑Arg⁹‑BK) and kallidin (des‑Arg¹⁰‑kallidin), which are generated by the removal of the C‑terminal arginine by carboxypeptidases. Unlike B2R, B1R **does not desensitise** and mediates sustained, prolonged signalling, making it particularly relevant to chronic pain. B1R expression is induced on:
        *   **DRG neurons** (upregulated after nerve injury)
        *   **Spinal cord** neurons and glial cells
        *   **Peripheral nerve** Schwann cells and infiltrating immune cells
        *   **Satellite glial cells**
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **Peripheral nerve**: In the injured nerve, bradykinin is generated from kininogen by kallikreins released from damaged cells and activated immune cells. Both B1 and B2 receptors are upregulated in the ipsilateral spinal nerve and hind paw skin after spinal nerve ligation.
    *   **DRG**: B2R is constitutively expressed and is the predominant functional bradykinin receptor in sensory and sympathetic ganglia. B1R mRNA is expressed in both sympathetic and sensory ganglia and is markedly upregulated following nerve injury. In human DRG tissue from organ donors, B2 and, to a lesser extent, B1 receptors are expressed by DRG neurons and satellite glial cells, with B2 receptors enriched in the nociceptor subpopulation. Notably, a donor's history of chronic pain and age may be predictors of higher B1 receptor expression in human DRG neurons.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: B2Rs are expressed on dorsal horn neurons in laminae I–II. B1R expression is upregulated in the spinal cord after partial sciatic nerve ligation.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: B1 and B2 receptors are widely distributed throughout the brain, including the periaqueductal grey, hypothalamus, and brainstem nuclei involved in descending pain modulation.
*   **Physiological functions**: Under normal conditions, kinins contribute to the regulation of vascular tone, blood pressure, renal function, and inflammation. B2R is the primary mediator of the physiological effects of kinins, while B1R is largely silent until induced by injury. In nociception, kinins function as powerful pronociceptive mediators that directly excite nociceptors, sensitise them to other stimuli, and promote central sensitisation.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of Bradykinin in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury, the KKS is activated at multiple anatomical levels, and bradykinin contributes to the generation and maintenance of neuropathic pain through a combination of direct nociceptor excitation and sensitisation, potentiation of central glutamatergic transmission, and promotion of neuroinflammation.

### 1. Direct Excitation and Sensitisation of Nociceptors via B2 Receptors

Bradykinin is a potent direct activator of nociceptive primary afferent neurons. The acute excitatory and sensitising effects of BK are primarily mediated by the **constitutively expressed B2 receptor** and involve multiple intracellular signalling cascades.

*   **B2R‑mediated excitation of nociceptors**: Activation of B2R (a Gαq/11‑coupled receptor) on nociceptor terminals triggers phospholipase C (PLC)‑mediated hydrolysis of PIP₂, generating **IP₃** and **diacylglycerol (DAG)** . IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores, and DAG activates **protein kinase C (PKC)** . This signalling leads to the excitation of nociceptors through the inhibition of **M‑type K⁺ channels** and the activation of **Ca²⁺‑activated Cl⁻ channels**, resulting in membrane depolarisation and action potential firing.
*   **Acute pain‑producing effects**: Intraplantar injection of bradykinin in rodents or intradermal injection in human volunteers produces spontaneous, short‑lasting pain, confirming that BK is an endogenous algogen in both species. In human volunteers, BK activates and sensitises mechanosensitive C‑nociceptors.
*   **TRPV1 sensitisation via B2R–PKCε pathway**: One of the most extensively characterised mechanisms of BK‑induced heat hyperalgesia is the sensitisation of the **transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)** channel. BK, acting through B2R, activates PKCε, which directly phosphorylates TRPV1 at **Ser⁸⁰⁰** (and other sites). This phosphorylation dramatically lowers the thermal activation threshold of TRPV1 from noxious (>43 °C) to physiological skin temperatures (~35‑37 °C). Consequently, TRPV1 becomes active at body temperature, generating ongoing nociceptor firing and contributing to spontaneous burning pain and thermal hyperalgesia.
*   **TRPA1 sensitisation**: In the context of chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy (e.g., cisplatin), the B2R mediates the sensitisation of **TRPA1** channels through intracellular kinase pathways involving PLC and PKCε. This contributes to both mechanical and cold allodynia. Antagonists for kinin B2 and B1 receptors and the TRPA1 channel attenuate these painful parameters.
*   **EP4 receptor‑dependent sensitisation**: BK‑mediated sensitisation of TRPV1‑mediated calcium responses in rat DRG neurons is dependent on **cyclooxygenase‑1 (COX‑1)** activity and utilises a pathway that involves **EP4 (prostaglandin E₂ receptor subtype 4)** , but not EP3 receptors. This provides evidence for a signalling pathway through which bradykinin may regulate TRPV1 ion channel function via EP4 receptors.
*   **Mechanical hyperalgesia via TRPV4**: Bradykinin, through both B1 and B2 receptors, sensitises the mechanosensitive channel **TRPV4**, contributing to the mechanical hyperalgesia observed in neuropathic pain conditions, including paclitaxel‑induced peripheral neuropathy.
*   **Modulation of voltage‑gated sodium channels**: BK, via PKC‑ and PKA‑dependent phosphorylation, enhances the activity of **Nav1.7**, **Nav1.8**, and **Nav1.9** in DRG neurons, increasing sodium current density, lowering the action potential threshold, and promoting ectopic firing.

### 2. Spinal Central Sensitisation via B2 Receptors

Beyond its peripheral actions, bradykinin is released in the spinal cord in response to nociceptor inputs and acts as a **synaptic neuromodulator** to produce central sensitisation and pain hypersensitivity.

*   **B2R expression in the spinal dorsal horn**: The B2 kinin receptor is expressed on rat dorsal horn neurons.
*   **Potentiation of glutamatergic synaptic transmission**: Bradykinin, a B2‑specific agonist, augments AMPA‑ and NMDA‑induced, and primary afferent‑evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs). It also increases the frequency and amplitude of miniature EPSCs in superficial dorsal horn neurons. This occurs through presynaptic enhancement of glutamate release and postsynaptic potentiation of glutamate receptor function.
*   **NMDA receptor‑dependent hyperalgesia**: Administration of bradykinin to the spinal cord *in vivo* produces an **NMDA receptor‑dependent hyperalgesia**. Intrathecal administration of a B2‑selective antagonist suppresses behavioural manifestations of central sensitisation, an activity‑dependent increase in glutamatergic synaptic efficacy. Primary afferent‑evoked central sensitisation is also reduced in B2 receptor knockout mice. Bradykinin potentiates synaptic glutamate release and action in the spinal cord via presynaptic and postsynaptic B2 receptors, contributing thereby to activity‑dependent central sensitisation and pain hypersensitivity.
*   **Multiple kinase activation**: BK enhances AMPA and NMDA receptor activity in spinal cord dorsal horn neurons by activating multiple kinases, including **PKC**, **PKA**, and **extracellular signal‑regulated kinase (ERK)** , which converge on the phosphorylation of NMDA receptor subunits (particularly GluN2B) and AMPA receptor subunits (GluA1), increasing their conductance and synaptic trafficking.

### 3. The B1 Receptor: Inducible Mediator of Chronic Pain

The B1 receptor is uniquely positioned to drive the transition from acute to chronic neuropathic pain due to its injury‑inducible expression, resistance to desensitisation, and sustained signalling capacity.

*   **Induction after nerve injury**: B1R mRNA is upregulated in the ipsilateral paw, sciatic nerve, and spinal cord of wild‑type mice after partial sciatic nerve ligation. In the rat spinal nerve ligation (SNL) model, B1R protein expression is enhanced in the ipsilateral L4–L6 spinal nerve and hind paw skin.
*   **B1R activation is essential to neuropathic pain development**: Mice lacking the gene for the B1 receptor (B1R knockout) exhibit a significant reduction in the early stages of mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia after partial sciatic nerve ligation. Furthermore, systemic treatment with the B1‑selective receptor antagonist **des‑Arg⁹‑[Leu⁸]‑bradykinin** reduces established mechanical allodynia observed 7–28 days after nerve lesion in wild‑type mice. These findings indicate that **kinin B1 receptor activation seems to be essential to neuropathic pain development**, suggesting that an oral‑selective B1 receptor antagonist might have therapeutic potential in the management of chronic pain.
*   **Switching of bradykinin‑mediated nociception after nerve injury**: Following partial sciatic nerve injury in mice, there is a switch in the receptor and fibre subtypes mediating BK nociception. This switch, from a predominantly B2R‑mediated, acute pain mechanism to one that also involves B1R and different fibre populations, may contribute to the pathobiology of neuropathic pain.

### 4. Contribution to Chemotherapy‑Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN)

The KKS is heavily implicated in the painful peripheral neuropathy caused by chemotherapeutic agents, including cisplatin, paclitaxel, and vincristine.

*   **Cisplatin‑induced neuropathy**: Antagonists for kinin B2 and B1 receptors attenuate cisplatin‑induced mechanical and cold allodynia in mice. The B2R mediates cisplatin‑induced painful peripheral neuropathy through intracellular kinase pathways and **TRPA1 channel sensitisation**, with PLC and PKCε as critical downstream effectors.
*   **Paclitaxel‑induced neuropathy**: Both B1 and B2 receptors contribute to mechanical hyperalgesia in paclitaxel‑treated mice through the sensitisation of **TRPV4** channels. The selective TRPV4 antagonist HC‑067047 inhibits the mechanical hyperalgesia induced by both a B2R agonist (bradykinin) and a B1R agonist (des‑Arg⁹‑BK).
*   **Vincristine‑induced neuropathy**: B1 and B2 receptors are engaged in the transmission of nociceptive stimuli in toxic neuropathy, and their antagonists reduce hyperalgesia.

### 5. Diabetic Neuropathy

*   **B1 receptor in diabetic hyperalgesia**: The kinin system mediates hyperalgesia through the inducible bradykinin B1 receptor subtype in both type 1 and type 2 diabetic neuropathy models. The B1R antagonist **R‑954** inhibits oxidative stress and restores Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase activity in the diabetic rat peripheral nervous system. The BKB1‑R subtype is overexpressed in the sciatic nerve during the development of streptozotocin‑induced diabetes.
*   **B2 receptor**: Together with B1 receptors, B2 receptors are engaged in the transmission of nociceptive stimuli in diabetic hyperalgesia.
*   **Electroacupuncture**: Alleviates diabetes‑induced mechanical allodynia and downregulates bradykinin B1 receptor expression in the spinal cord dorsal horn.

### 6. Spinal Cord Injury‑Induced Pain

*   Bradykinin is one of the first compounds produced at the site of tissue injury and subsequently initiates a cascade of reactions that produce the cardinal features of inflammation. In spinal cord trauma and brain injury, kinins play a significant role in the pathophysiology, including the initiation of nociceptive pain.
*   Following spinal cord injury in rats, administration of a B1 receptor antagonist (Lys‑[Des‑Arg⁹,Leu⁸]‑bradykinin) produces antihyperalgesic effects, with the effect appearing more specific to the reversal of thermal hyperalgesia compared to a TRPV1 antagonist.

### 7. Neuroinflammation and Immune Cell Activation

Bradykinin promotes neuroinflammation through multiple mechanisms:
*   **Vasodilation and increased vascular permeability**: BK, acting on B2Rs on endothelial cells, stimulates the release of nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin, causing vasodilation and increased permeability of the blood‑nerve barrier. This facilitates the extravasation of plasma proteins and the infiltration of immune cells (neutrophils, macrophages) into the injured nerve and DRG.
*   **Immune cell activation**: BK is chemotactic for neutrophils and can stimulate the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6) from macrophages and mast cells. The resulting inflammatory milieu further sensitises nociceptors and contributes to the chronicity of neuropathic pain.
*   **Prostaglandin synthesis**: BK, via B2R, stimulates the activity of **phospholipase A₂**, releasing arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids and promoting the synthesis of **prostaglandins** (PGE₂, PGI₂) via cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes. PGE₂ is a powerful sensitising agent that acts on EP receptors on nociceptors to enhance TRPV1 and sodium channel activity.

### 8. Interaction with the Opioid System

Kinins also interact with the endogenous opioid system. In mice lacking both B1 and B2 receptors, peripheral μ‑ and δ‑opioid‑induced analgesia after chronic constriction injury was reduced by 30–35%, highlighting a **protective function of kinins via interactions with the opioid system**. This suggests that kinin receptors may tonically support opioid receptor signalling in the periphery, and complete kinin receptor blockade could paradoxically impair endogenous opioid analgesia.

### 9. Switching of Receptor Subtypes and Fibre Populations After Nerve Injury

In the uninjured state, BK‑mediated nociception is mediated primarily by B2 receptors on a subset of nociceptors. After partial sciatic nerve injury, there is a **switching** of both receptor subtype and sensory fibre type that mediates BK nociception. This phenotypic switch—involving the recruitment of B1 receptors and altered fibre populations—may contribute to the altered pharmacology of neuropathic pain and its relative resistance to B2‑selective antagonists alone. The B1 receptor, once induced, becomes the dominant mediator of sustained nociceptor sensitisation.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

A large and consistent body of pharmacological and genetic evidence demonstrates that targeting the kinin system alleviates neuropathic pain in multiple animal models.

### 1. Selective B1 Receptor Antagonists

*   **des‑Arg⁹‑[Leu⁸]‑bradykinin (DALBK)** : The prototypical peptide B1 receptor antagonist. Systemic treatment:
    *   Reduces established mechanical allodynia observed 7–28 days after partial sciatic nerve ligation in wild‑type mice.
    *   Selectively blocks responses triggered by the B1 agonist des‑Arg⁹‑bradykinin (DABK) in the SNL model.
    *   Alleviates, partially and transiently, established cold allodynia, heat hyperalgesia, and (to a lesser extent) tactile allodynia in rats subjected to L5/L6 SNL.
*   **R‑954**: A selective B1R antagonist that inhibits oxidative stress and restores Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase activity in the sciatic nerve of diabetic rats.
*   **Lys‑(Des‑Arg⁹,Leu⁸)‑bradykinin**: A B1 antagonist with antihyperalgesic effects following spinal cord injury in rats, particularly against thermal hyperalgesia.
*   **Fulmetibant (BAY 2395840)** : An orally bioavailable, selective B1 receptor antagonist that reached Phase 2a clinical trials for diabetic neuropathic pain. While robustly effective preclinically, the clinical trial did not demonstrate significant analgesic efficacy (see Clinical Implications).

### 2. Selective B2 Receptor Antagonists

*   **HOE 140 (Icatibant)** : A peptidic, highly selective B2 receptor antagonist that is clinically approved for the treatment of acute attacks of hereditary angioedema. In preclinical neuropathic pain models, HOE 140:
    *   Selectively blocks responses triggered by bradykinin in the SNL model.
    *   Alleviates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and cold allodynia in nerve‑injured rats.
    *   Suppresses behavioural manifestations of central sensitisation when administered intrathecally, confirming the role of spinal B2 receptors.
*   **Bradyzide**: A potent, non‑peptide B2 receptor antagonist with oral bioavailability. It has shown analgesic efficacy in preclinical inflammatory and neuropathic pain models.

### 3. Genetic Models

*   **B1 receptor knockout mice**: Exhibit a significant reduction in the early stages of mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia after partial sciatic nerve ligation, with no alteration in acute nociceptive thresholds. This confirms that B1R activation is essential for the development of neuropathic pain.
*   **B2 receptor knockout mice**: Primary afferent‑evoked central sensitisation is reduced in B2 receptor knockout mice. B2KO mice also show impaired inflammatory hyperalgesia.
*   **B1/B2 double knockout mice**: Mice lacking both B1 and B2 receptors have been studied to assess the overall contribution of kinins to pain. In these animals, baseline nociceptive responses to heat are unaltered, nocifensive responses to bradykinin are abolished, and acute inflammatory pain is reduced. However, **chronic neuropathic pain (CCI‑induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity) is unchanged** in double knockout mice, suggesting that kinin receptors are important for acute and short‑lasting inflammation but are **less essential in chronic stages of pain**, possibly due to compensatory engagement of other pronociceptive systems. Peripheral opioid analgesia is also reduced in these mice, highlighting the protective kinin–opioid interaction.
*   **Kininogen‑deficient rats**: Rats lacking kininogen (and thus unable to generate kinins) show reduced pain behaviour in the CCI model, with most rats showing some pain behaviour that is further reduced by B1 or B2 antagonists.

### 4. Kallikrein and ECE‑1 Inhibitors

Targeting the generation of bradykinin upstream of the receptors represents an alternative strategy:
*   Inhibitors of **tissue kallikrein** reduce kininogen cleavage and bradykinin production, attenuating pain in preclinical models.
*   **ACE inhibitors**, which block the degradation of bradykinin (as well as cleaving angiotensin I), can paradoxically increase bradykinin levels. This can contribute to the cough and angioedema side effects of ACE inhibitors but may also have implications for pain in specific contexts.

### 5. Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Electroacupuncture**: Alleviates diabetes‑induced mechanical allodynia and downregulates bradykinin B1 receptor expression in the spinal cord dorsal horn, contributing to its analgesic effect.
*   **Remimazolam**: A short‑acting benzodiazepine that has been shown to alleviate neuropathic pain via regulating bradykinin receptor B1 and autophagy, nominating B1R as a primary target for this drug.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Icatibant (Firazyr)** : A peptide B2 receptor antagonist approved for the acute treatment of attacks of hereditary angioedema (HAE). It is the only clinically used kinin receptor antagonist. In HAE, icatibant reduces swelling, inflammation, and pain caused by excessive bradykinin production. The fact that icatibant is well‑tolerated by patients with HAE provides proof of concept that kinin B2 receptor antagonism can be safely used in humans. A clinical trial investigating icatibant for sweating‑induced dermal pain has been conducted (NCT04153084). However, icatibant is a peptide requiring subcutaneous injection, limiting its utility for chronic, daily pain management.
*   **Fulmetibant (BAY 2395840) and the BRADiNP study**: The most significant clinical development for a kinin receptor antagonist in neuropathic pain has been the Phase 2a **BRADiNP study** (NCT05219812), a multicenter, randomised, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, two‑treatment complete crossover study evaluating the efficacy and safety of the selective B1 receptor antagonist **fulmetibant** in patients with diabetic neuropathic pain (DNP).
    *   **Eligibility**: Adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus with painful distal symmetric sensorimotor neuropathy of >6 months' duration and neuropathic pain.
    *   **Design**: After blinded washout of prior DNP treatment, patients were randomised 1:1 to start with either fulmetibant (450 mg once daily) or placebo in the first 4‑week treatment period, and vice versa in the second period.
    *   **Primary endpoint**: Change from baseline in weekly mean 24‑hour average pain intensity score at week 4.
    *   **Results**: 79 participants were treated with fulmetibant and 79 with placebo. At week 4, the mean treatment difference was **0.07 (−0.170 to 0.314)** , which was not statistically significant. Adverse events were mostly mild or moderate and occurred in 51.3% of treated participants.
    *   **Conclusion**: **The results of this study show that preclinical efficacy seen with this bradykinin 1 receptor antagonist did not translate into a meaningful therapeutic approach for pain management in patients with DNP.** The bradykinin 1 receptor antagonist fulmetibant did not reduce pain scores vs placebo.
*   **Reasons for translational failure**: The negative results of the BRADiNP study highlight several potential challenges:
    *   **Temporal dominance of B1 vs. B2 receptors**: In chronic, established neuropathic pain in humans, the role of B1 receptors may be less dominant than in the acute and early chronic phases, where preclinical models often show the most robust efficacy. Double‑knockout studies have indicated that kinins are less essential in chronic stages of pain.
    *   **Redundancy and compensatory mechanisms**: B2 receptors, kinin‑independent pronociceptive systems (e.g., TNF‑α, IL‑1β, NGF‑TrkA, CCL2/CCR2), and central sensitisation mechanisms that have become kinin‑independent over time may compensate for B1 receptor blockade in the chronic phase.
    *   **Human vs. rodent pharmacology**: Human DRG neurons express B2 and, to a lesser extent, B1 receptors. B2Rs are enriched in the nociceptor subpopulation. While B1R expression is increased in donors with a history of chronic pain, the absolute level and functional significance of human B1R may differ from that in rodent models.
    *   **Acute vs. prolonged BK effects**: In human DRG neurons, acute bradykinin increases neuronal excitability, whereas prolonged exposure to bradykinin can paradoxically **decrease** neuronal excitability in a subpopulation of human DRG neurons. This bidirectional modulation—dependent on the duration of BK exposure and the physiological subtype of the sensory neuron—could complicate the effects of receptor blockade in chronic pain states.
*   **Dual B1/B2 antagonism**: Given the compensation between B1 and B2 receptors, dual antagonists or combination therapy may be more effective than selective blockade of a single receptor subtype. This approach is supported by preclinical data but has not yet been tested clinically for neuropathic pain.
*   **Oral, non‑peptide kinin receptor antagonists**: The development of orally bioavailable, small‑molecule antagonists for B1 and B2 receptors with good CNS penetration remains an active area of pharmaceutical research. The failure of fulmetibant has not extinguished interest in the target but has shifted focus towards dual antagonists and more careful patient selection.
*   **Biomarker‑guided patient stratification**: Donors' history of chronic pain and age may be predictors of higher B1 receptor expression in human DRG neurons. This suggests that B1R expression profiling (e.g., in skin biopsies or through surrogate markers) could identify a subpopulation of patients most likely to benefit from B1R‑targeted therapy. The timing of intervention—early after injury versus chronic established pain—may also be critical.
*   **Combination strategies**:
    *   **B1/B2 antagonist + TRPV1 or TRPA1 antagonist**: Given the extensive BK–TRP channel crosstalk, dual inhibition could produce additive or synergistic analgesia.
    *   **B1/B2 antagonist + opioid**: Since kinin receptor blockade can reduce peripheral opioid analgesia, careful dosing or the use of centrally acting opioids could mitigate this interaction.
    *   **B1/B2 antagonist + NSAID**: BK stimulates prostaglandin production; combining receptor blockade with COX inhibition could provide enhanced analgesia with lower NSAID doses.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Kinins are less essential in chronic pain stages**: Data from B1/B2 double knockout mice indicate that kinin receptors are important for nociception associated with acute short‑lasting inflammation but are **less essential in chronic stages of pain**. Chronic neuropathic pain in these animals was unchanged, suggesting that compensatory pronociceptive pathways assume dominance over time. This may explain why B1R blockade in established chronic human diabetic neuropathy was ineffective.
*   **Protective functions of kinins via the opioid system**: Kinins interact with the opioid system to support peripheral opioid analgesia. Complete kinin receptor blockade could impair endogenous opioid pain‑control mechanisms, potentially offsetting the analgesic benefit of receptor antagonism. This is a critical consideration for the development of kinin‑targeted analgesics.
*   **Icatibant and B2R antagonism**: While icatibant is approved and well‑tolerated for HAE, its peptide nature, short half‑life, and requirement for subcutaneous injection make it unsuitable for chronic pain management. Small‑molecule, orally bioavailable B2R antagonists with CNS penetration are needed, but none have yet reached clinical trials for pain.
*   **Physiological roles of kinins**: The KKS is essential for cardiovascular homeostasis, renal function, inflammation, and tissue repair. Chronic systemic kinin receptor blockade could impair these physiological processes. B2R antagonism can cause vasoconstriction and increase blood pressure, a side effect that is not observed with icatibant (which is used acutely) but could be problematic with chronic oral antagonists.
*   **Bradykinin as a pain biomarker and potential for paradoxical effects**: Bradykinin itself, while an algogen peripherally, can have central antinociceptive effects when acting in the periaqueductal grey and other brainstem regions. Elevated bradykinin levels have been found in the plasma of patients with complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) and sickle cell disease pain, where they may serve as a biomarker of active disease.
*   **Sex differences**: Preclinical studies have used both male and female animals, but the majority of mechanistic studies have been conducted in males. Sex‑specific differences in kinin receptor expression and function in human DRG neurons are not well characterised.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**: The kinin system is integrated into the broader neuroimmune network:
    *   **TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPV4**: BK sensitises these TRP channels via B2R‑ and B1R‑dependent PKCε and PKA pathways, directly linking kinins to the primary transduction channels of nociceptors.
    *   **Prostaglandins**: BK stimulates COX‑1‑ and COX‑2‑dependent prostaglandin synthesis (particularly PGE₂), which amplifies nociceptor sensitisation and inflammation.
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: BK stimulates the release of these pro‑inflammatory cytokines from immune cells, linking the KKS to the broader cytokine network that sustains neuropathic pain.
    *   **NGF and TrkA**: NGF sensitises nociceptors and can upregulate B1 receptor expression, while BK can stimulate NGF release from fibroblasts and Schwann cells, forming a reciprocal loop.
    *   **ERK/MAPK and PKC**: BK activates the ERK pathway and PKC in nociceptors, converging on the phosphorylation of Nav1.7, Nav1.8, TRPV1, and NMDA receptors.
    *   **CCL2/CCR2 and CX3CL1/CX3CR1**: Bradykinin can promote the expression of chemokines, linking the KKS to the recruitment and activation of microglia and macrophages.
    *   **Endothelin**: Both bradykinin and endothelin are potent algogenic peptides that activate Gαq/11‑coupled receptors and sensitise TRP channels through overlapping intracellular pathways, suggesting potential synergy.
    *   **Opioid system**: As described, kinin receptors support peripheral opioid analgesia; blockade may impair endogenous and exogenous opioid efficacy.
