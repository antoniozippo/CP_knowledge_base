# Involvement of Prostaglandins in Neuropathic Pain

Prostaglandins (PGs) are bioactive lipid mediators derived from arachidonic acid through the action of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes. While classically associated with acute inflammation and peripheral sensitization, a large body of evidence now implicates prostaglandins—particularly prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), prostacyclin (PGI2), and prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α)—as pivotal players in the initiation and maintenance of neuropathic pain. They act at multiple anatomical levels: the injured peripheral nerve, the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), the spinal dorsal horn, and supraspinal structures. Their pronociceptive actions include direct excitation and sensitization of nociceptors, facilitation of central sensitization, promotion of neuroinflammation, and disruption of the blood‑spinal cord barrier. Both COX‑1 and COX‑2 are involved, challenging the traditional view that only COX‑2 is relevant to pain.

### 🧬 Prostaglandin Synthesis After Nerve Injury

Prostaglandin synthesis begins with the release of arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2 (PLA2). Arachidonic acid is then converted by COX enzymes to PGH2, which is subsequently metabolized by cell‑specific terminal synthases into the five primary prostanoids: PGE2, PGD2, PGI2, PGF2α, and thromboxane A2 (TXA2).

*   **COX‑1**: Historically considered a constitutive enzyme, COX‑1 is now known to be dynamically upregulated in the spinal cord after peripheral nerve injury. Its expression increases in microglia in the superficial dorsal horn (laminae I–II) in a time‑ and laminar‑dependent manner, contributing to the production of pronociceptive prostaglandins, particularly PGD2[reference:0][reference:1].
*   **COX‑2**: COX‑2 is dramatically upregulated in macrophages and other inflammatory cells at the nerve injury site, in DRG neurons, and in spinal endothelial cells following various types of nerve injury[reference:2][reference:3][reference:4]. This upregulation is driven, in part, by microglia‑derived TNF‑α acting on TNFR1 and TNFR2 receptors on endothelial cells[reference:5].
*   **Terminal Synthases**: The specific profile of prostaglandins produced depends on which terminal synthase is expressed. For example:
    *   **Microsomal PGE synthase‑1 (mPGES‑1)** is co‑localized with COX‑2 in spinal endothelial cells and is upregulated after nerve injury, driving PGE2 synthesis[reference:6].
    *   **Hematopoietic PGD synthase (H‑PGDS)** and **lipocalin‑type PGDS (L‑PGDS)** are upregulated in spinal microglia and contribute to PGD2 production[reference:7][reference:8].
    *   **PGI2 synthase (PGIS)** is induced in spinal endothelial cells alongside COX‑2 after spared nerve injury (SNI)[reference:9].

### 🔬 Key Prostaglandins and Their Mechanisms in Neuropathic Pain

#### 1. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2): The Central Pronociceptive Prostanoid

PGE2 is the most extensively studied prostaglandin in pain. Following nerve injury, PGE2 levels are chronically elevated at the injury site, in the DRG, and in the spinal cord[reference:10][reference:11]. It signals through four G‑protein‑coupled receptors (EP1–EP4), all of which are upregulated in the injured nerve[reference:12].

*   **EP1 Receptor**:
    *   EP1 is coupled to Gq and mobilizes intracellular Ca²⁺. It is predominantly expressed on infiltrating macrophages in the injured nerve (≈80% of EP1‑IR cells are ED1‑positive macrophages)[reference:13].
    *   Systemic or oral administration of the selective EP1 antagonist **ONO‑8711** significantly reduces mechanical hyperalgesia and allodynia in the chronic constriction injury (CCI) model, and suppresses injury‑induced c‑fos expression in the spinal dorsal horn[reference:14][reference:15].

*   **EP2 Receptor**:
    *   EP2 is coupled to Gs and increases cAMP. In the spinal cord, EP2 is expressed on dorsal horn neurons, where it receives PGE2 signals derived from endothelial cells[reference:16].
    *   In chemotherapy‑induced neuropathic pain (CIPN) models, paclitaxel upregulates spinal EP2 expression and PGE2 release, driving microglial activation and mechanical hypersensitivity. Blocking this pathway with allopregnanolone or EP2 antagonists reduces pain[reference:17].
    *   *Caveat*: Some studies suggest that EP2‑mediated inhibition of glycinergic neurotransmission may not contribute to neuropathic pain after peripheral nerve injury, indicating context‑dependent roles[reference:18].

*   **EP3 Receptor**:
    *   EP3 is coupled to Gi and reduces cAMP. Genetic deletion of EP3, but not EP1, EP2, or EP4, reduces the development of late‑stage mechanical allodynia in the SNI model[reference:19].
    *   EP3 activation on macrophages and other immune cells at the injury site induces the release of CC‑chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2/MCP‑1), a potent chemokine that recruits additional immune cells and amplifies neuroinflammation. Antagonists against the CCL2 receptor (CCR2) reduce mechanical allodynia, an effect absent in EP3 knockout mice[reference:20][reference:21].

*   **EP4 Receptor**:
    *   EP4, like EP2, is Gs‑coupled. It is the most abundant EP receptor subtype in the injured nerve[reference:22]. EP4 is also expressed on approximately half of IB4‑binding nociceptors in the DRG[reference:23].
    *   Peripheral PGE2 stimulation drives the synthesis and anterograde axonal trafficking of EP4 receptors to nociceptor terminals, prolonging nociceptor sensitization and contributing to the transition from acute to chronic pain[reference:24].
    *   EP4 activation in DRG neurons triggers the *de novo* synthesis of interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) via PKA, PKC, ERK/MAPK, CREB, and NF‑κB pathways, linking PGE2 to cytokine‑driven neuroinflammation[reference:25].
    *   EP4 blockade with selective antagonists prevents or shortens inflammagen‑prolonged nociceptor sensitization[reference:26].

#### 2. Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2): Microglia‑to‑Neuron Pain Signal

PGD2 is the most abundant prostanoid in the CNS. Its role in neuropathic pain is mediated primarily through the **DP2 receptor** (also known as CRTH2), while the DP1 receptor may have opposing, protective functions.

*   **COX‑1‑Dependent Production in Microglia**: After peripheral nerve injury, COX‑1 is upregulated in spinal microglia, which also express elevated levels of hematopoietic PGD synthase (H‑PGDS). This leads to COX‑1‑dependent, microglia‑derived PGD2 production[reference:27].
*   **DP2‑Mediated Neuronal Excitation**: PGD2 released from microglia diffuses to spinal dorsal horn neurons, where it activates DP2 receptors. This microglia‑to‑neuron signaling cascade is a key triggering factor for mechanical allodynia[reference:28]. Intrathecal injection of a DP2 receptor antagonist significantly attenuates mechanical allodynia in the SNI model[reference:29].
*   **Cisplatin‑Induced Neuropathy**: In chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy, enhanced PGD2 signaling via upregulation of lipocalin‑type PGDS in the spinal cord contributes to mechanical allodynia through DP2 receptors. Blocking DP2 represents a potential therapeutic target for CIPN[reference:30][reference:31].
*   **DP1 Receptor – Protective Role**: In contrast to DP2, the DP1 receptor appears to exert antinociceptive and anti‑inflammatory effects. Spinal application of a DP1 antagonist increases responses to mechanical stimulation of inflamed joints, suggesting that endogenous PGD2 attenuates central sensitization through DP1 activation under inflammatory conditions[reference:32]. This dual DP1/DP2 functionality mirrors the yin‑yang pattern seen in other pain‑modulating systems.

#### 3. Prostacyclin (Prostaglandin I2, PGI2): Endothelial‑Derived Early‑Phase Pain

PGI2 is a potent vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation. Its role in neuropathic pain is tightly linked to the neurovascular unit and the early phase after injury.

*   **Microglia–Endothelial Crosstalk**: Microglial TNF‑α, released rapidly after nerve injury, induces COX‑2 and PGI2 synthase (PGIS) expression in spinal endothelial cells. This glia–endothelial interaction of the neurovascular unit produces endothelial PGI2[reference:33][reference:34].
*   **Neuronal IP Receptor Activation**: PGI2 acts on IP receptors constitutively expressed on dorsal horn neurons. Both COX‑2 inhibitors and IP receptor antagonists attenuate pain behavior specifically in the early phase of neuropathic pain[reference:35][reference:36].
*   **Direct Excitation of Injured Afferents**: PGI2 directly excites C‑fibers in chronic neuromas of rat sciatic nerve, contributing to ectopic activity and post‑injury pain[reference:37].

#### 4. Prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α): Spinal Mechanical Hyperalgesia

PGF2α signals through the FP receptor, which couples to Gq and mobilizes intracellular Ca²⁺.

*   **Spinal Pronociceptive Effects**: Intrathecal administration of PGF2α produces robust, dose‑dependent mechanical hyperalgesia and increases the responses of nociceptive‑specific (NS) spinal dorsal horn neurons to mechanical stimuli. It also produces modest increases in wide dynamic range (WDR) neuron activity[reference:38][reference:39].
*   **Mechanistic Specificity**: The mechanical hyperalgesia induced by spinal PGF2α is mediated primarily by changes in NS neurons, distinguishing its central effects from those of other prostaglandins[reference:40].

#### 5. Thromboxane A2 (TXA2): Emerging Pain Mediator

TXA2, synthesized by thromboxane A synthase from PGH2, is well known for its role in platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction. Its role in neuropathic pain is less well characterized but is gaining attention.

*   **Radicular Pain**: TXA2 has been implicated in radicular pain induced by herniated nucleus pulposus, where it acts synergistically with leukotriene B4 (LTB4) to promote inflammation and pain[reference:41][reference:42].
*   **TP Receptor in Neuropathic Pain**: Recent evidence indicates that intrathecal administration of a thromboxane A2 receptor (TP receptor) agonist can modulate pain behavior in the L5 spinal nerve transection (SNT) model, suggesting a direct role in spinal pain processing[reference:43].

### 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis or receptor blockade consistently reduces neuropathic pain in animal models.

*   **COX Inhibitors**:
    *   **Non‑selective COX inhibitors (NSAIDs)** : Local injection of ketorolac into the injury site or plantar surface reverses tactile allodynia for >5 days in the partial sciatic nerve ligation (PSNL) model and suppresses injury‑induced CREB phosphorylation in the spinal dorsal horn[reference:44].
    *   **Selective COX‑1 inhibitors**: Intrathecal COX‑1 inhibitors attenuate mechanical allodynia in neuropathic pain models, consistent with the upregulation of COX‑1 in spinal microglia[reference:45][reference:46].
    *   **Selective COX‑2 inhibitors**: Systemic or intrathecal COX‑2 inhibitors reduce pain behavior, particularly in the early phase after nerve injury[reference:47][reference:48]. The centrally active COX‑2 inhibitor AAT‑076 has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models of neuropathic pain and is under clinical development[reference:49].
    *   **Dual COX‑1/COX‑2 inhibition**: Combined inhibition of both COX isoforms provides broader analgesic effects in formalin‑evoked pain models[reference:50].

*   **Prostanoid Receptor Antagonists**:
    *   **EP1 antagonist (ONO‑8711)** : Reduces hyperalgesia, allodynia, and spinal c‑fos expression in CCI[reference:51].
    *   **EP3 genetic deletion**: Reduces late‑stage mechanical allodynia in SNI[reference:52].
    *   **EP4 antagonists**: Prevent or shorten nociceptor sensitization and reduce IL‑6 upregulation in DRG[reference:53][reference:54].
    *   **DP2 antagonists**: Attenuate mechanical allodynia in SNI and cisplatin‑induced neuropathy models[reference:55][reference:56].
    *   **IP receptor antagonists**: Attenuate early‑phase neuropathic pain behavior[reference:57].

*   **Genetic Models**:
    *   **EP1 and EP3 knockout mice**: EP1‑/‑ and EP3‑/‑ mice show altered pain responses to PGE2, with EP3‑/‑ mice specifically showing reduced late‑stage mechanical allodynia after SNI[reference:58][reference:59].
    *   **L‑PGDS‑deficient mice**: These mice, which cannot synthesize PGD2 in the CNS, show a lack of tactile allodynia, confirming the pronociceptive role of central PGD2[reference:60].

### 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **NSAIDs in Neuropathic Pain**: Despite their widespread use, the clinical efficacy of oral NSAIDs for neuropathic pain is inconsistent. A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute the use of oral NSAIDs in any neuropathic pain condition[reference:61]. This likely reflects the fact that systemic NSAID concentrations may be insufficient to block central COX activity, and that neuropathic pain involves COX‑independent pathways.
*   **COX‑2 Inhibitors in Postherpetic Neuralgia (PHN)** : A randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled trial of the selective COX‑2 inhibitor GW406381 in patients with PHN was inconclusive regarding the clinical relevance of COX‑2 inhibition in this condition[reference:62]. This does not negate the preclinical data but highlights the challenge of translating mechanism‑based therapies to heterogeneous patient populations.
*   **Peripheral vs. Central Targeting**: Local injection of NSAIDs into the injury site or perineurally produces more robust and sustained analgesia than systemic administration in animal models[reference:63]. This suggests that achieving adequate drug concentrations at the site of prostaglandin production is a key therapeutic challenge.
*   **Prostanoid Receptor Antagonists as Novel Analgesics**: The discrete receptor pharmacology of prostaglandins offers an opportunity for more targeted interventions with potentially fewer side effects than global COX inhibition. Specifically:
    *   **EP1 antagonists**: May be useful for neuropathic pain with a strong peripheral inflammatory component.
    *   **EP3 antagonists**: Could reduce late‑stage mechanical allodynia and immune cell recruitment to the injured nerve.
    *   **EP4 antagonists**: May prevent the transition from acute to chronic pain by blocking nociceptor sensitization and IL‑6 synthesis.
    *   **DP2 antagonists**: Represent a novel approach to interrupt microglia‑to‑neuron pain signaling in the spinal cord.
*   **Biomarker Potential**: Elevated PGE2, PGD2, or their metabolites in cerebrospinal fluid or serum may reflect ongoing neuroinflammation and help identify patients likely to benefit from anti‑prostaglandin therapies.

### ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Dual and Opposing Roles**: Like many pain modulators, prostaglandins can have opposing effects depending on the receptor subtype and context:
    *   EP2 activation can be either pronociceptive (spinally) or antinociceptive (peripherally, under certain conditions).
    *   DP1 receptors mediate antinociceptive effects, while DP2 receptors are pronociceptive.
    *   EP3 can mediate antinociception during inflammation, despite its pronociceptive role in neuropathic pain[reference:64].
    *   This receptor‑specific yin‑yang means that broad COX inhibition may inadvertently block protective prostaglandin signaling, contributing to the limited clinical efficacy of NSAIDs in neuropathic pain.

*   **Physiological Functions**: Prostaglandins are essential for gastrointestinal mucosal protection, renal function, platelet aggregation, and vascular homeostasis. Chronic COX inhibition, particularly of COX‑1, carries well‑known risks (gastrointestinal bleeding, renal impairment). Selective receptor antagonists may offer a better safety profile, but their physiological roles must be carefully considered.

*   **Temporal Dynamics**: The COX/prostaglandin system appears to be particularly important in the initiation and early maintenance phases of neuropathic pain, while later chronic stages may become partially prostaglandin‑independent. The therapeutic window for intervention must be carefully defined.

*   **Sex Differences**: Some studies report sex‑dependent differences in prostaglandin synthesis and receptor expression after nerve injury, but this remains understudied compared to other pain mechanisms.

*   **Interactions with Other Pain Pathways**:
    *   **TNF‑α**: Microglia‑derived TNF‑α directly induces COX‑2 and PGIS expression in spinal endothelial cells, creating a positive‑feedback loop that amplifies prostaglandin‑driven neuroinflammation[reference:65][reference:66].
    *   **BDNF/TrkB**: PGE2 contributes to the synthesis of BDNF in primary sensory neurons, linking prostaglandin signaling to neurotrophin‑driven central sensitization[reference:67].
    *   **IL‑6**: PGE2, via EP4 receptors, drives the *de novo* synthesis of IL‑6 in DRG neurons, coupling lipid mediator signaling to pro‑inflammatory cytokine cascades[reference:68].
    *   **CCL2/CCR2**: EP3 activation induces CCL2 release, which recruits immune cells to the injured nerve and amplifies neuroinflammation[reference:69].
    *   **BBB/BSCB Integrity**: PGI2 is a potent vasodilator and can influence vascular permeability. Prostaglandin‑mediated endothelial activation contributes to barrier dysfunction and facilitates immune cell infiltration.
    *   **RhoA/ROCK**: Prostaglandin receptors couple to G12/13, which can activate RhoA, linking prostaglandin signaling to cytoskeletal changes in neurons and glia.
    *   **HIF1α/VEGFA**: HIF1α can upregulate COX‑2 expression, and VEGFA‑induced vascular remodeling may facilitate prostaglandin access to neural tissues.
