# Involvement of Endothelin (ET‑1) and Endothelin Receptors (ETA/ETB) in Neuropathic Pain

Endothelin‑1 (ET‑1) is a 21‑amino acid peptide that was originally identified as a potent vasoconstrictor produced by endothelial cells, but has since been recognised as a multifunctional signalling molecule with profound effects on nociceptive processing. In the context of neuropathic pain, ET‑1 is locally upregulated at the site of nerve injury and in the spinal cord, where it acts as a **pronociceptive algogen**—one of the most potent endogenous pain‑producing substances known. ET‑1 directly excites and sensitises nociceptors, potentiates the effects of other pain mediators (capsaicin, formalin, arachidonic acid), and drives central sensitisation through its actions in the spinal dorsal horn. Its effects are mediated by two G‑protein‑coupled receptors: the **endothelin type A receptor (ETA)** , which primarily transduces pronociceptive and vasoconstrictive signals, and the **endothelin type B receptor (ETB)** , which can be either pronociceptive or antinociceptive depending on its cellular location and the context of its activation. Pharmacological blockade of ETA receptors, or dual ETA/ETB receptor antagonism, consistently reduces mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and cold allodynia in diverse preclinical models—including traumatic nerve injury, chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy, diabetic neuropathy, and spinal cord injury‑induced pain. Conversely, activation of ETB receptors on keratinocytes and possibly on nociceptors can trigger the release of β‑endorphin and produce endogenous analgesia, revealing a functional yin‑yang that complicates therapeutic targeting. Clinically, several endothelin receptor antagonists are already approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension (bosentan, ambrisentan, macitentan), and post‑hoc analyses of large clinical trials have provided evidence that endothelin receptor blockade reduces pain in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease. These findings, together with a growing body of preclinical data, position the endothelin system as a promising, yet complex, target for non‑opioid analgesic development.

## 🧬 The Endothelin System: Peptides, Receptors, and Normal Functions

*   **Gene and peptide family**: The human endothelin family comprises three 21‑amino acid peptides (ET‑1, ET‑2, ET‑3), each encoded by a distinct gene (*EDN1*, *EDN2*, *EDN3*). **ET‑1**, encoded by *EDN1* on chromosome 6p24.1, is the predominant isoform in the cardiovascular and nervous systems and the primary mediator of pain. ET‑2 and ET‑3 have overlapping but more restricted roles.
*   **Biosynthesis and activation**: ET‑1 is synthesised as a 212‑amino acid precursor, **prepro‑endothelin‑1**, which is cleaved by a furin‑like protease to generate **big endothelin‑1** (38 amino acids). Big ET‑1 is then converted to mature ET‑1 by **endothelin‑converting enzyme‑1 (ECE‑1)** , a membrane‑bound metalloprotease. ECE‑1 is the rate‑limiting step in ET‑1 production and is itself upregulated at sites of nerve injury.
*   **Receptors**:
    *   **Endothelin type A receptor (ETA, ETAR)** : A Gαq/11‑coupled receptor (also coupling to Gαs and Gα12/13) with high affinity for ET‑1 and ET‑2, but very low affinity for ET‑3. ETA is expressed on:
        *   **Primary sensory neurons** of the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and trigeminal ganglia
        *   **Spinal dorsal horn neurons** and glial cells
        *   **Vascular smooth muscle cells**, where it mediates vasoconstriction
        *   **Peripheral nerve Schwann cells** and infiltrating macrophages
    *   **Endothelin type B receptor (ETB, ETBR)** : A Gαq/11‑ and Gαi‑coupled receptor with equal affinity for all three endothelin peptides. ETB is expressed on:
        *   **Subsets of DRG neurons** and satellite glial cells
        *   **Spinal cord glial cells** (microglia, astrocytes)
        *   **Endothelial cells**, where it mediates vasodilation via nitric oxide and prostacyclin release
        *   **Keratinocytes**, where it plays a critical role in peripheral analgesia
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **DRG and peripheral nerve**: Both ETA and ETB receptors are present on small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptive neurons and on non‑neuronal cells (satellite glial cells, Schwann cells). ET‑1 co‑localises with ETA and TRPV1 in a substantial subpopulation of nociceptors.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: ETA receptors are expressed on neurons in laminae I–II and on glial cells. ETB receptors are present on astrocytes, microglia, and some neuronal populations.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: Endothelin receptors are widely distributed in the brain, including the hypothalamus, periaqueductal grey (PAG), and brainstem nuclei involved in descending pain modulation. In the PAG, central ET‑1 can exert antinociceptive effects that are distinct from its peripheral pronociceptive actions.
*   **Physiological functions**:
    *   **Vascular tone**: ET‑1 is the most potent endogenous vasoconstrictor, acting on vascular smooth muscle ETA receptors. ETB receptors on endothelial cells counterbalance this by promoting vasodilation.
    *   **Development**: Endothelin signalling is essential for the development of neural crest‑derived tissues, including the enteric nervous system and melanocytes.
    *   **Nociception**: Under physiological conditions, low levels of ET‑1 tonically modulate nociceptor sensitivity and participate in the normal processing of acute pain.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of Endothelin in Neuropathic Pain

After peripheral nerve injury, the endothelin system is markedly upregulated at multiple levels—peripheral nerve, DRG, and spinal cord—where it engages complex, receptor‑specific mechanisms that can either promote or suppress pain depending on the receptor subtype, cellular location, and anatomical compartment.

### 1. Upregulation of Endothelin‑1 and Its Receptors After Nerve Injury

*   In the **chronic constriction injury (CCI)** model, ET‑1 and ETA receptor mRNA and protein are locally upregulated at the site of nerve injury, suggesting that ET‑1 is involved in the establishment of neuropathic pain after nerve damage[reference:0].
*   In **spinal cord injury (SCI)** , ETA receptor (ETAR) mRNA and protein are significantly upregulated in the spinal cord and dorsal root ganglia from day 14 onwards, coinciding with the development of mechanical allodynia. ETB receptor mRNA is also increased, but at the protein level, only ETA is upregulated in spinal grey matter[reference:1].
*   In a mouse model of antigen‑induced arthritis, ET‑1 and ET‑2 showed marked upregulation in lumbar DRGs during the acute phase of arthritis, indicating that joint inflammation rapidly engages the endothelin system in sensory neurons[reference:2].
*   In **diabetic neuropathy**, the expression of **ETB receptors** is significantly reduced in the sciatic nerves, while ETA receptor expression remains unaltered. ETB‑deficient transgenic rats show alterations in pain perception similar to those observed in diabetic rats, suggesting that a loss of protective ETB‑mediated signalling contributes to mechanical hyperalgesia and tactile allodynia in chronic diabetes[reference:3].

### 2. Direct Excitation and Sensitisation of Nociceptors via ETA Receptors

ET‑1 is one of the most potent direct activators of nociceptors, producing pain behaviour when injected into the hindpaw, applied to the sciatic nerve, or administered intradermally in both animals and humans. These effects are primarily mediated by ETA receptors on nociceptor terminals.

*   **In rats**: Local injection of ET‑1 into the plantar hindpaw or application to the sciatic nerve produces dose‑dependent, ipsilateral hindpaw flinching and other nocifensive behaviours. These are blocked by ETA receptor‑selective antagonists but not by ETB antagonists[reference:4].
*   **In humans**: Intradermal injection of ET‑1 produces spontaneous, short‑lasting pain at higher concentrations, confirming that ET‑1 is an endogenous algogen in human skin[reference:5]. ET‑1 also activates and sensitises mechanosensitive (but not mechano‑insensitive) C‑nociceptors in human volunteers, with effects partially independent of histamine release from mast cells[reference:6].
*   **Selective activation of nociceptors by ETA**: Sensory fibres resistant to tetrodotoxin (TTX) show selective excitation by ET‑1 via ETA receptor activation, demonstrating that ET‑1 targets a specific, pronociceptive subpopulation of primary afferents[reference:7].
*   **ETA‑dependent neuronal excitation**: ETA is a Gαq/11‑coupled receptor that activates phospholipase C, generating IP₃‑mediated Ca²⁺ release from intracellular stores and diacylglycerol‑dependent PKC activation. This leads to:
    *   Depolarisation of nociceptor terminals.
    *   Enhanced action potential firing.
    *   Increased intracellular calcium that sensitises downstream ion channels.

### 3. Potentiation of TRPV1 and TRPA1 Channels via ETA–PKC and ETA–PKA Pathways

A critical mechanism by which ET‑1 produces pain is through the sensitisation of TRP channels on nociceptors.

*   **TRPV1 potentiation via ETA–PKC**: ET‑1, acting via the ETA receptor, potentiates capsaicin‑induced TRPV1 currents by a **protein kinase C (PKC)‑dependent mechanism**. In cultured DRG neurons and HEK293 cells co‑expressing TRPV1 and ETA, simultaneous application of ET‑1 and capsaicin produces markedly larger currents than capsaicin alone. ETA and TRPV1 are co‑expressed in small sensory neurons, providing the anatomical substrate for this functional interaction[reference:8]. This ETA–PKC‑mediated TRPV1 sensitisation is considered a major contributor to the algogenic and hyperalgesic effects of ET‑1[reference:9].
*   **TRPA1 sensitisation via ETA–PKA**: ET‑1 also sensitises the TRPA1 channel through the ETA receptor, but via a **PKA‑dependent pathway** rather than PKC. This contributes to ET‑1‑induced mechanical hyperalgesia[reference:10].
*   **TRPV1 involvement in ET‑1‑induced pain behaviour**: The importance of TRPV1 is demonstrated by studies showing that ET‑1‑induced pain‑like behaviours (licking, flinching, biting) are significantly attenuated in **TRPV1‑deficient mice** and are blocked by an ETA receptor antagonist[reference:11].
*   **TRPV1 contribution to ET‑1‑induced thermal hyperalgesia**: Sensitisation of TRPV1 through the ETA–PKC pathway also underlies ET‑1‑induced thermal hyperalgesia, and a strategy targeting TRPV1 may be effective to reduce pain associated with elevated ET‑1 levels[reference:12].

### 4. Central Pronociceptive Actions at the Spinal Cord

Beyond peripheral nociceptor sensitisation, ET‑1 acts at the spinal cord level to drive central sensitisation.

*   **Central ETA receptor antagonism alleviates pain**: Intrathecal administration of the selective ETA receptor antagonist **BQ‑123** dose‑dependently alleviates mechanical allodynia in the spinal nerve ligation (SNL) model. Repeated intrathecal BQ‑123 over three days produced a significant and sustained increase in mechanical withdrawal thresholds, with effects persisting on day 7 even after cessation of treatment[reference:13].
*   **Spinal ETA actions in chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy**: In the oxaliplatin model, intrathecal administration of an ETA receptor antagonist prevented the development of long‑lasting mechanical allodynia. In contrast, an intraplantar (peripheral) ETA antagonist suppressed cold allodynia but had only a partial and transient effect on mechanical allodynia. This demonstrates that **mechanical allodynia requires both spinal and peripheral ETA receptor activation**, while **cold allodynia is driven predominantly by peripheral ETA receptors**[reference:14].
*   **ETA upregulation after spinal cord injury**: Following traumatic thoracic spinal cord injury in rats, ETA receptor protein is upregulated in the spinal grey matter, and intrathecal administration of the ETA antagonist BQ‑123 on day 21 post‑injury transiently but significantly reduces SCI‑induced mechanical allodynia. Oral administration of the dual ETA/ETB antagonist bosentan similarly reduces allodynia, while the selective ETB antagonist BQ‑788 is ineffective, confirming that spinal ETA receptors mediate pain in this model[reference:15].
*   **Central vs. peripheral dichotomy**: The central nervous system actions of endothelin are complex and context‑dependent. While peripheral ET‑1 is unequivocally pronociceptive via ETA, central ET‑1 can be **antinociceptive** in certain regions—particularly in the PAG and when acting via ETB receptors. This highlights the need for receptor‑specific and site‑specific targeting.

### 5. ETB Receptors: Dual Roles in Pain Modulation

ETB receptors play a complex, context‑dependent role in pain that can be either analgesic or pronociceptive.

*   **ETB‑mediated endogenous analgesia**: One of the most striking findings is that **ETB receptor activation on keratinocytes triggers the release of β‑endorphin**, which then acts on μ‑opioid receptors on nociceptor terminals to produce local analgesia. This represents an endogenous, opioid‑dependent pain‑control circuit that is engaged by ET‑1 acting on peripheral ETB receptors[reference:16].
*   **ETB‑mediated direct hyperpolarisation of nociceptors**: In some DRG neuron populations, ET‑1 can hyperpolarise nociceptors by blocking voltage‑gated sodium currents (INa) and potentiating a potassium current (IKT) through selective activation of ETB receptors. This may represent an underlying mechanism for the reported antinociceptive effects of ET‑1 observed in some studies[reference:17].
*   **Loss of ETB tone in diabetic neuropathy**: As described above, ETB receptor expression is reduced in peripheral nerves in diabetic rats, and this loss contributes to mechanical hyperalgesia and allodynia. The protective, analgesic tone provided by ETB receptors is diminished in diabetes, shifting the balance towards ETA‑driven pronociception[reference:18].
*   **ETB pronociceptive roles**: In some contexts, ETB receptors on nociceptors or glial cells can contribute to pain. The ETB‑selective antagonist BQ‑788 can alleviate pain in some models (though not in SCI‑induced pain), suggesting that the net effect of ETB depends on the specific injury type and cellular expression pattern.

### 6. Interaction with Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs) and Inflammation

Endothelin‑1 and its receptors interact with matrix metalloproteinases in the generation of pain. Endothelin‑1 has been implicated in central and peripheral pathological pain, and MMPs may be downstream effectors or co‑mediators of endothelin‑induced nociceptive signalling[reference:19]. In the spinal nerve ligation model, both endothelin receptors and MMPs are upregulated, and their interaction contributes to the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain.

### 7. Endothelin in Cancer Pain

ET‑1 is overexpressed in several cancers, most notably prostate cancer, and is implicated in tumour‑associated pain. ET‑1 can both directly sensitise nociceptors within the tumour microenvironment and promote tumour growth and angiogenesis through ETA receptors. This dual role makes ETA antagonists particularly attractive in oncology settings, where they may simultaneously reduce pain and slow tumour progression.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

A substantial body of pharmacological and genetic evidence demonstrates that modulating the endothelin system can alleviate neuropathic pain.

### 1. Selective ETA Receptor Antagonists

*   **BQ‑123**: The prototypical peptide ETA‑selective antagonist. Intrathecal or systemic administration:
    *   Acutely and significantly reduces thermal and mechanical hyperalgesic responses in the CCI model[reference:20].
    *   Alleviates mechanical allodynia when administered intrathecally in the SNL model, with repeated doses producing sustained analgesia[reference:21].
    *   Transiently reduces SCI‑induced mechanical allodynia at 40 and 90 pmol intrathecally[reference:22].
*   **ABT‑627 (atrasentan)** : A non‑peptide, orally bioavailable, highly selective ETA receptor antagonist.
    *   In the streptozotocin‑induced diabetic rat model of neuropathic pain, systemic administration of ABT‑627 (10 mg/kg) produces a significant 40–50% increase in tactile allodynia thresholds within 0.5–2 hours. Chronic oral administration of ABT‑627 for 7 days maintains antinociceptive efficacy without tolerance[reference:23].
    *   The selective ETB antagonist A‑192621 does not significantly alter allodynia thresholds, and combined administration of ABT‑627 and A‑192621 produces effects comparable to ABT‑627 alone, confirming that ETA blockade is the primary analgesic mechanism in diabetic neuropathy[reference:24].
*   **Ambrisentan**: An ETA‑selective antagonist approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension. In a mouse model of antigen‑induced arthritis, ambrisentan **failed** to produce detectable anti‑inflammatory or antinociceptive activity, suggesting that dual ETA/ETB blockade may be required for arthritis‑associated pain[reference:25].

### 2. Dual ETA/ETB Receptor Antagonists

*   **Bosentan**: A non‑selective, orally active dual ETA/ETB receptor antagonist approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension.
    *   In the oxaliplatin‑induced neuropathic pain model, systemic (intraperitoneal) bosentan effectively blocked the development of both mechanical and cold allodynia, with effects comparable to selective ETA blockade[reference:26].
    *   In the SCI model, oral bosentan (30 and 100 mg/kg) transiently reduced SCI‑induced mechanical allodynia[reference:27].
    *   In antigen‑induced arthritis in mice, daily oral bosentan significantly attenuated knee joint swelling and inflammation, reduced mechanical hyperalgesia, and protected against joint destruction during disease flares—to an extent **comparable to dexamethasone**. These anti‑inflammatory and antinociceptive effects were predominantly mediated via the ETB receptor, in contrast to the ETA‑dominant mechanism observed in neuropathic pain[reference:28].
*   **SB‑209670**: A potent non‑selective endothelin receptor antagonist that prevents or delays axonal degeneration after spinal cord injury in rats, suggesting a potential neuroprotective effect in addition to analgesia[reference:29].

### 3. Genetic Models

*   **Conditional deletion of ETA in nociceptors**: Mice with Cre‑mediated deletion of ETA receptors specifically in Nav1.8‑positive nociceptors show reduced ET‑1‑induced nociceptive hypersensitivity, confirming that ETA receptors on nociceptors are essential for ET‑1‑mediated pain. ETB receptors on non‑neural peripheral cells also contribute to the full expression of ET‑1‑induced pain, demonstrating that both neuronal and non‑neuronal components are involved[reference:30].
*   **ETB‑deficient transgenic rats**: These animals exhibit alterations in pain perception similar to those seen in diabetic rats (mechanical hyperalgesia and tactile allodynia), indicating that loss of ETB signalling alone is sufficient to produce a pain phenotype resembling diabetic neuropathy[reference:31].
*   **ET‑1 overexpression and administration studies**: Intrathecal administration of ET‑1 can both produce and alleviate pain depending on dose and site, while intraplantar or perineural ET‑1 reliably produces pain behaviours. These studies have established the functional yin‑yang of central vs. peripheral endothelin actions.

### 4. ECE‑1 Inhibitors and Big ET‑1 Conversion

Inhibitors of endothelin‑converting enzyme‑1 (ECE‑1), which block the conversion of big ET‑1 to mature ET‑1, represent an alternative strategy to reduce ET‑1 levels. While less extensively studied than receptor antagonists, ECE‑1 inhibition has shown analgesic potential in some preclinical pain models. ECE‑1 is also a negative regulator of ET‑1‑induced pruritus (itch), indicating that the endothelin system is involved in both pain and itch processing.

### 5. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Resveratrol** and **curcumin**: Suppress ET‑1 expression by inhibiting NF‑κB and oxidative stress‑mediated transcriptional activation of the *EDN1* gene, contributing to their analgesic effects.
*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces ET‑1 levels in injured peripheral nerves and DRG, which may contribute to its analgesic effects in neuropathic pain models.
*   **Omega‑3 fatty acids**: Have been shown to reduce endothelin‑1 production in endothelial cells and may have a modest analgesic effect in inflammatory pain.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Post‑hoc analysis of the SONAR trial**: The strongest clinical evidence supporting the analgesic potential of endothelin receptor antagonism comes from a post‑hoc analysis of the **SONAR** trial (Study of Diabetic Nephropathy with Atrasentan). In this large, randomised, placebo‑controlled trial, the selective ETA receptor antagonist **atrasentan** (0.75 mg/day) was associated with a significant reduction in pain‑related adverse events and reduced use of analgesic medications in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease[reference:32]. This finding provides direct human evidence that ETA receptor blockade can reduce pain in a patient population with a high prevalence of neuropathic pain.
*   **Repurposing approved endothelin receptor antagonists**: Bosentan, ambrisentan, and macitentan are FDA‑approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension and have well‑characterised safety profiles. Their repurposing for neuropathic pain could be rapidly tested in clinical trials:
    *   **Bosentan** (dual ETA/ETB antagonist) has been shown to have anti‑inflammatory and analgesic effects in preclinical arthritis models, and its clinical use could be extended to chronic pain conditions with an inflammatory component.
    *   **Ambrisentan** (ETA‑selective) is effective preclinically in neuropathic pain, though it failed in an arthritis model, suggesting that its analgesic efficacy may be restricted to non‑inflammatory, neuropathic pain states.
    *   **Atrasentan** (ETA‑selective) has the strongest clinical evidence for pain reduction and has been investigated in a Phase II trial for painful diabetic neuropathy.
*   **Peripheral vs. central targeting**: The ability of peripherally restricted ETA antagonists to alleviate certain pain modalities (e.g., cold allodynia in oxaliplatin‑induced neuropathy) while requiring spinal penetration for others (e.g., mechanical allodynia) has practical implications for drug design. An ideal therapeutic approach may involve **peripherally acting ETA antagonists** for conditions dominated by peripheral sensitisation (e.g., chemotherapy‑induced cold allodynia, diabetic neuropathy), and **CNS‑penetrant antagonists** for central neuropathic pain (e.g., spinal cord injury pain).
*   **Cancer pain**: The dual role of ET‑1 as both a pronociceptive mediator and a tumour‑promoting factor (via angiogenesis and proliferation) makes ETA antagonists particularly attractive for cancer‑associated pain. Clinical trials of ETA antagonists in patients with advanced prostate cancer have been conducted, and pain outcomes were secondary endpoints in these studies.
*   **Biomarker potential**: Elevated plasma ET‑1 levels are found in many painful conditions, including sickle cell disease, Raynaud’s disease, complex regional pain syndrome, and cancer[reference:33]. Plasma ET‑1 could serve as a biomarker for identifying patients likely to benefit from endothelin receptor antagonist therapy.
*   **Combination strategies**:
    *   **ETA antagonist + ETB agonist**: The opposing roles of ETA (pronociceptive) and ETB (analgesic in certain contexts) suggest that combining an ETA antagonist with an ETB agonist could provide superior analgesia. The ETB agonist **sovateltide** (ILR‑1620) has been shown to improve motor function and reduce hyperalgesia in a rat model of spinal cord injury, exhibiting neuroprotective and antinociceptive properties[reference:34].
    *   **ETA antagonist + opioid**: Because ETB receptor activation releases endogenous β‑endorphin, combining an ETA antagonist with a low‑dose opioid (or enhancing endogenous opioid tone) could synergise.
    *   **ETA antagonist + TRPV1 antagonist**: Given the ETA–TRPV1 interaction, dual blockade could produce additive or synergistic analgesia for thermal hyperalgesia.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **The ETA/ETB yin‑yang**: The divergent roles of ETA and ETB receptors—pronociceptive and analgesic, respectively—complicate therapeutic targeting. A global ETA antagonist could be analgesic, but a dual ETA/ETB antagonist that blocks the protective ETB‑mediated endogenous opioid release could paradoxically worsen pain in some conditions. The optimal strategy depends on the specific pain aetiology, the anatomical site of action, and the relative expression of receptor subtypes.
*   **Central vs. peripheral dichotomy**: Peripheral ET‑1 is unequivocally pronociceptive via ETA receptors, whereas **central ET‑1 can be antinociceptive** in the CNS, particularly in the PAG and via ETB receptor‑mediated mechanisms[reference:35]. Intrathecal administration of ET‑1 has been shown to have antinociceptive effects in a rat model of postoperative pain. This central–peripheral dichotomy means that CNS‑penetrant endothelin receptor antagonists could potentially block endogenous descending pain control, limiting their net analgesic effect or even exacerbating pain.
*   **Physiological roles of endothelin**: Endothelin‑1 is essential for vascular homeostasis, cardiac function, renal function, and neural crest development. Chronic systemic endothelin receptor blockade is associated with:
    *   **Fluid retention and peripheral oedema**: The most common adverse effect of ERAs, mediated primarily by ETA receptor blockade in the renal collecting duct.
    *   **Hepatotoxicity**: Particularly with bosentan, requiring regular liver function monitoring.
    *   **Teratogenicity**: Endothelin signalling is essential for craniofacial development; all ERAs are contraindicated in pregnancy.
    *   **Headache, nasopharyngitis, flushing, and hypotension**: Common mild adverse effects[reference:36][reference:37].
    These side effects, while manageable in life‑threatening conditions like pulmonary arterial hypertension, may limit the use of systemic ERAs for non‑life‑threatening chronic pain conditions. Peripherally restricted ETA antagonists or topical formulations may mitigate systemic exposure.
*   **Context‑dependent roles of ETB**: The ETB receptor is a double‑edged sword. While its activation on keratinocytes produces analgesia, ETB activation on nociceptors or glial cells can be pronociceptive in some contexts. In trigeminal ganglia, ETA‑mediated TRPV1 hyperactivation and ETB‑mediated Ca²⁺ mobilisation occur in different subsets of neurons, contributing to orofacial pain[reference:38]. The net effect of ETB modulation depends on the specific cell type and pain condition.
*   **Species differences**: The relative expression and function of ETA and ETB receptors in nociceptors may differ between rodents and humans. Human C‑nociceptors are activated by ET‑1, and this is partly ETA‑dependent, but the role of ETB in human nociception is less well characterised. This could affect the translatability of preclinical findings.
*   **Sex differences**: Most preclinical studies on endothelin in neuropathic pain have been performed in male animals. Given the well‑established sex differences in nociceptive processing and the potential interaction of oestrogen with endothelin signalling, sex‑specific effects of endothelin receptor antagonists are plausible but largely unexplored.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**:
    *   **TRPV1 and TRPA1**: The ETA–PKC–TRPV1 and ETA–PKA–TRPA1 sensitisation pathways directly link endothelin to the ion channel mediators of nociceptor transduction and sensitisation.
    *   **Opioid system**: ETB receptor activation triggers the release of β‑endorphin from keratinocytes, activating μ‑opioid receptors on nociceptors and producing endogenous analgesia. This crosstalk provides a potential combination strategy.
    *   **Bradykinin**: ET‑1 and bradykinin, both Gαq/11‑coupled GPCR ligands, produce nociceptor sensitisation through overlapping and distinct intracellular pathways, including store‑operated calcium entry[reference:39].
    *   **Prostaglandins**: ET‑1 can stimulate COX‑2 and prostaglandin production, and prostaglandins can in turn modulate endothelin release, forming a reciprocal regulatory loop.
    *   **MMPs**: Endothelin receptors and matrix metalloproteinases are co‑regulated in neuropathic pain, and MMPs may be downstream effectors of endothelin‑induced pain signalling.
    *   **TRPV4**: Endothelin‑1 can modulate TRPV4 channels, contributing to mechanical hyperalgesia.
    *   **Sickle cell pain**: ET‑1 is elevated in sickle cell disease and contributes to the painful vaso‑occlusive crises through both vascular and nociceptor‑dependent mechanisms.
