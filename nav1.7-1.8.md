# Involvement of Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 in Neuropathic Pain

Voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav) are essential for the initiation and propagation of action potentials in nociceptive sensory neurons. Among the nine Nav α‑subunits (Nav1.1–Nav1.9), two are critically implicated in neuropathic pain: **Nav1.7** (encoded by *SCN9A*) and **Nav1.8** (encoded by *SCN10A*). Their selective expression in peripheral nociceptors, combined with compelling human and mouse genetic evidence, has established them as high‑priority non‑opioid analgesic targets. Yet their roles in pain pathophysiology are distinct: Nav1.7 acts as a threshold channel that amplifies small depolarizing inputs and supports neurotransmitter release, while Nav1.8 is the major contributor to the action potential upstroke in nociceptors and is essential for cold and mechanical pain. After nerve injury, both channels are upregulated in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons and undergo post‑translational modifications that enhance their activity, driving neuronal hyperexcitability, ectopic firing, and central sensitization. However, translating these findings into clinical therapies has proven challenging: despite robust preclinical validation, highly selective Nav1.7 inhibitors have largely failed in clinical trials, whereas **Nav1.8 blockers** have recently achieved regulatory success, with suzetrigine (VX‑548) becoming the first FDA‑approved Nav1.8 inhibitor for acute pain. This document reviews the distinct and overlapping roles of Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 in neuropathic pain, the mechanisms that drive their pathological upregulation, the preclinical and clinical evidence for their modulation, and the complexities that have shaped the search for sodium channel‑targeted analgesics.

---

## 1. Genetics and Human Validation

### 1.1 Nav1.7 (SCN9A)
- **Gain‑of‑function (GOF) mutations**: Cause two severe inherited pain disorders:
    - *Inherited erythromelalgia (IEM)*: characterized by burning pain and redness of the extremities in response to mild warmth.
    - *Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD)*: characterized by episodic pain in the rectum, eye, and mandible.
    - GOF mutations produce hyperpolarizing shifts in activation, depolarizing shifts in steady‑state inactivation, slowing of deactivation, and increased ramp currents, all augmenting the channel's response to small stimuli[reference:0][reference:1].
- **Loss‑of‑function (LOF) mutations**: Cause *congenital indifference to pain (CIP)*, a condition in which affected individuals perceive no pain but are otherwise neurologically normal—except for anosmia (loss of smell)[reference:2][reference:3]. This phenotype provided the original rationale for developing Nav1.7 inhibitors as human analgesics.

### 1.2 Nav1.8 (SCN10A)
- **Gain‑of‑function mutations**: Identified in patients with painful peripheral neuropathies. A well‑characterized Nav1.8 mutation shifts activation to hyperpolarized potentials and increases DRG neuron excitability[reference:4].
- **Loss‑of‑function studies in mice**: Genetic deletion of Nav1.8 in mice leads to a profound loss of pain, and antagonists are effective analgesics[reference:5].

### 1.3 Human DRG Evidence
In patients with severe neuropathic pain following brachial plexus avulsion (≥3 months duration), expression of Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and TrkB (NTRK2) is significantly increased in DRG neurons. Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 form supramolecular active clusters that geometrically increase neuronal hyperexcitability[reference:6].

## 2. Channel Structure, Localisation, and Physiological Roles

| Property | Nav1.7 | Nav1.8 |
|----------|--------|--------|
| **Gene** | *SCN9A* | *SCN10A* |
| **TTX sensitivity** | TTX‑sensitive (blocked by nanomolar TTX) | TTX‑resistant (requires micromolar TTX) |
| **Primary expression** | Peripheral nociceptors (small/medium DRG), sympathetic ganglion neurons, olfactory epithelium | Peripheral nociceptors (small/medium DRG), especially C‑fibre nociceptors |
| **Activation threshold** | Relatively hyperpolarized (≈ −55 to −50 mV) | Depolarized (≈ −40 to −30 mV) |
| **Inactivation kinetics** | Fast inactivation | Slow inactivation; sustains current during prolonged depolarization |
| **Electrophysiological role** | **Threshold channel**: amplifies small, sub‑threshold depolarizations; produces "ramp" currents that bring the membrane to action potential threshold. Essential for neurotransmitter release from nociceptor central terminals. | **Action potential upstroke**: contributes the majority of the inward current underlying the depolarizing phase of the action potential in C‑fibre nociceptors; enables repetitive firing during sustained depolarization. |
| **Olfaction** | Required; LOF causes anosmia | Not required |

### 2.1 Structural Features
- Both channels are pseudo‑tetrameric, with four homologous domains (DI–DIV), each containing six transmembrane segments (S1–S6). The S4 segment serves as the voltage sensor; the S5–S6 loop forms the pore.
- **Nav1.7** possesses a unique, long inactivation gate (DIII–DIV linker) that enables its fast‑inactivation property.
- **Nav1.8** contains a distinct set of amino acids in the DI‑S6 and DII‑S6 segments that confer TTX resistance.

## 3. Functional Redundancy and Interaction

### 3.1 Nociceptors Can Achieve Similar Excitability Using Different Nav Combinations
Contrary to earlier claims that Nav1.7 is strictly necessary for nociceptor action potential initiation, recent studies demonstrate that nociceptors can achieve similar excitability using different combinations of Nav1.3, Nav1.7, and Nav1.8. This functional redundancy has profound implications for the analgesic efficacy of isoform‑selective drugs, as blocking a single channel may be compensated by upregulation of others[reference:7].

### 3.2 Supramolecular Clustering with TrkB
In both mouse models of neuropathic pain (SNI, diabetic neuropathy) and human DRG neurons from brachial plexus avulsion patients, Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 are not merely co‑upregulated but form supramolecular active clusters—often in association with TrkB receptors. These clusters may allow coordinated, geometric amplification of hyperexcitability and suggest that inhibition of both Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 may be required to achieve efficient relief of neuropathic pain[reference:8].

### 3.3 Cellular Context Determines Phenotype
A single Nav1.7 mutation (L858H) associated with inherited erythromelalgia produces hyperexcitability in DRG sensory neurons but hypoexcitability in sympathetic ganglion neurons. The selective presence of Nav1.8—which remains available for activation at depolarized membrane potentials—in sensory but not sympathetic neurons is a major determinant of these opposing effects[reference:9].

## 4. Mechanisms of Dysregulation in Neuropathic Pain

### 4.1 Transcriptional and Epigenetic Upregulation
- **Nerve injury‑induced transcription factors**: The transcription factors CREB, STAT3, and AP‑1 drive *SCN9A* and *SCN10A* expression in DRG neurons after nerve injury.
- **MicroRNAs**: Downregulation of miR‑182, miR‑30b, and miR‑96 (which normally suppress Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 mRNAs) occurs after nerve injury, contributing to channel upregulation.
- **Histone modifications**: The BET bromodomain protein BRD4 binds acetylated histones at the *Scn9a* and *Scn10a* loci, promoting their transcription. BET inhibitors (e.g., JQ1) reduce Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 expression and alleviate neuropathic pain.
- **Epigenetic restraint by HDAC2**: HDAC2 constitutively represses *Cacna2d1* (encoding the α₂δ‑1 calcium channel subunit); after nerve injury, CK2‑mediated phosphorylation of HDAC2 causes its nuclear export, de‑repressing *Cacna2d1* and enhancing calcium currents that facilitate Nav channel‑mediated excitability.

### 4.2 Post‑Translational Modifications
- **Phosphorylation**:
    - **PKA and PKC**: Activated downstream of prostaglandin E₂, bradykinin, and NGF. Phosphorylate Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 at multiple cytoplasmic loop sites, enhancing channel activity and increasing membrane trafficking.
    - **ERK/MAPK**: Modulates Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 current density and kinetics in response to inflammatory mediators.
- **SUMOylation and ubiquitination**: Regulate channel stability and degradation.
- **Glycosylation**: Altered glycosylation patterns in diabetic neuropathy can modulate channel gating and expression.

### 4.3 Altered Biophysical Properties
- **Increased current density**: Peak sodium current density increases in small‑ and medium‑diameter DRG neurons after nerve injury, driven by increased channel number and enhanced single‑channel conductance.
- **Shifted voltage dependence**: Activation curves shift to more hyperpolarized potentials, while inactivation curves shift to more depolarized potentials, increasing the window current.
- **Enhanced ramp currents**: Especially prominent for Nav1.7, allowing activation by slow, sub‑threshold depolarizations.
- **Resurgent currents**: Some Nav1.7 mutations (and possibly wild‑type channels under pathological conditions) generate resurgent sodium currents that promote high‑frequency firing.

### 4.4 Contribution to Neuronal Hyperexcitability and Pain

- **Peripheral sensitization**: Increased Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 activity lowers the action potential threshold in nociceptor peripheral terminals, enhancing responses to thermal, mechanical, and chemical stimuli.
- **Ectopic firing**: After nerve injury, Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 accumulate at sites of axonal injury and in neuromas, generating spontaneous ectopic discharges that drive ongoing pain.
- **Central sensitization**: Enhanced sodium influx at the central terminals of nociceptors in the spinal dorsal horn increases glutamate and CGRP release, contributing to NMDAR‑dependent and NMDAR‑independent central sensitization.
- **Sympathetic‑sensory coupling**: In some neuropathic pain conditions, Nav1.7‑expressing sympathetic postganglionic neurons sprout into DRG, creating aberrant coupling that amplifies nociceptor activity.

## 5. Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 5.1 Genetic Knockout Studies

#### Nav1.7
- **Embryonic deletion**: Mice with Nav1.7 deletion in all sensory neurons (Advillin‑Cre) are profoundly analgesic but viable, with anosmia. Analgesia is mediated by enhanced endogenous opioid signalling (reversed by naloxone) and diminished neurotransmitter release, without loss of neuronal excitability[reference:10][reference:11].
- **Adult deletion**: Adult‑onset deletion of Nav1.7 in sensory neurons leads to analgesia through diminished sensory and autonomic neuron excitability, with no opioid component and no anosmia[reference:12].
- **Conditional knockout in Nav1.8‑positive neurons**: Deletion of Nav1.7 specifically in Nav1.8‑expressing neurons (which represent most nociceptors) recapitulates much of the analgesic phenotype, confirming the critical role of Nav1.7 in these cells.

#### Nav1.8
- **Global knockout**: Mice lacking Nav1.8 globally show profound deficits in mechanical and cold pain, as well as reduced inflammatory pain. Antagonists are effective analgesics in these models[reference:13].
- **Double knockout (Nav1.7 + Nav1.8)**: Surprisingly, double knockout mice still develop normal levels of neuropathic pain in some models, indicating that other sodium channels (e.g., Nav1.3) can compensate[reference:14].

### 5.2 Pharmacological Inhibitors

#### Selective Nav1.7 Blockers
- **ProTx‑II**: A peptide toxin from tarantula venom that potently and selectively blocks Nav1.7. Demonstrates strong analgesic efficacy in preclinical neuropathic and inflammatory pain models. However, its peptide nature limits clinical utility.
- **Small‑molecule Nav1.7 inhibitors (e.g., PF‑05089771, GDC‑0276, VX‑150)** : Showed robust target engagement and analgesic efficacy in preclinical models, but most have failed in Phase II clinical trials for neuropathic pain due to lack of efficacy or dose‑limiting side effects.
- **Aryl sulfonamide series (e.g., compound 52)** : A class of state‑dependent Nav1.7 inhibitors with long residence time that show improved efficacy in inflammatory and neuropathic pain models at low multiples of the IC50.

#### Selective Nav1.8 Blockers
- **A‑803467**: A prototypical, state‑dependent Nav1.8 blocker. Produces analgesia in multiple rodent neuropathic pain models (SNL, CCI, diabetic neuropathy) without affecting motor function or cardiac conduction.
- **PF‑01247324**: A selective Nav1.8 blocker that reached Phase II trials for neuropathic pain (discussed below).
- **VX‑548 (suzetrigine)** : A highly selective, orally bioavailable Nav1.8 inhibitor that blocks the channel in a state‑independent manner. Shows broad preclinical analgesic efficacy. In 2025, suzetrigine became the first FDA‑approved Nav1.8 inhibitor for the treatment of acute pain, and clinical trials for neuropathic pain are ongoing[reference:15][reference:16].

#### Dual and Multi‑Target Blockers
- **ANP‑230**: A state‑independent blocker that equipotently inhibits Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Nav1.9 with similar potency. Demonstrates dose‑dependent analgesic efficacy superior to pregabalin in multiple neuropathic and inflammatory pain models, with negligible motor impairment and peripheral‑restricted distribution. Potentiates morphine analgesia when co‑administered[reference:17][reference:18].
- **SGC‑PIKFYVE‑1**: An inhibitor of the lipid kinase PIKfyve that indirectly suppresses Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 currents without affecting calcium or potassium currents. Systemic administration alleviates mechanical and cold hypersensitivity in SNI and inflammatory pain models in both male and female mice, without motor impairment[reference:19].
- **Cilnidipine**: A dual N‑type calcium channel and Nav1.7/1.8 blocker; originally approved for hypertension, now under investigation for neuropathic pain.

### 5.3 siRNA and Gene Silencing
- **Nav1.7 siRNA**: Intrathecal delivery of siRNA targeting Nav1.7 reduces channel expression in DRG neurons and produces sustained analgesia in neuropathic pain models. Avoids the hyperthermia and anosmia associated with pharmacological antagonists.
- **Nav1.8 siRNA**: Similarly reduces pain behaviours, with the advantage of avoiding any effect on olfaction.
- **AAV‑delivered microRNA**: Viral vectors expressing microRNAs that target *SCN9A* or *SCN10A* 3′UTRs are under preclinical development for long‑term gene silencing.

### 5.4 Natural Compounds and Indirect Modulators
- **Resveratrol**: Suppresses Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 expression via SIRT1‑mediated NF‑κB inhibition.
- **Curcumin**: Reduces Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 expression and channel activity by inhibiting p38 MAPK and ERK signalling.
- **Electroacupuncture**: Alleviates neuropathic pain in part by reducing Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 expression and function in DRG neurons.

## 6. Clinical Development Landscape

### 6.1 Nav1.8: The Successful Path

| Agent | Mechanism | Clinical Status | Key Findings |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Suzetrigine (VX‑548)** | Selective Nav1.8 inhibitor | **FDA‑approved (2025)** for acute pain | Demonstrated significant reduction in acute pain scores versus placebo. Phase II/III trials for neuropathic pain are ongoing. |
| **PF‑04531083** (also known as PF‑01247324) | Selective Nav1.8 blocker | Phase II (completed/discontinued) | Showed analgesic trends in postherpetic neuralgia and painful diabetic neuropathy but did not meet primary endpoints. |
| **LTGO‑33** | Selective Nav1.8 blocker | Phase I | In early clinical development for neuropathic pain indications. |
| **OLP‑1002** | Subcutaneous peptide inhibitor of Nav1.8 | Phase I/II | Being investigated for chronic osteoarthritis pain. |

### 6.2 Nav1.7: A Challenging Road

Despite more than 15 years of intensive drug discovery efforts and dozens of clinical candidates, **no selective Nav1.7 inhibitor has successfully passed Phase II/III clinical trials** for neuropathic pain[reference:20].

| Agent | Mechanism | Clinical Status | Key Findings |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| **PF‑05089771** | Selective Nav1.7 blocker | Phase II (discontinued) | Failed to demonstrate efficacy in painful diabetic neuropathy and postherpetic neuralgia. |
| **GDC‑0276** | Selective Nav1.7 blocker | Phase I (discontinued) | Acceptable safety but development halted due to insufficient therapeutic window. |
| **VX‑150** | Selective Nav1.7 blocker | Phase II (completed) | Modest analgesic efficacy in acute pain and osteoarthritis; insufficient for neuropathic pain. |
| **Ralfinamide** | Mixed sodium channel blocker (Nav1.7, Nav1.3, Nav1.8) | Phase II/III (mixed results) | Some analgesic benefit in neuropathic pain but inconsistent across trials. |

### 6.3 Multi‑Target Approaches in Development

- **ANP‑230**: A peripherally restricted, state‑independent blocker of Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Nav1.9. Demonstrates potent, dose‑dependent analgesic efficacy superior to pregabalin in multiple preclinical pain models, with no motor impairment. In clinical testing[reference:21][reference:22].
- **HBW‑004285**: A next‑generation Nav1.8 blocker in Phase I trials.
- **Cilnidipine**: Repurposing of an approved antihypertensive that also blocks Nav1.7 and Nav1.8; under investigation for neuropathic pain.

### 6.4 Pharmacological Challenges and Lessons Learned

- **Insufficient target occupancy in human DRG**: Preclinical efficacy requires >90% channel occupancy, but achieving this in human DRG at tolerable doses has proven difficult for selective Nav1.7 inhibitors.
- **Species differences**: Rodent and human Nav1.7 have subtle biophysical and pharmacological differences that may affect inhibitor binding and efficacy.
- **State‑dependent vs. state‑independent inhibition**: Many early Nav1.7 inhibitors were state‑dependent (preferentially binding inactivated channels). Under conditions of rapid firing in neuropathic pain, a higher proportion of channels may be in open or resting states, limiting inhibitor efficacy.
- **Functional redundancy**: As nociceptors can achieve similar excitability using different Nav subtypes, blocking only Nav1.7 may be insufficient when Nav1.3 and Nav1.8 are upregulated[reference:23].
- **Central and autonomic expression of Nav1.7**: Nav1.7 is expressed in sympathetic ganglia and CNS neurons beyond nociceptors. Systemic pharmacological inhibition leads to dose‑limiting autonomic side effects (e.g., hypotension, bradycardia)[reference:24].

## 7. Sex Differences

- **Nav1.8‑MOP knockouts**: Male and female Nav1.8‑MOP double knockout mice show similar heat hyperalgesia to wild‑type littermates. However, all female genotypes were more sensitive to heat than their respective male groups, indicating sex‑dependent modulation of thermal pain pathways that may involve Nav1.8[reference:25].
- **Nav1.7 mutations**: The clinical penetrance and expressivity of SCN9A mutations can differ between sexes, possibly due to hormonal modulation of channel expression.
- **Preclinical drug efficacy**: Some Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 inhibitors have shown sex‑dependent analgesic efficacy in rodent models, though data remain limited and inconsistent.
- **PIKfyve inhibitor targeting Nav1.7/1.8**: The PIKfyve inhibitor SGC‑PIKFYVE‑1 alleviated neuropathic pain in **both male and female mice**, suggesting that indirect modulation of Nav1.7/1.8 may overcome sex‑specific differences seen with direct channel blockers[reference:26].

## 8. Clinical Implications and Future Directions

### 8.1 Precision Medicine
- **Genotype‑guided therapy**: Patients with gain‑of‑function *SCN9A* or *SCN10A* mutations may be especially responsive to Nav1.7‑ or Nav1.8‑targeted therapies. Genetic screening could identify individuals most likely to benefit.
- **Sensory profiling**: Quantitative sensory testing (QST) may identify patient subgroups with a predominant sodium channel‑mediated pain phenotype (e.g., paroxysmal, electric‑shock‑like pain) that could preferentially respond to Nav channel blockade.

### 8.2 Emerging Strategies
- **Multi‑target or broad‑spectrum Nav blockade**: Compounds like ANP‑230 that block Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Nav1.9 simultaneously may overcome the functional redundancy that limits isoform‑selective inhibitors.
- **Peripheral restriction**: Ensuring that Nav inhibitors do not cross the blood‑brain barrier and have minimal activity at cardiac Nav1.5 channels reduces central and cardiac side effects.
- **Combination therapy**: Co‑administration of Nav inhibitors with:
    - **Opioids**: ANP‑230 potentiates morphine analgesia, allowing lower opioid doses.
    - **Gabapentinoids**: Synergistic reduction of neuronal excitability by targeting distinct channels (calcium vs. sodium).
    - **Anti‑inflammatory agents**: Reducing the inflammatory milieu that sensitizes Nav channels.
- **Gene therapy and siRNA**: Long‑term silencing of Nav1.7 or Nav1.8 in nociceptors using AAV‑delivered microRNAs or RNA interference could provide sustained analgesia without the need for daily dosing or systemic exposure.

### 8.3 Biomarker Potential
- **Circulating Nav fragments**: Proteolytic fragments of Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 released into CSF or blood after nerve injury could serve as biomarkers of channel upregulation and neuronal damage.
- **microRNA signatures**: Specific circulating miRNA profiles (e.g., miR‑182, miR‑30b) may reflect DRG Nav channel expression and could guide treatment selection.
- **Skin biopsy**: Quantification of Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 protein in intra‑epidermal nerve fibres may indicate the degree of peripheral sensitization and predict response to Nav‑targeted therapies.

## 9. Caveats and Complexity

- **Constitutive vs. pharmacological ablation**: The profound analgesia observed in Nav1.7‑null humans and mice is not recapitulated by pharmacological inhibition, likely because: (a) embryonic deletion triggers compensatory changes (upregulation of Nav1.1, Nav1.2, β4 subunits) that are not elicited by acute blockade; and (b) complete, sustained loss of channel function is not achievable with current inhibitors at tolerable doses[reference:27].
- **Paradoxical effects**: Some Nav1.7 blockers have shown hyperalgesic or pro‑nociceptive effects at low doses in certain pain models, possibly due to differential block of tonic versus phasic channel activity.
- **Off‑target effects on Nav1.5**: Cardiac Nav1.5 is essential for myocardial action potential propagation. Many early Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 inhibitors had insufficient selectivity over Nav1.5, raising cardiovascular safety concerns.
- **The double‑edged sword of TTX‑resistance**: Nav1.8's TTX resistance, which makes it an attractive target, also complicates electrophysiological screening, as heterologous expression systems must control for endogenous TTX‑sensitive currents.
- **Injury‑type and temporal dependence**: The relative contribution of Nav1.7 versus Nav1.8 to neuropathic pain varies with the type of nerve injury (traumatic, metabolic, toxic) and the time after injury, with some models showing a switch from Nav1.7‑ to Nav1.8‑dependent hyperexcitability in the chronic phase.
- **Compensatory upregulation**: In Nav1.8‑knockout mice, Nav1.7 and Nav1.9 are upregulated, and in Nav1.7‑knockout mice, Nav1.8 is upregulated. This functional plasticity may limit the durability of single‑channel‑targeted monotherapy.

## 10. Integration with the Wider Pain Network

The Nav1.7/Nav1.8 system is deeply interconnected with many of the pathways and targets previously discussed in this series:

- **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 form supramolecular active clusters with TrkB in DRG neurons. BDNF/TrkB signalling can enhance Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 trafficking and phosphorylation, while Nav‑mediated depolarization can promote BDNF release from nociceptor terminals.
- **TRPV1**: PKC‑mediated phosphorylation of both Nav1.7/Nav1.8 and TRPV1 occurs downstream of common inflammatory mediators. Nav channel activity shapes the depolarization that recruits TRPV1, and TRPV1‑mediated Ca²⁺ influx can modulate Nav channel trafficking.
- **TNF‑α and IL‑1β**: Pro‑inflammatory cytokines upregulate Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 expression in DRG neurons via NF‑κB and MAPK signalling and enhance channel activity through PKC and PKA.
- **P2X receptors**: ATP‑mediated depolarization through P2X3 and P2X2/3 receptors on nociceptors can activate Nav1.7‑dependent firing, linking purinergic and sodium channel signalling.
- **CX3CL1/CX3CR1 and CCL2/CCR2**: Chemokine‑activated intracellular kinases (ERK, p38) phosphorylate Nav1.7 and Nav1.8, enhancing their activity.
- **RhoA/ROCK**: RhoA/ROCK‑mediated cytoskeletal changes can alter Nav channel clustering and membrane localization at nodes of Ranvier and axon initial segments.
- **NLRP3 inflammasome**: IL‑1β released from the NLRP3 inflammasome sensitizes Nav channels, and Nav‑mediated depolarization may enhance ATP release, further driving inflammasome activation.
- **KCC2**: The disinhibition caused by KCC2 downregulation amplifies the postsynaptic response to Nav‑dependent neurotransmitter release, making Nav‑mediated hyperexcitability more behaviourally consequential.
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction**: ATP depletion impairs Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase function, increasing intracellular sodium and depolarizing the resting membrane potential, which facilitates Nav1.7 activation.
- **Perineuronal nets**: Loss of PNNs around PV interneurons reduces inhibitory tone, amplifying the downstream effects of Nav‑driven nociceptor hyperactivity.
