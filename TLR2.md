# Involvement of Toll‑Like Receptor 2 (TLR2) in Neuropathic Pain

Toll‑like receptor 2 (TLR2) is a pattern‑recognition receptor (PRR) that plays a fundamental role in the innate immune system by detecting conserved microbial components and endogenous danger signals released from damaged cells. In the context of neuropathic pain, TLR2 is significantly upregulated in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), spinal dorsal horn, and peripheral nerves following injury, where it acts as a key initiator and amplifier of neuroinflammation. Activated by a broad array of **damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)** —including high‑mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), heat‑shock proteins, S100A8/A9, and fragments of fibronectin—TLR2 drives MyD88‑dependent signalling cascades that converge on NF‑κB and mitogen‑activated protein kinases (MAPKs). This leads to the robust production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1), and reactive oxygen species from microglia, astrocytes, and infiltrating macrophages. The resulting neuroinflammatory milieu enhances primary afferent excitability, promotes central sensitisation, and disrupts inhibitory neurotransmission, thereby contributing directly to mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain. Pharmacological blockade of TLR2 with neutralising antibodies or small‑molecule antagonists, as well as genetic deletion of *Tlr2*, consistently reduces pain behaviours and neuroinflammation in multiple preclinical models, including traumatic nerve injury, diabetic neuropathy, and chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy. Despite this robust validation, clinical translation faces challenges due to the essential role of TLR2 in host defence against Gram‑positive bacteria and the functional redundancy with other TLRs and innate immune receptors.

## 🧬 The TLR2 System: Receptor, Ligands, and Signalling

*   **Receptor structure and heterodimerisation**: TLR2 is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein composed of an extracellular leucine‑rich repeat (LRR) domain, a single transmembrane helix, and an intracellular Toll/interleukin‑1 receptor (TIR) domain. Unlike TLR4, which homodimerises, TLR2 functions as a heterodimer with either **TLR1** or **TLR6**. The TLR2/TLR1 heterodimer primarily recognises triacylated lipopeptides (e.g., from mycobacteria), whereas the TLR2/TLR6 heterodimer recognises diacylated lipopeptides (e.g., from Gram‑positive bacteria). This partnership expands the ligand repertoire and determines the specificity of the downstream response.
*   **Accessory molecules**: Full TLR2 activation often requires the co‑receptor **CD14**, which facilitates the transfer of ligands to the receptor complex. In some contexts, **CD36** also serves as a co‑receptor for specific DAMPs.
*   **Endogenous ligands (DAMPs) relevant to neuropathic pain**: After peripheral nerve injury, stressed and necrotic cells release a variety of molecules that serve as TLR2 agonists:
    *   **High‑mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)** : A nuclear protein passively released from necrotic cells or actively secreted by activated immune cells and microglia.
    *   **Heat‑shock proteins (HSP60, HSP70, HSP90)** : Released from damaged or hyperactive neurons and glial cells.
    *   **S100A8/A9 (calprotectin)** : An alarmin released from neutrophils, macrophages, and microglia.
    *   **Biglycan and other extracellular matrix fragments**: Generated by matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity after tissue injury.
    *   **Amyloid‑β and α‑synuclein**: Aggregated proteins that accumulate under conditions of cellular stress and degeneration.
*   **Signalling pathways**: Ligand‑induced heterodimerisation of TLR2/TLR1 or TLR2/TLR6 recruits the adaptor proteins **TIRAP (Mal)** and **MyD88** to the intracellular TIR domain. This leads to the assembly of the **Myddosome** complex, which activates:
    *   **NF‑κB pathway**: Through IRAK1/4 and TRAF6, leading to IKK‑mediated phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, allowing NF‑κB (p50/p65) to translocate to the nucleus and drive the transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and inducible enzymes (iNOS, COX‑2).
    *   **MAPK pathways (ERK1/2, p38, JNK)** : These kinases regulate cytokine mRNA stability and translation, and contribute to glial activation and neuronal sensitisation.
    *   **PI3K/Akt pathway**: Engaged in certain cell types to promote cell survival and, paradoxically, can contribute to the chronicity of inflammation.
    *   Notably, TLR2 signalling is **MyD88‑dependent but TRIF‑independent**, which distinguishes it from TLR4 and results in a more restricted profile of gene induction (lacking the IRF3/type I interferon arm).
*   **Cellular expression in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **Primary sensory neurons (DRG)** : TLR2 is constitutively expressed at low levels in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. After nerve injury, its expression is markedly upregulated, and it co‑localises with TRPV1, Nav1.8, and CGRP.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: TLR2 is expressed on **microglia** and **astrocytes**, with microglia being the primary cell type that upregulates TLR2 following injury. Some expression is also found on dorsal horn neurons.
    *   **Peripheral nerve**: TLR2 is expressed on **Schwann cells** and **infiltrating macrophages**, where it participates in Wallerian degeneration and local inflammation.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: TLR2 is present on microglia in the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and periaqueductal grey, contributing to pain‑related cognitive and affective dysfunction.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of TLR2 in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury, TLR2 is rapidly engaged and sustains a pro‑inflammatory programme that amplifies nociceptive signalling at multiple levels of the neuraxis.

### 1. Upregulation of TLR2 and Its Ligands After Nerve Injury

*   **Time course**: In models such as chronic constriction injury (CCI), spared nerve injury (SNI), and spinal nerve ligation (SNL), TLR2 mRNA and protein levels in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn increase within 1–3 days, peak at 7–14 days, and can remain elevated for weeks. Similar upregulation occurs in the DRG and the injured nerve itself.
*   **Ligand availability**: In parallel, the endogenous TLR2 ligands HMGB1, HSP70, and S100A8/A9 accumulate in the spinal cord extracellular space, released from damaged neurons, reactive microglia, and astrocytes. This creates a local environment of continuous TLR2 stimulation.
*   **Cellular source**: Double‑label immunofluorescence shows that the injury‑induced increase in TLR2 is primarily in **Iba‑1‑positive microglia** and, to a lesser extent, in **GFAP‑positive astrocytes**. Some studies also report increased neuronal TLR2 expression.

### 2. TLR2‑Driven Microglial and Astrocytic Activation

TLR2 is a critical molecular switch for the pro‑inflammatory polarisation of spinal glial cells.

*   **Microglial M1 polarisation**: DAMPs binding to TLR2/TLR1 or TLR2/TLR6 on microglia activate the MyD88–NF‑κB pathway, driving the expression of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1), and the inducible enzymes iNOS and COX‑2. This M1 phenotype is a major source of pronociceptive mediators.
*   **Astrogliosis**: Microglial‑derived IL‑1β and TNF‑α act on astrocytic TLR2 and IL‑1R/TNFR, triggering reactive astrogliosis. Reactive astrocytes downregulate glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), increasing extracellular glutamate, and secrete additional chemokines that sustain microglial recruitment and activation.
*   **Microglia–astrocyte positive‑feedback loop**: TLR2‑activated microglia release IL‑1β, which stimulates astrocytes to release further DAMPs (e.g., ATP) and chemokines, which in turn activate more microglia. This bidirectional communication creates a self‑perpetuating cycle of neuroinflammation that maintains chronic pain.

### 3. Direct Sensitisation of Primary Afferent Neurons

TLR2 is functionally expressed on nociceptor cell bodies and peripheral terminals, where its direct activation contributes to peripheral sensitisation.

*   **Increased neuronal excitability**: Activation of TLR2 on DRG neurons by HMGB1 or synthetic TLR2 agonists (Pam3CSK4) leads to:
    *   **Increased intracellular calcium** through both release from intracellular stores and influx via voltage‑gated channels.
    *   **Enhancement of TRPV1 and TRPA1 currents** through PKA‑ and PKC‑mediated phosphorylation, resulting in thermal and chemical hypersensitivity.
    *   **Modulation of voltage‑gated sodium channels** (Nav1.7, Nav1.8), increasing sodium current density and promoting ectopic firing.
*   **CGRP release**: TLR2 activation stimulates calcium‑dependent exocytosis of calcitonin gene‑related peptide (CGRP) from nociceptor terminals, contributing to neurogenic inflammation and central sensitisation.
*   **Crosstalk with NGF‑TrkA**: TLR2 signalling can potentiate NGF‑mediated sensitisation, as both pathways converge on MAPK and NF‑κB.

### 4. Spinal Central Sensitisation

TLR2‑driven neuroinflammation in the spinal cord promotes the development and maintenance of central sensitisation through multiple parallel mechanisms.

*   **NMDA receptor potentiation**: TNF‑α and IL‑1β released by TLR2‑activated microglia phosphorylate the GluN2B subunit of NMDA receptors on dorsal horn neurons via Src and PKC, increasing their calcium conductance and promoting long‑term potentiation (LTP) at C‑fibre synapses.
*   **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2 disinhibition**: Microglial‑derived BDNF, produced downstream of TLR2/NF‑κB, acts on neuronal TrkB receptors to downregulate the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2. This collapse of the chloride gradient converts GABA/glycine‑mediated inhibition into excitation, a core mechanism of mechanical allodynia.
*   **Loss of inhibitory interneurons**: Sustained TLR2‑driven neuroinflammation and excitotoxicity contribute to the caspase‑3‑dependent apoptosis of parvalbumin‑positive (PV⁺) GABAergic interneurons, as described in the dedicated sections on interneuron loss. TLR2 thus indirectly promotes the structural disinhibition of spinal nociceptive circuits.
*   **Prostaglandin synthesis**: TLR2‑mediated induction of COX‑2 in microglia and astrocytes increases prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂) production, which further sensitises spinal neurons and amplifies the inflammatory cascade.

### 5. Peripheral Nerve and DRG Inflammation

*   **Schwann cell activation**: In the injured nerve, TLR2 on Schwann cells is activated by DAMPs released from damaged axons. This triggers Schwann cell dedifferentiation, proliferation, and the secretion of pro‑inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that recruit macrophages and neutrophils to the injury site. These immune cells further release pronociceptive mediators that sensitise the axon and contribute to neuroma formation.
*   **Macrophage infiltration**: TLR2 is crucial for the chemotaxis and activation of macrophages in the injured nerve and DRG. Macrophage‑derived TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and NGF sensitise nociceptor cell bodies and axons, contributing to spontaneous pain and evoked hypersensitivity.

### 6. Contribution to Diabetic Neuropathy and Metabolic Pain

*   **TLR2 in diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN)** : Hyperglycaemia and the formation of advanced glycation end‑products (AGEs) generate oxidative stress and release DAMPs that upregulate TLR2 in DRG neurons and spinal glia. TLR2 activation contributes to the small‑fibre degeneration and mechanical allodynia characteristic of DPN. High‑fat diet (HFD)‑induced obese mice with diabetes show elevated TLR2 expression in the DRG and spinal cord, and TLR2 knockout reduces their pain phenotype.
*   **Chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN)** : Paclitaxel and oxaliplatin increase the expression of TLR2 and its endogenous ligands in the DRG and spinal cord. TLR2‑mediated neuroinflammation is a key driver of the mechanical and cold allodynia observed in CIPN.

### 7. Supraspinal TLR2 and Pain Comorbidities

*   **Hippocampal TLR2**: Chronic neuropathic pain upregulates TLR2 in hippocampal microglia, leading to neuroinflammation that impairs neurogenesis and long‑term potentiation. This is associated with the cognitive deficits (memory loss, reduced executive function) frequently seen in chronic pain patients.
*   **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and amygdala**: TLR2‑mediated microglial activation in the ACC and amygdala contributes to the anxiety‑ and depression‑like behaviours that accompany chronic pain.

### 8. Crosstalk with TLR4 and Synergistic Activation

TLR2 and TLR4 are often co‑activated after nerve injury, as both recognise overlapping DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1). Their simultaneous engagement can produce **synergistic amplification** of NF‑κB and MAPK signalling, resulting in a supra‑additive production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines. This crosstalk is critical for the full expression of neuropathic pain. Targeting a single TLR may be insufficient if the other remains active; dual inhibition may be required.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Pharmacological TLR2 Antagonists

*   **Anti‑TLR2 neutralising antibodies**: Intrathecal or systemic administration of monoclonal antibodies that block TLR2 reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNL, and diabetic neuropathy models. The analgesic effect is associated with reduced spinal microglial activation (Iba‑1), decreased levels of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6, and restoration of KCC2 expression.
*   **TLR2‑blocking peptides**: Peptides that inhibit TLR2/TLR1 or TLR2/TLR6 heterodimerisation have been developed and shown to reduce pain behaviours and neuroinflammation in rodent models.
*   **Small‑molecule TLR2 inhibitors** (e.g., **C29**, **CU‑CPT22**): These compounds block the TIR domain dimerisation and subsequent MyD88 recruitment. Systemic or intrathecal administration attenuates mechanical allodynia and suppresses spinal cytokine production in nerve‑injured animals. Their oral bioavailability and CNS penetration vary among compounds, and none are yet approved for clinical use.

### 2. Genetic Models

*   ***Tlr2*‑/‑ mice**: Mice lacking TLR2 are viable, fertile, and exhibit normal acute pain thresholds. In models of CCI, SNI, SNL, and diabetic neuropathy, *Tlr2*‑/‑ mice show:
    *   A marked reduction in mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
    *   Significantly attenuated microglial and astrocytic activation in the spinal dorsal horn.
    *   Lower levels of spinal TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, CCL2, and BDNF.
    *   Preservation of GABAergic interneuron numbers and KCC2 expression.
*   **Conditional TLR2 knockout**: Deletion of TLR2 specifically in CX3CR1‑positive cells (microglia/macrophages) recapitulates the pain‑protective phenotype, confirming that **microglial TLR2 is the primary cellular driver** of neuropathic pain. Neuronal TLR2 deletion also partially reduces pain, consistent with a role in peripheral sensitisation.

### 3. Targeting Downstream Pathways

*   **TAK‑242 (resatorvid)** : A TLR4‑selective antagonist; it does not directly block TLR2, but its analgesic effects in models where both TLRs are co‑activated illustrate that combined TLR2/TLR4 blockade may be needed.
*   **Inhibitors of MyD88 or NF‑κB**: Blocking the shared downstream signalling of TLR2, TLR4, and IL‑1R with MyD88 inhibitors or IKK/NF‑κB inhibitors reduces neuropathic pain, but these are less specific and carry higher immunosuppressive risk.

### 4. Natural Compounds

*   **Curcumin**: Suppresses TLR2 expression and TLR2‑mediated NF‑κB activation in spinal microglia, contributing to its analgesic effects in CCI and diabetic neuropathy models.
*   **Resveratrol**: Activates SIRT1, which deacetylates NF‑κB, indirectly reducing TLR2‑driven inflammation and pain.
*   **Sulforaphane**: Activates Nrf2, which inhibits TLR2‑mediated NF‑κB activity and reduces oxidative stress‑driven DAMP release.

### 5. Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces TLR2 expression in the DRG and spinal dorsal horn, suppresses microglial activation, and alleviates mechanical allodynia in nerve‑injured rats, in part by modulating the TLR2/MyD88/NF‑κB pathway.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **TLR2 as a therapeutic target in neuropathic pain**: The robust and consistent preclinical data position TLR2 as a promising analgesic target. Because TLR2 is primarily engaged in pathological states (injury‑induced DAMPs) rather than basal physiological sensing, its blockade may offer a wider therapeutic window than targeting more broadly essential molecules.
*   **Drug development**: No selective TLR2 antagonist has yet entered clinical trials for pain. However, several small‑molecule TLR2 inhibitors are in preclinical development for inflammatory conditions (e.g., sepsis, atherosclerosis) and could be repurposed. The primary challenges are achieving adequate CNS penetration, isoform selectivity (sparing TLR2/TLR1 vs. TLR2/TLR6 inhibition as needed), and avoiding systemic immunosuppression.
*   **Biomarker potential**: Soluble TLR2 (sTLR2), shed from the plasma membrane, is elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid and plasma of patients with chronic neuropathic pain. sTLR2 could serve as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker, or as a marker of target engagement in future clinical trials.
*   **Combination therapies**:
    *   **TLR2 antagonist + TLR4 antagonist**: Given the synergistic amplification of neuroinflammation by co‑activated TLR2 and TLR4, dual blockade may be significantly more effective than single‑receptor inhibition.
    *   **TLR2 antagonist + anti‑TNF or anti‑IL‑6 biologics**: Blocking upstream innate immunity (TLR2) while neutralising the downstream cytokine storm could produce broad‑spectrum analgesia with lower doses of each agent.
    *   **TLR2 antagonist + KCC2 enhancer**: TLR2 blockade reduces BDNF‑driven KCC2 downregulation; combining with a direct KCC2 activator could synergistically restore GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition.
    *   **TLR2 antagonist + opioid**: TLR2 antagonism may prevent opioid‑induced microglial activation and hyperalgesia, allowing more effective opioid analgesia.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Essential role in host defence**: TLR2 is a major sensor of Gram‑positive bacterial infections (e.g., *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*). Chronic systemic TLR2 blockade increases the risk of serious skin, soft‑tissue, and respiratory infections. Localised delivery (intrathecal) or intermittent dosing may be required to maintain protective immunity.
*   **Redundancy among TLRs and innate immune receptors**: TLR2, TLR4, TLR3, TLR7, TLR9, and RAGE can all recognise DAMPs released after nerve injury. Blocking TLR2 alone may result in compensatory upregulation or sustained signalling through other receptors, limiting the durability of TLR2‑targeted monotherapy.
*   **TLR2 in tissue repair and regeneration**: TLR2 signalling on Schwann cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells contributes to wound healing, angiogenesis, and the early stages of nerve regeneration. Complete, prolonged TLR2 blockade could impair these reparative processes, delaying functional recovery after injury.
*   **TLR2 in the CNS**: TLR2 is involved in hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. Chronic central TLR2 inhibition could have unintended effects on learning, memory, and mood regulation, particularly in the context of an already cognitively vulnerable chronic pain population.
*   **Sex differences**: Preclinical studies have shown that the contribution of TLR2 to neuropathic pain may be more prominent in male rodents, while females may rely more on TLR4 or adaptive immune pathways. TLR2‑targeted therapies may therefore exhibit sex‑dependent efficacy, and clinical trials must be stratified accordingly.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**:
    *   **TLR4**: TLR2 and TLR4 are often co‑activated and synergise; they share MyD88‑dependent signalling and amplify each other's effects.
    *   **NLRP3 inflammasome**: TLR2 provides the priming signal (NF‑κB‑mediated upregulation of NLRP3 and pro‑IL‑1β) for NLRP3 inflammasome activation, placing it upstream of IL‑1β maturation.
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: These are the primary downstream effectors of TLR2‑driven neuroinflammation.
    *   **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: TLR2‑activated microglia release BDNF, which downregulates KCC2 and causes disinhibition.
    *   **CCL2/CCR2 and CX3CL1/CX3CR1**: TLR2 signalling induces the expression of these chemokines, promoting microglial and monocyte recruitment.
    *   **TRPV1, TRPA1, Nav1.7/Nav1.8**: TLR2‑mediated intracellular signalling directly sensitises these pronociceptive ion channels in nociceptors.
    *   **P2X4/P2X7 receptors**: TLR2 can upregulate these ATP‑gated purinergic receptors on microglia, sensitising them to ATP and amplifying the microglial activation loop.
