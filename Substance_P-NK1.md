# Involvement of Substance P / NK1 Receptor in Neuropathic Pain

Substance P (SP) is an 11‑amino acid neuropeptide of the tachykinin family that acts as a key mediator of nociceptive transmission and neurogenic inflammation. In the context of neuropathic pain, SP is released from primary afferent nociceptors and engages the neurokinin‑1 (NK1) receptor—a Gq‑coupled GPCR—at multiple levels of the neuraxis. This signaling drives central sensitization by potentiating NMDA receptor function, activating spinal microglia and astrocytes, and amplifying pro‑inflammatory cytokine cascades. Simultaneously, SP/NK1R signaling in limbic and cortical regions contributes to the emotional‑affective dimensions of chronic pain. Interestingly, SP also exerts antinociceptive actions in certain brain regions (e.g., striatum, periaqueductal grey) depending on concentration, receptor subtype, and circuit context, reflecting a functional duality. Preclinical blockade of NK1 receptors consistently reduces mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and pain‑related anxiety; however, clinical translation has been challenging, with multiple NK1 antagonists failing in Phase II/III pain trials. Nonetheless, the recent approval of NK1 antagonists for pruritus, the exploration of biased agonists, and combination strategies with CGRP‑targeted therapies have renewed interest in this pathway for chronic pain management.

## 🧬 The Substance P/NK1 System: Ligand, Receptor, and Physiological Functions

*   **Substance P structure and gene**: SP is an undecapeptide (RPKPQQFFGLM‑NH₂) derived from the preprotachykinin‑A gene (*TAC1*). It belongs to the tachykinin family, which also includes neurokinin A (NKA) and neurokinin B (NKB), each with distinct receptor affinities.
*   **NK1 receptor (TACR1)** : A Gαq/11‑coupled seven‑transmembrane receptor that activates phospholipase C, leading to IP₃‑mediated intracellular Ca²⁺ release and diacylglycerol‑dependent PKC activation. NK1 receptor activation also stimulates MAP kinase (ERK, p38) and NF‑κB pathways. The receptor undergoes rapid ligand‑induced internalization, a feature used as a marker of SP release in vivo.
*   **Distribution**:
    *   **Primary sensory neurons**: SP is synthesized in small‑ and medium‑diameter DRG neurons (C‑fibre nociceptors) and transported to both peripheral and central terminals. Co‑localization with CGRP and glutamate is common.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: Dense SP‑positive terminals in laminae I and II; NK1 receptors are highly expressed on postsynaptic projection neurons, local interneurons, and glial cells (microglia and astrocytes).
    *   **Brain**: NK1 receptors are abundant in the amygdala, hypothalamus, periaqueductal grey (PAG), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), striatum, and brainstem nuclei involved in autonomic and affective regulation.
    *   **Peripheral tissues**: SP is released from sensory nerve endings onto immune cells (mast cells, macrophages, dendritic cells), vascular endothelium, and keratinocytes, driving neurogenic inflammation.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of Substance P/NK1 in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury (traumatic, metabolic, or toxic), SP/NK1R signaling is persistently amplified at every level of the pain pathway.

### 1. Upregulation and Phenotypic Switch After Nerve Injury

*   **SP synthesis and release**: In the days to weeks after nerve injury, SP mRNA and peptide levels increase in the ipsilateral DRG and spinal dorsal horn. SP is released both basally and in response to noxious stimuli from the central terminals of nociceptors.
*   **Phenotypic switch of Aβ‑fibres**: Large myelinated low‑threshold mechanoreceptors (Aβ‑fibres) normally do not express SP. After nerve injury, a subset of these fibres *de novo* synthesizes and releases SP, substance P, and other nociceptive markers, directly contributing to mechanical allodynia.
*   **NK1 receptor upregulation**: Spinal NK1 receptor expression is upregulated on dorsal horn neurons and glial cells. In the amygdala and ACC, NK1 receptor expression also increases, correlating with pain‑related anxiety behaviours.
*   **Internalization as a readout**: Sustained SP release leads to massive NK1 receptor internalization in lamina I neurons, visible by immunohistochemistry. This internalization is a direct indicator of ongoing nociceptor activity and is blocked by NK1 antagonists and by analgesics such as opioids.

### 2. Peripheral Sensitization and Neurogenic Inflammation

SP released from the peripheral terminals of nociceptors initiates a cascade of events that sensitize primary afferent endings.

*   **Direct nociceptor sensitization**: SP, via NK1 autoreceptors on the same or adjacent nociceptors, enhances TRPV1 and TRPA1 currents through PKC‑mediated phosphorylation. It also modulates voltage‑gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8), increasing excitability.
*   **Immune cell activation**: SP is a potent chemoattractant and activator of mast cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells. Activated immune cells release histamine, serotonin, TNF‑α, IL‑1β, NGF, and additional SP, creating a positive‑feedback loop of neurogenic inflammation.
*   **Vascular effects**: SP induces vasodilation and increased vascular permeability via NK1 receptors on endothelial cells, promoting oedema and facilitating immune cell infiltration into the nerve and surrounding tissue.

### 3. Spinal Central Sensitization and Glial Activation

SP released from central nociceptor terminals engages NK1 receptors on spinal dorsal horn neurons and glial cells to powerfully amplify synaptic transmission.

*   **Postsynaptic excitation**: SP binding to NK1 receptors on lamina I projection neurons depolarizes the membrane through inhibition of K⁺ channels and activation of non‑selective cation conductances, producing a slow, sustained EPSP that lasts for tens of seconds. This temporal summation facilitates the temporal integration of nociceptive inputs.
*   **NMDA receptor potentiation**: The PKC and Src kinases activated downstream of NK1 receptors phosphorylate the GluN2B subunit of NMDA receptors, relieving their Mg²⁺ block and increasing calcium permeability. This is a central mechanism of wind‑up and long‑term potentiation at nociceptive synapses.
*   **Microglial and astrocytic activation**: NK1 receptors are expressed on spinal microglia and astrocytes. SP binding triggers microglial release of BDNF, TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and reactive oxygen species. Microglial‑derived BDNF acts on neuronal TrkB receptors to downregulate KCC2, collapsing the chloride gradient and converting GABA/glycine‑mediated inhibition into excitation—the canonical disinhibition mechanism of mechanical allodynia.
*   **Loss of GABAergic inhibition**: SP directly suppresses GABA release from inhibitory interneurons through presynaptic NK1 receptors and, via the glial‑dependent pathway, impairs the efficacy of remaining GABAergic synapses.

### 4. Supraspinal SP/NK1 and Pain Comorbidities

SP/NK1 signaling in limbic and cortical regions modulates the emotional and cognitive dimensions of chronic pain.

*   **Amygdala**: The central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA) is rich in NK1 receptors. SP released in the CeA during pain increases anxiety‑like behaviours. NK1 receptor blockade in the CeA reduces pain‑related anxiety, but its effects on mechanical allodynia are complex and can be sex‑dependent (greater efficacy in females in some models).
*   **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** : SP/NK1R activation in the ACC enhances excitatory synaptic transmission and contributes to the aversive quality of pain. NK1 antagonists injected into the ACC reduce the emotional‑affective component without altering sensory thresholds.
*   **Periaqueductal grey (PAG)** : SP in the PAG can have **biphasic effects**: low concentrations facilitate descending inhibition (analgesia) through OFF‑cell activation, while high concentrations facilitate pain through ON‑cell excitation. This dual role complicates global NK1 antagonism.
*   **Striatum**: A notable antinociceptive function of SP exists in the striatum. Continuous infusion of SP into the striatum relieves neuropathic pain in rodents via NK1 and muscarinic M4 receptor‑mediated mechanisms, suggesting a pain‑suppressive role in this region.

### 5. Osteoarthritis and Inflammatory Chronic Pain

In models of osteoarthritis‑induced chronic pain, the SP/NK1R axis plays a critical role in the transition from acute to persistent pain.

*   Early, repeated intra‑articular injection of SP produces long‑lasting joint pain and inflammation, associated with increased macrophage activation, pro‑inflammatory cytokine expression (CCL2, TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), and spinal microgliosis.
*   Early NK1 receptor blockade with antagonists (e.g., **L‑733,060**) suppresses these inflammatory processes and prevents the development of chronic pain, highlighting the importance of targeting the pathway before irreversible central changes occur.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. NK1 Receptor Antagonists

*   **First‑generation (quinuclidine and piperidine series)** :
    *   **CP‑96,345** and **RP‑67580**: Prototypical non‑peptide NK1 antagonists. Intrathecal or systemic administration reduces mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spinal Fos expression in models of chronic constriction injury (CCI), spinal nerve ligation (SNL), and diabetic neuropathy.
    *   **L‑733,060**: A brain‑penetrant NK1 antagonist that reduces pain behaviours and anxiety‑like responses. Effective in the early stage of osteoarthritis pain when given intra‑articularly.
*   **Aprepitant (MK‑869)** : A potent, centrally active NK1 antagonist approved for chemotherapy‑induced nausea and vomiting. In preclinical neuropathic pain models, aprepitant shows variable efficacy; it is more effective when administered before or shortly after injury, with reduced efficacy in established chronic pain. Its failure in human pain clinical trials (see below) has been attributed to species differences in NK1 pharmacology.
*   **Casopitant, orvepitant, and netupitant**: Other NK1 antagonists with mixed clinical outcomes. Orvepitant has shown some promise in animal models of inflammatory pain, but human data are limited.

### 2. Genetic Approaches

*   **NK1 receptor knockout mice (TACR1⁻/⁻)** : These mice exhibit attenuated mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia after nerve injury and inflammation. They also show reduced anxiety‑ and depression‑like behaviours, confirming the role of NK1 in pain affect. However, some compensatory upregulation of NK2 and NK3 receptors may occur.
*   **Substance P / TAC1 knockout**: Mice lacking SP show reduced neurogenic inflammation and moderately attenuated pain behaviours, but residual pain indicates the contribution of other tachykinins and co‑transmitters.

### 3. Substance P‑Saporin (SP‑SAP)

*   This is a neurotoxin conjugate consisting of SP linked to the ribosome‑inactivating protein saporin. It is internalized via NK1 receptors and selectively ablates NK1‑expressing lamina I projection neurons. Intrathecal SP‑SAP produces profound and long‑lasting analgesia in models of neuropathic, inflammatory, and cancer pain, with preservation of motor function and acute nociception. However, the irreversibility and potential for off‑target effects limit its clinical translation.

### 4. siRNA and Viral Silencing

*   Intrathecal delivery of siRNA targeting *TACR1* (NK1 receptor) reduces receptor expression in the spinal cord, attenuates neuropathic pain, and suppresses glial activation. AAV‑delivered shRNA achieves longer‑term silencing.

### 5. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Capsaicin**: Topical or intrathecal capsaicin defunctionalises TRPV1‑expressing nociceptors, depleting SP from central and peripheral terminals. This is a clinically used strategy for post‑herpetic neuralgia and other localized neuropathic pain conditions.
*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces SP expression in the DRG and spinal cord and normalizes NK1 receptor internalization, contributing to its analgesic effects.
*   **Resveratrol, curcumin, and paeonol**: Downregulate SP synthesis via inhibition of NF‑κB and MAPK pathways, with associated reductions in mechanical allodynia and neuroinflammation.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **The translational paradox**: Despite robust and reproducible preclinical efficacy across dozens of animal models, **highly selective NK1 receptor antagonists have consistently failed in large, placebo‑controlled Phase II/III clinical trials** for various pain conditions, including dental pain, osteoarthritis, diabetic neuropathy, and post‑herpetic neuralgia. Compounds such as aprepitant, casopitant, and orvepitant showed no significant analgesic effect.
*   **Reasons for failure**:
    *   **Species differences in NK1 pharmacology**: Rat and human NK1 receptors differ in their antagonist binding pocket; many early antagonists were optimized for rodent receptors and have sub‑optimal affinity for the human receptor. Even aprepitant, a potent human NK1 antagonist, may not achieve sufficient receptor occupancy in the human spinal cord.
    *   **Redundancy of tachykinin systems**: Neurokinin A (acting on NK2 receptors) and neurokinin B (acting on NK3 receptors) are co‑released with SP and can compensate for NK1 blockade. Broad‑spectrum tachykinin antagonists or combined NK1/NK2/NK3 blockers may be required.
    *   **Circuit complexity**: SP has both pronociceptive (spinal) and antinociceptive (striatal, PAG) functions. Global NK1 blockade may simultaneously relieve spinal hyperexcitability while impairing descending inhibition, yielding a null net effect.
    *   **Established central sensitization**: In clinical populations, neuropathic pain is typically chronic and well‑established, with extensive central plasticity that may be less dependent on ongoing SP release. Preclinical models often target the initiation or early maintenance phase.
*   **Aprepitant for pruritus**: The same NK1 antagonist that failed for pain has proven effective for chronic pruritus, leading to FDA approval. This demonstrates that NK1 blockade can successfully treat a neurogenic symptom in humans and has revived interest in exploring NK1 antagonists for pain subtypes where SP‑driven neuroinflammation is dominant (e.g., neuropathic itch, some forms of neuropathic pain).
*   **Biased NK1 agonists**: Research into biased ligands that preferentially activate G‑protein over β‑arrestin pathways (or vice versa) may allow separation of analgesic from pro‑inflammatory or emetic effects.
*   **Peripheral NK1 antagonism**: Non‑brain‑penetrant NK1 antagonists could block peripheral neurogenic inflammation and nociceptor sensitization while sparing central antinociceptive circuits and avoiding CNS side effects. This approach remains preclinical.
*   **Biomarker potential**: Elevated SP levels in cerebrospinal fluid, plasma, or synovial fluid have been correlated with pain severity in some neuropathic and inflammatory conditions. NK1 receptor internalization in skin punch biopsies can serve as a quantitative marker of nociceptor activity and treatment response.
*   **Combination therapy**: Because SP is co‑released and functionally synergistic with CGRP, dual blockade of NK1 and CGRP receptors may be more effective than either alone. Preclinical studies support this concept, and clinical trials of combined anti‑CGRP/anti‑NK1 therapies are under discussion.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Species‑dependent receptor pharmacology**: The translational failure of NK1 antagonists serves as a cautionary tale. Future development must utilize humanized animal models or directly test human receptor engagement in early clinical development.
*   **Redundancy within tachykinin family**: NK2 and NK3 receptors also contribute to pain processing (e.g., NK2 mediates smooth muscle contraction and visceral pain; NK3 modulates central dopamine and mood). Selective NK1 blockade may simply shift the tachykinin tone to other receptors.
*   **Central vs. peripheral SP**: In some pain states, such as acid‑induced muscle pain, SP can play a net antinociceptive role by activating unconventional NK1 receptor signalling pathways that inhibit nociceptors. Thus, indiscriminate blockade could paradoxically worsen certain pain components.
*   **Emotional and cognitive side effects**: NK1 receptors in the amygdala and prefrontal cortex are critical for fear extinction and mood regulation. Clinical trials of NK1 antagonists for depression showed inconsistent results, and chronic blockade could cause emotional blunting, apathy, or impaired fear extinction.
*   **Sex differences**: Preclinical data indicate that SP/NK1R signalling in the amygdala drives pain‑related anxiety more prominently in females, while males may engage different neuropeptide systems. This sex‑dependent contribution may influence the efficacy of NK1‑targeted therapies in a clinical setting.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**:
    *   **CGRP**: Co‑released with SP; the two neuropeptides synergize to amplify nociceptive transmission. SP enhances CGRP release from nociceptor terminals, and CGRP potentiates SP‑induced NK1 internalization. Dual inhibition is a compelling strategy.
    *   **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: SP‑activated spinal microglia release BDNF, which downregulates KCC2 and causes disinhibition. This directly links SP to the core GABAergic dysfunction in neuropathic pain.
    *   **TRPV1**: Capsaicin‑induced TRPV1 activation triggers SP release, and SP in turn sensitizes TRPV1 via PKC, forming a positive‑feedback loop. TRPV1 agonists (desensitization) and antagonists both reduce SP‑mediated pain.
    *   **NMDA receptor**: SP is a major driver of NMDAR‑dependent central sensitization through GluN2B phosphorylation. This positions SP upstream of NMDAR‑mediated calcium influx and LTP.
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, NF‑κB**: SP directly stimulates glial cytokine production; cytokines then upregulate SP synthesis, creating a feed‑forward inflammatory loop.
    *   **Wnt and Notch**: SP may activate or cross‑talk with Wnt and Notch pathways to sustain the proliferative and transcriptional programs that drive chronic pain.
    *   **Opioid system**: SP modulates the release of endogenous opioids (e.g., enkephalins) in the PAG. NK1 receptor blockade may enhance opioid analgesia, providing a rationale for combination therapy.
