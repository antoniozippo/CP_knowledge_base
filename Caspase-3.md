# Involvement of Caspase‑3 in Neuropathic Pain

Caspase‑3 (cysteine‑aspartic protease‑3) is the principal executioner caspase of the apoptotic cascade, responsible for the orchestrated dismantling of the cell during programmed cell death. In the adult nervous system, caspase‑3 also participates in a range of **non‑apoptotic functions** that are essential for synaptic plasticity, dendritic pruning, and the regulation of glial reactivity. In the context of neuropathic pain, caspase‑3 is aberrantly activated in specific populations of neurons and glial cells within the spinal dorsal horn, dorsal root ganglia (DRG), and supraspinal pain‑processing regions. Its most established pronociceptive mechanism is the **selective apoptosis of fast‑spiking, parvalbumin‑positive (PV⁺) GABAergic inhibitory interneurons** in the superficial spinal dorsal horn, which removes a critical source of synaptic inhibition and directly produces mechanical allodynia. Caspase‑3 is also activated in DRG nociceptors and in peripheral nerve cells after traumatic or metabolic injury, contributing to primary afferent hyperexcitability and neuroinflammation. Beyond cell death, caspase‑3 exerts non‑apoptotic effects—including the cleavage of protein kinase Cδ (PKCδ) to generate a constitutively active fragment that promotes microglial activation, and the cleavage of synaptic proteins (e.g., AMPA receptor subunits) that facilitates maladaptive plasticity. Pharmacological inhibition of caspase‑3 with selective peptide inhibitors (z‑DEVD‑fmk, Ac‑DEVD‑CHO, M‑826) or genetic deletion of the *Casp3* gene consistently reduces neuronal loss in the dorsal horn, preserves GABAergic inhibition, and attenuates mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in diverse preclinical models. However, the indispensable physiological roles of caspase‑3 in development, immune cell homeostasis, and cognitive function impose a narrow therapeutic window, and current efforts are directed towards cell‑type‑specific inhibition, intrathecal delivery, and targeting of upstream triggers such as mitochondrial dysfunction and calcium overload.

## 🧬 Caspase‑3: Gene, Structure, Activation, and Normal Function

- **Gene and protein**: Caspase‑3 is encoded by the *CASP3* gene located on human chromosome 4q35.1. It is synthesised as an inactive 32 kDa zymogen (**procaspase‑3**) that contains an N‑terminal pro‑domain, a large subunit (p17), and a small subunit (p12). The protein belongs to the CED‑3 subfamily of caspases and cleaves substrates after aspartate residues within the consensus motif DXXD.
- **Activation mechanisms**:
    - **Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway**: In response to cellular stress (DNA damage, oxidative stress, calcium overload, growth factor deprivation), the outer mitochondrial membrane is permeabilised, releasing cytochrome *c* into the cytosol. Cytochrome *c* binds to apoptotic protease‑activating factor‑1 (Apaf‑1) and dATP to form the **apoptosome**, which recruits and activates **caspase‑9**. Caspase‑9 then proteolytically cleaves procaspase‑3 at Asp¹⁷⁵, generating the active enzyme composed of two p17/p12 heterodimers. This is the dominant pathway in neuropathic pain, triggered by **calcium overload** and **mitochondrial dysfunction** in hyperactive GABAergic interneurons.
    - **Extrinsic (death receptor) pathway**: Binding of death ligands (TNF‑α, FasL, TRAIL) to their cognate receptors (TNFR1, Fas, DR4/DR5) recruits adaptor proteins (TRADD, FADD) and procaspase‑8, forming the death‑inducing signalling complex (DISC). Active caspase‑8 can directly cleave procaspase‑3 or amplify the signal through Bid cleavage and mitochondrial engagement.
    - **Non‑canonical activation**: Calpain, a calcium‑activated cysteine protease, can directly cleave procaspase‑3, bypassing caspase‑9. This is relevant in hyperactive GABAergic neurons where calpain is over‑activated.
- **Substrates and cellular effects**: Active caspase‑3 cleaves over 1,000 cellular proteins, including:
    - **Structural proteins**: Actin, fodrin, lamin A/C, resulting in membrane blebbing and nuclear fragmentation.
    - **DNA repair enzymes**: Poly(ADP‑ribose) polymerase‑1 (PARP‑1), leading to DNA degradation.
    - **Inhibitor of caspase‑activated DNase (ICAD)** , releasing CAD to fragment genomic DNA into nucleosomal units (DNA laddering).
    - **Anti‑apoptotic proteins**: Bcl‑2, Bcl‑xL (cleaved into pro‑apoptotic fragments), amplifying the death signal.
    - **Synaptic proteins**: AMPA receptor subunits (GluA1, GluA2/3), postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD‑95), and the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2 (indirectly via calpain; caspase‑3 can cleave KCC2 at a non‑canonical site in some contexts).
- **Non‑apoptotic roles in the nervous system**:
    - **Synaptic plasticity**: Transient, spatially restricted activation of caspase‑3 is required for hippocampal long‑term depression (LTD) and for the pruning of dendritic spines during learning.
    - **Axon guidance and neurite remodelling**: Caspase‑3 participates in the local degradation of cytoskeletal proteins at the growth cone.
    - **Microglial activation**: Caspase‑3 cleaves PKCδ to generate a catalytically active fragment that is essential for the full pro‑inflammatory response of microglia to ATP and other stimuli.
- **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    - **Spinal dorsal horn**: Procaspase‑3 is broadly expressed. After nerve injury, active caspase‑3 is predominantly detected in **PV⁺ GABAergic interneurons** in laminae I–III, and to a lesser extent in microglia and astrocytes.
    - **DRG neurons**: Caspase‑3 is present in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. Activation occurs after nerve transection, compression, or in diabetic neuropathy.
    - **Peripheral nerve**: Schwann cells and infiltrating macrophages show caspase‑3 activation during Wallerian degeneration.
    - **Supraspinal regions**: Caspase‑3 activation in the hippocampus and anterior cingulate cortex has been linked to pain‑related cognitive deficits and emotional dysregulation.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of Caspase‑3 in Neuropathic Pain

Caspase‑3 contributes to neuropathic pain through two distinct, but often interrelated, routes: the **apoptotic death** of inhibitory interneurons and primary sensory neurons, and **non‑apoptotic** signalling events that amplify neuroinflammation and maladaptive plasticity.

### 1. Selective Apoptosis of Spinal GABAergic Interneurons and Disinhibition

This is the most extensively characterised and functionally validated mechanism linking caspase‑3 to mechanical allodynia.

- **Selective vulnerability**: After peripheral nerve injury (chronic constriction injury, spinal nerve ligation, spared nerve injury), a significant number of **PV⁺ GABAergic interneurons** in the superficial spinal dorsal horn undergo apoptosis. This selective loss is driven by the combination of:
    - **Calcium‑permeable AMPA receptors (CP‑AMPARs)** : PV interneurons express high levels of GluA2‑lacking, Ca²⁺‑permeable AMPARs. Sustained glutamatergic drive from injured afferents causes massive Ca²⁺ influx.
    - **Reduced calcium buffering**: The calcium‑binding protein parvalbumin itself is downregulated or saturated under hyperactive conditions, impairing the neuron’s ability to buffer Ca²⁺.
    - **Mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload**: Ca²⁺ flooding into mitochondria exceeds the capacity of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) and triggers opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), leading to cytochrome *c* release.
- **Caspase‑3 activation**: Cytochrome *c* activates the apoptosome, leading to caspase‑9 and then caspase‑3 activation. Caspase‑3 executes the cell death programme, resulting in the loss of GABAergic synapses and a net reduction in inhibitory tone.
- **Functional consequence—disinhibition**: The loss of PV interneurons removes the feedforward inhibition that normally prevents Aβ‑fibre tactile input from activating nociceptive projection neurons. This disinhibition directly produces **mechanical allodynia**—the defining symptom of many neuropathic pain states.
- **Pharmacological and genetic validation**:
    - Intrathecal administration of the caspase‑3‑selective inhibitor **M‑826** or the pan‑caspase inhibitor **z‑DEVD‑fmk** prevents the loss of PV interneurons, preserves GABAergic inhibition, and attenuates mechanical allodynia in nerve‑injured animals.
    - Transgenic mice overexpressing the anti‑apoptotic protein **Bcl‑2** in neurons show reduced interneuron apoptosis and less severe allodynia after nerve injury.
    - Global **Casp3‑/‑ mice** exhibit a near‑complete protection against nerve injury‑induced mechanical allodynia, with sparing of dorsal horn GABAergic neurons.

### 2. Caspase‑3 Activation in DRG Neurons and Peripheral Nerve Damage

- **Primary afferent apoptosis**: After nerve transection (axotomy), a significant proportion of DRG neurons die via caspase‑3‑dependent apoptosis, particularly small‑diameter nociceptors. This loss contributes to sensory deficits but also triggers compensatory sprouting, neuroma formation, and aberrant spontaneous activity from surviving and regenerating neurites, which contributes to ongoing pain.
- **Chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy (CIPN)** : Agents such as paclitaxel, oxaliplatin, and bortezomib induce caspase‑3 activation in DRG neurons through mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and calcium dysregulation. Caspase‑3‑mediated neuronal apoptosis is a major contributor to the irreversible sensory neuropathy and the associated pain. Caspase‑3 inhibitors have been shown to prevent DRG neuron loss and attenuate pain in CIPN models.
- **Diabetic neuropathy**: Hyperglycaemia‑induced mitochondrial ROS and ER stress activate caspase‑3 in DRG neurons and Schwann cells, contributing to small‑fibre degeneration and pain. Caspase‑3 inhibition protects against these degenerative changes.

### 3. Non‑Apoptotic Roles: Microglial Activation and Neuroinflammation

Caspase‑3 can be activated in spinal microglia at levels that are insufficient to induce cell death, but sufficient to modulate the microglial phenotype via non‑apoptotic cleavage of specific substrates.

- **Cleavage of PKCδ**: Caspase‑3 cleaves PKCδ to generate a constitutively active fragment (δCF). This fragment translocates to the plasma membrane and is required for the full pro‑inflammatory response of microglia to ATP and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), including the production of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and reactive oxygen species. Genetic deletion of PKCδ or inhibition of its cleavage reduces microglial activation and neuropathic pain.
- **Cleavage of other substrates**: Caspase‑3 can cleave **protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A)** , removing its inhibitory effect on mitogen‑activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways and thereby enhancing NF‑κB signalling.
- **Microglial apoptosis and debris clearance**: While microglia themselves can undergo caspase‑3‑mediated apoptosis in response to severe injury, low‑level caspase‑3 activity may facilitate the phagocytic removal of apoptotic neuronal debris without killing the microglial cell, promoting resolution of inflammation.

### 4. Caspase‑3 and Maladaptive Synaptic Plasticity in Central Sensitisation

Non‑apoptotic caspase‑3 activity is required for certain forms of synaptic depression and spine pruning that, when dysregulated, can contribute to the maladaptive plasticity underlying chronic pain.

- **Long‑term depression (LTD)** : In the spinal dorsal horn, caspase‑3 is transiently activated during the induction of LTD at nociceptive synapses. This involves the caspase‑3‑mediated cleavage of **AMPA receptor subunit GluA1** at its C‑terminus, leading to internalisation of synaptic AMPA receptors. While this may be initially protective, dysregulated or sustained caspase‑3 activation could excessively depress inhibitory synapses, contributing to the net disinhibition of nociceptive circuits.
- **Cleavage of PSD‑95**: Caspase‑3 cleaves PSD‑95, a major scaffold protein at excitatory synapses, disrupting the organisation of the postsynaptic density and altering the balance of synaptic inputs onto spinal neurons.

### 5. Caspase‑3 as a Downstream Effector of Calcium Overload and Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Caspase‑3 sits at the convergence point of the mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis, making it a critical effector of the calcium overload–mitochondrial failure–oxidative stress cycle that is central to GABAergic interneuron pathology in neuropathic pain.

- The sequence—**excessive Ca²⁺ influx → mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload → mPTP opening → cytochrome *c* release → caspase‑9 activation → caspase‑3 activation → cell death**—is a core mechanism of inhibitory neuron loss.
- Because caspase‑3 also cleaves subunits of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (e.g., NDUFS1), it directly impairs mitochondrial respiration, creating a self‑amplifying loop of mitochondrial failure and caspase activation.
- Caspase‑3 can further cleave and inactivate the plasma membrane Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX), impairing calcium extrusion and exacerbating calcium overload in a positive‑feedback manner.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Pharmacological Caspase‑3 Inhibitors

- **z‑DEVD‑fmk** (benzyloxycarbonyl‑Asp‑Glu‑Val‑Asp‑fluoromethyl ketone): A cell‑permeable, irreversible, and relatively selective caspase‑3/7 inhibitor. Intrathecal delivery:
    - Prevents the loss of PV⁺ GABAergic interneurons in the spinal dorsal horn after CCI, SNL, and SNI.
    - Attenuates mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in a dose‑dependent manner.
    - Reduces spinal microglial activation (likely by blocking PKCδ cleavage) and normalises cytokine levels.
- **Ac‑DEVD‑CHO** (acetyl‑Asp‑Glu‑Val‑Asp‑aldehyde): A reversible, selective inhibitor of caspase‑3/7. Similar analgesic effects when administered intrathecally.
- **M‑826**: A highly specific, non‑peptide caspase‑3 inhibitor that shows excellent selectivity over other caspases and has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models of neuropathic pain and cerebral ischemia.
- **Pancaspase inhibitors** (e.g., **z‑VAD‑fmk**, **Q‑VD‑OPh**): Broader inhibition of caspases also reduces pain behaviours, but with a greater risk of off‑target effects due to blockade of caspase‑1 (inflammasome) and other caspases.
- **Timing**: Caspase‑3 inhibitors are most effective when administered in the **early phase** after nerve injury (first few days), as this is the window during which interneuron apoptosis occurs. Late administration, after significant cell loss has taken place, has reduced efficacy.

### 2. Genetic Models

- **Casp3‑/‑ mice** (caspase‑3 knockout): These mice are viable but show defects in brain development (exencephaly in some backgrounds) and reduced neuronal apoptosis. In models of peripheral nerve injury, Casp3‑/‑ mice exhibit:
    - Profound protection against mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
    - Preservation of PV⁺ GABAergic neuron numbers in the spinal dorsal horn.
    - Reduced microglial activation and cytokine expression.
    - However, the absence of caspase‑3 also impairs the programmed cell death that is necessary for normal development and tissue homeostasis, leading to the accumulation of abnormal cells and potential immune dysregulation.
- **Conditional caspase‑3 knockout**: Using Cre‑loxP systems to delete caspase‑3 specifically in **GABAergic interneurons** (e.g., via Dlx5/6‑Cre or PV‑Cre) or in **Nav1.8‑positive nociceptors** has confirmed the cell‑type‑specific contributions to neuropathic pain. Neuronal‑specific deletion recapitulates the protective phenotype without the developmental defects of global knockout.
- **Overexpression of anti‑apoptotic proteins**: Viral vector‑mediated overexpression of **Bcl‑2** or **Bcl‑xL** in spinal dorsal horn neurons prevents nerve injury‑induced apoptosis of GABAergic interneurons, preserves inhibition, and reduces mechanical allodynia.

### 3. Indirect Inhibition via Upstream Targets

Because caspase‑3 is activated downstream of mitochondrial cytochrome *c* release, interventions that preserve mitochondrial integrity can indirectly suppress caspase‑3 activation:
- **mPTP inhibitors** (cyclosporin A, NIM811): Reduce cytochrome *c* release and caspase‑3 activation.
- **Calpain inhibitors** (MDL‑28170): Prevent the non‑canonical cleavage of procaspase‑3 by calpain, particularly in neurons undergoing calcium overload.
- **Antioxidants targeted to mitochondria** (MitoQ, MitoTEMPO): Reduce mitochondrial ROS and prevent mPTP opening.
- **KCC2 enhancers** and **inhibitors of BDNF‑TrkB signalling**: By reducing neuronal hyperexcitability and the driving force for calcium overload, these agents indirectly decrease caspase‑3 activation.

### 4. Natural Compounds

- **Resveratrol**: Activates SIRT1, which deacetylates and inactivates p53, reducing caspase‑3‑dependent apoptosis in DRG neurons and spinal cord.
- **Curcumin**: Inhibits caspase‑3 activation by reducing upstream mitochondrial dysfunction and by blocking NF‑κB‑mediated induction of pro‑apoptotic factors.
- **Ginsenoside Rb1**: Attenuates caspase‑3 activation and reduces neuropathic pain in diabetic neuropathy models.
- **Epigallocatechin‑3‑gallate (EGCG)** : Reduces caspase‑3 cleavage and protects GABAergic neurons in the spinal cord.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

- **Caspase‑3 inhibitors as neuroprotective analgesics**: The concept of using a caspase‑3 inhibitor to prevent the loss of inhibitory interneurons and thus preserve endogenous pain‑control mechanisms represents a **disease‑modifying approach**, distinct from simply suppressing symptoms. Preventing the structural disinhibition of spinal nociceptive circuits could halt the progression from acute to chronic pain.
- **Intrathecal delivery**: Because caspase‑3 is ubiquitously expressed and essential for systemic homeostasis, intrathecal delivery of caspase‑3 inhibitors via an implanted pump or sustained‑release formulation is the most clinically viable route. This limits exposure to the spinal cord and DRG, minimising systemic immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, and other side effects.
- **Biomarker‑guided therapy**: Cleaved caspase‑3 fragments and cytochrome *c* are detectable in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and could serve as biomarkers to identify patients with ongoing spinal neuronal apoptosis who would most benefit from caspase‑3 inhibition.
- **Repurposing of existing caspase inhibitors**: While no selective caspase‑3 inhibitor is yet approved for any indication, several pan‑caspase inhibitors (e.g., emricasan, IDN‑6556) have been in clinical trials for liver diseases and other conditions. Their safety profile in humans, including when delivered locally, could accelerate clinical testing for neuropathic pain.
- **Combination strategies**:
    - **Caspase‑3 inhibitors + KCC2 enhancers**: Caspase‑3 inhibition prevents the death of GABAergic neurons, while KCC2 enhancers restore the function of surviving inhibitory synapses, providing a synergistic approach to restoring inhibition.
    - **Caspase‑3 inhibitors + anti‑inflammatory agents**: Combining a caspase‑3 inhibitor with a TNF‑α or IL‑1β blocker could suppress both the apoptotic and the neuroinflammatory arms of the pain cascade.
    - **Caspase‑3 inhibitors + growth factors (BDNF, NGF)** : In the peripheral nerve, caspase‑3 inhibitors could protect DRG neurons while growth factors promote regeneration.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

- **Physiological essentiality and developmental role**: Caspase‑3 is indispensable for embryonic development, and its complete genetic deletion in mice can cause perinatal lethality or severe brain malformations. Even in adulthood, caspase‑3 is required for immune cell turnover, wound healing, and the physiological apoptosis that clears senescent or damaged cells. Chronic, systemic caspase‑3 inhibition carries a theoretical risk of autoimmunity, impaired tissue repair, and tumorigenesis (failure to eliminate pre‑cancerous cells). Intrathecal, time‑limited, or cell‑specific delivery is essential to mitigate these risks.
- **Non‑apoptotic functions in learning and memory**: Caspase‑3 plays a critical role in hippocampal LTD and in the structural plasticity of dendritic spines that underlies learning and memory. Global caspase‑3 inhibition could cause cognitive deficits, a particularly concerning side effect in patients already suffering from pain‑related cognitive impairment.
- **Dual role in pain resolution**: Apoptosis is not solely destructive; it is also required for the clearance of hyperactive, damaged neurons that could otherwise continue to fire aberrantly and drive pain. Indiscriminate inhibition of caspase‑3 might preserve dysfunctional, hyperexcitable neurons that would otherwise be eliminated, potentially worsening pain. The goal should be to **normalise** excessive caspase‑3 activity, not to abolish it entirely.
- **Redundancy with other cell death pathways**: Caspase‑3 is not the only executioner of cell death; caspase‑7 (which shares many substrates), calpain‑mediated necrosis, necroptosis (RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL), and pyroptosis (caspase‑1/GSDMD) can all contribute to neuronal loss. Blocking caspase‑3 alone may shift the mode of cell death to one of these alternative pathways, limiting the durability of caspase‑3‑targeted monotherapy.
- **Sex differences**: Preclinical studies have indicated that the extent of caspase‑3‑mediated interneuron apoptosis in the spinal dorsal horn after nerve injury is greater in male rodents than in females. Females may rely more on functional silencing of interneurons (e.g., via KCC2 downregulation) rather than cell death. Caspase‑3 inhibitors may therefore be more effective in males, but this requires careful investigation in human trials.
- **Interactions with the wider pain network**:
    - **Calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction**: The primary upstream trigger of caspase‑3 in GABAergic interneurons is the excessive Ca²⁺ influx and subsequent mitochondrial failure, linking caspase‑3 to the broader pathways of excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and impaired autophagy.
    - **Calpain**: Calpain and caspase‑3 often co‑activate in dying neurons; they share several substrates, including KCC2, and their combined inhibition may be required for optimal neuroprotection.
    - **BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2**: BDNF‑mediated KCC2 downregulation increases neuronal excitability, driving the calcium overload that activates caspase‑3. Caspase‑3 can also cleave KCC2, contributing to its downregulation and the resulting disinhibition.
    - **TNF‑α**: TNF‑α can trigger both the extrinsic caspase‑8/caspase‑3 pathway and the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis, linking neuroinflammation to inhibitory neuron death.
    - **Parvalbumin interneurons and perineuronal nets (PNNs)** : The loss of PNNs after injury increases the vulnerability of PV interneurons to excitotoxic death, and caspase‑3 is the executioner of that death.
    - **RhoA/ROCK**: RhoA/ROCK signalling can promote caspase‑3 activation through the regulation of cytoskeletal dynamics and the Bcl‑2 family of proteins.
    - **NLRP3 inflammasome**: While caspase‑3 does not directly process IL‑1β, it can cleave and activate caspase‑1 in some contexts, and the overall disruption of cellular integrity by caspase‑3 releases DAMPs (ATP, HMGB1) that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome on neighbouring cells.
