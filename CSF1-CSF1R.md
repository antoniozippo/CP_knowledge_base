# Involvement of CSF1/CSF1R in Neuropathic Pain

Colony‑stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) and its receptor CSF1R are a pivotal neuron–immune signaling axis that drives the expansion and activation of microglia and macrophages after peripheral nerve injury. De novo expression of CSF1 in injured dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons—and in some contexts, astrocytes—triggers the proliferation and neuroinflammatory polarization of CSF1R‑expressing microglia in the spinal cord and macrophages in the DRG and peripheral nerve. This glial response is causally linked to the development of mechanical allodynia and other pain behaviours. Pharmacological inhibition of CSF1R, genetic deletion of the ligand or receptor, or sequestration of CSF1 with a soluble decoy receptor all markedly reduce neuropathic pain in preclinical models, positioning the CSF1/CSF1R pathway as an attractive upstream target for therapeutic intervention.

### 🧬 CSF1/CSF1R: The Ligand‑Receptor System

CSF1 (also known as macrophage colony‑stimulating factor, M‑CSF) is a haematopoietic growth factor that regulates the survival, proliferation, and differentiation of mononuclear phagocytes, including microglia and macrophages. Two endogenous ligands signal through CSF1R: CSF1 itself and the structurally distinct interleukin‑34 (IL‑34). Both ligands bind to overlapping but non‑identical regions of the receptor, activating similar downstream signalling cascades.

CSF1R is a type III receptor tyrosine kinase encoded by the *Csf1r* proto‑oncogene. In the nervous system, it is predominantly expressed by:
- **Microglia** in the spinal cord and brain
- **Macrophages** in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), peripheral nerve, and meninges
- **A small subset of neurons** in certain brain regions

Under physiological conditions, CSF1R signalling is essential for the development and maintenance of microglia. In the context of nerve injury, however, this pathway becomes pathological, driving a sustained expansion of glial populations that fuel central and peripheral sensitization[reference:0].

### 🔬 Mechanisms of CSF1/CSF1R in Neuropathic Pain

#### 1. Neuronal CSF1 is the Primary Injury Signal

Under normal conditions, sensory neurons express little or no CSF1. Following peripheral nerve injury (traumatic, inflammatory, or metabolic), CSF1 is induced *de novo* in injured DRG neurons and anterogradely transported to their central terminals in the spinal cord dorsal horn[reference:1][reference:2].

This injury‑specific upregulation means that CSF1 acts as a neuron‑derived "danger signal" that directly couples nociceptor damage to central immune activation. Importantly, CSF1 is also upregulated in injured motoneurons, where it drives ventral horn microgliosis, indicating that the CSF1/CSF1R axis contributes to both sensory and motor responses after nerve trauma[reference:3].

#### 2. Microglial Activation and Proliferation via CSF1R

CSF1 released from nociceptor central terminals engages CSF1R on spinal microglia, triggering their proliferation (microgliosis) and activation. This expansion is robust, with a several‑fold increase in microglial numbers in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn within days of injury[reference:4][reference:5].

The activated microglia adopt a pro‑inflammatory phenotype characterised by:
- Upregulation of Iba‑1 and CD68
- Enhanced phagocytic activity
- Release of pronociceptive mediators, including brain‑derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), tumour necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), and reactive oxygen species (ROS)[reference:6][reference:7]

#### 3. Downstream Signalling: The DAP12 and MAPK Pathways

Downstream of CSF1R, at least two distinct intracellular cascades mediate the pain phenotype:

- **DAP12‑dependent signalling**: The microglial transmembrane adaptor protein DAP12 (TYROBP) is required for CSF1‑induced upregulation of pain‑related microglial genes and the development of mechanical hypersensitivity. However, DAP12 is *not* required for microglial proliferation. This indicates that proliferation and neuroinflammatory gene expression are separable, parallel pathways downstream of CSF1R[reference:8].
- **p38 MAPK cascade**: CSF1R activation stimulates p38 mitogen‑activated protein kinase (MAPK), which in turn drives the synthesis and release of BDNF from microglia. Microglial‑derived BDNF then acts on TrkB receptors on spinal lamina I neurons to downregulate KCC2, collapsing the chloride gradient and producing disinhibition of nociceptive circuits—the core mechanism of tactile allodynia[reference:9].
- **ERK and NF‑κB pathways**: In the DRG, CSF1R signalling through extracellular signal‑regulated kinase (ERK) and nuclear factor‑κB (NF‑κB) promotes macrophage infiltration, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and pro‑inflammatory cytokine release[reference:10].

#### 4. Astrocytic CSF1 as an Alternative Source

Although neuronal CSF1 is the primary driver of microgliosis after traumatic nerve injury, astrocytes represent an additional source in certain pain conditions. In the chronic post‑ischemic pain (CPIP) model, astrocytic CSF1 expression is markedly increased and, together with neuronal CSF1, activates microglial CSF1R. Blockade of astrocytic CSF1 with fluorocitrate attenuates microgliosis and pain, demonstrating that the cell of origin of CSF1 may vary with the pain aetiology[reference:11].

#### 5. The IL‑34/CSF1R Axis in the DRG

In addition to CSF1, **interleukin‑34 (IL‑34)** is a second, functionally distinct ligand for CSF1R. After peripheral nerve injury, Schwann cells in the DRG secrete IL‑34, which engages CSF1R on resident and infiltrating macrophages. This Schwann cell–macrophage crosstalk promotes macrophage proliferation, migration, and pro‑inflammatory activation, contributing to DRG neuroinflammation and peripheral sensitization[reference:12][reference:13].

#### 6. Macrophage Accumulation in DRG and Peripheral Nerve

CSF1/CSF1R signalling is not limited to the spinal cord. In the DRG and injured peripheral nerve, CSF1R‑expressing macrophages accumulate in a CSF1‑dependent manner. These macrophages release pronociceptive mediators that sensitize nociceptor cell bodies and axons, contributing to both spontaneous pain and evoked hypersensitivity. Macrophage infiltration correlates with the degree of nerve damage and pain severity[reference:14][reference:15].

#### 7. Membrane Trafficking and Signal Amplification

Recent work has identified novel upstream regulators that modulate CSF1R availability at the plasma membrane. **Sphingomyelin synthase 1 (SMS1)** is upregulated in spinal microglia after nerve injury. SMS1 promotes Golgi‑associated diacylglycerol (DAG) production, leading to protein kinase D (PKD) activation. Activated PKD enhances vesicular transport of CSF1R from the Golgi to the plasma membrane, increasing surface CSF1R density and amplifying signalling intensity. This mechanism links sphingolipid metabolism to microglial neuroimmune function and identifies SMS1 as a potential target upstream of CSF1R itself[reference:16].

### 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

A large and consistent body of evidence from pharmacological, genetic, and viral‑vector approaches demonstrates that interrupting the CSF1/CSF1R axis reduces neuropathic pain in multiple animal models.

#### Pharmacological CSF1R Inhibitors

- **PLX5622**: A highly selective, brain‑penetrant CSF1R inhibitor (IC₅₀ = 0.016 µM) that is the most widely used tool compound. In the partial sciatic nerve ligation model, PLX5622 administered at 65 mg/kg reduced spinal Iba‑1⁺ microglia, decreased macrophage infiltration into the sciatic nerve, lowered TNF‑α and IL‑1β levels, and alleviated both mechanical and cold allodynia[reference:17]. Sustained CSF1R inhibition with PLX5622 from two days pre‑surgery to day 14 post‑surgery significantly reduced neuropathic pain behaviours[reference:18].
- **PLX3397 (pexidartinib)** : Another CSF1R inhibitor that suppresses microglial activation and proliferation. In the CPIP model, PLX3397 inhibited CSF1/CSF1R signalling, reduced BDNF upregulation and neuronal hyperactivity in the dorsal horn, and attenuated mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia[reference:19]. Notably, pexidartinib exhibits sexually dimorphic effects on nerve injury‑induced neuropathic pain in mice, with potentially different efficacy in males versus females[reference:20].
- **GW2580**: A competitive CSF1R inhibitor that, when administered orally, blocked DRG macrophage and spinal microglia activation, inhibited pro‑inflammatory cytokine release from injured intervertebral discs, and eliminated neuropathic pain secondary to disc injury. GW2580 also decreased disc degeneration as assessed by MRI and histopathology, suggesting a disease‑modifying effect[reference:21].

#### Genetic Approaches

- **Sensory neuron‑specific *Csf1* deletion**: Cre‑mediated deletion of *Csf1* in sensory neurons completely prevented nerve injury‑induced mechanical hypersensitivity and reduced microglial activation and proliferation. This genetic experiment definitively established that neuronal CSF1 is necessary for pain development[reference:22].
- **CSF1R conditional knockout**: Specific deletion of CSF1R in CX3CR1⁺ cells (microglia and macrophages) using CX3CR1CreER:CSF1Rᶠˡ/ᶠˡ mice eliminated neuropathic pain secondary to disc injury and reduced intervertebral disc degeneration[reference:23][reference:24].
- **CSF1R overexpression and bioinformatics**: Intrathecal CSF1 induces mechanical hypersensitivity and microglial proliferation in wild‑type animals, confirming that CSF1/CSF1R activation is sufficient to trigger pain[reference:25]. Moreover, bioinformatics analyses of large‑scale gene expression datasets have identified *CSF1R* as an essential gene mediating neuropathic pain, further validating its central role[reference:26].

#### Soluble Decoy Receptors and RNA Silencing

- **sCSF1R (soluble CSF1R)** : Over‑expression of a non‑functional, soluble CSF1R using an adeno‑associated virus 9 (AAV9) vector acts as a decoy that sequesters CSF1. Intrathecal AAV9/sCSF1R administration prior to partial sciatic nerve ligation significantly reduced microglial and macrophage accumulation in the spinal cord, DRG, and sciatic nerve, and alleviated mechanical allodynia. This approach has the advantage of specifically targeting the extracellular ligand without directly inhibiting CSF1R signalling essential for microglial survival[reference:27].
- **CSF1R siRNA**: Small interfering RNA‑mediated knockdown of CSF1R in the spinal cord has been reported to reduce pain behaviours, although data are more limited than for pharmacological inhibitors.

#### Targeting Upstream Regulators and the IL‑34/CSF1R Axis

- **Paeonol**: A natural phenolic compound that alleviates CCI‑induced neuropathic pain by inhibiting the IL‑34–CSF1R interaction, suppressing Schwann cell–macrophage crosstalk, and reducing DRG neuroinflammation. Paeonol also decreased serum inflammatory mediators and NLRP3 inflammasome components[reference:28].
- **Stigmasterol**: A plant sterol that modulates the IL‑34/CSF1R axis, reducing the Schwann cell–macrophage cascade in the DRG and alleviating neuropathic pain[reference:29].
- **SMS1 as a target**: As described above, SMS1 acts as a metabolic gatekeeper that amplifies CSF1R signalling by controlling receptor trafficking to the plasma membrane. Targeting SMS1 offers a novel strategy to indirectly reduce CSF1R signalling without direct receptor blockade[reference:30].

### 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

- **Drug repurposing and development**: Pexidartinib (PLX3397) is already FDA‑approved for tenosynovial giant cell tumours, providing pharmacokinetic and safety data that could accelerate repurposing for pain indications. The sexually dimorphic effects observed preclinically suggest that sex‑stratified trial designs may be necessary[reference:31].
- **Soluble CSF1R as a gene therapy**: AAV‑mediated delivery of sCSF1R represents a one‑time, localised (intrathecal) approach that avoids the systemic side effects of CSF1R inhibition. Preclinical results are promising, though long‑term safety and efficacy remain to be established[reference:32].
- **Targeting the IL‑34/CSF1R interaction in the DRG**: Inhibiting the Schwann cell–macrophage crosstalk mediated by IL‑34 and CSF1R could specifically address the peripheral component of neuropathic pain, potentially reducing the need for centrally acting drugs[reference:33][reference:34].
- **Upstream regulators (SMS1, DAP12)** : Modulating CSF1R trafficking or downstream signalling components such as DAP12 may allow finer control of the pro‑nociceptive microglial phenotype while preserving microglial survival and homeostatic functions[reference:35][reference:36].
- **Biomarker potential**: CSF1, IL‑34, or soluble CSF1R fragments in cerebrospinal fluid or serum may reflect the degree of neuroimmune activation and could serve as biomarkers for patient stratification or treatment response monitoring.
- **Human genetics**: Mendelian randomisation and genome‑wide association studies have linked variations in the *CSF1R* locus to pain phenotypes, providing human genetic evidence that reinforces the preclinical data[reference:37].

### ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

- **Physiological roles of CSF1R**: CSF1R signalling is essential for microglial development, survival, and normal turnover. Chronic, complete CSF1R inhibition depletes microglia throughout the CNS, which impairs synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. In the periphery, CSF1R is critical for macrophage homeostasis and bone remodelling. The therapeutic window for CSF1R inhibition therefore needs to be defined carefully—partial, intermittent, or localised inhibition may be required.
- **Sex differences**: Pexidartinib exhibits sexually dimorphic effects on neuropathic pain in mice, with potentially different efficacy and side‑effect profiles in males and females. The mechanisms underlying this dimorphism are not fully understood but may involve sex‑dependent differences in microglial and macrophage biology[reference:38].
- **Dual role of microglia and macrophages**: While activated microglia and macrophages are pronociceptive, they also perform critical repair functions, including debris clearance, remyelination support, and tissue remodelling. Complete ablation of these cells may impair long‑term recovery after nerve injury.
- **Disease‑specific contributions**: The relative importance of neuronal versus astrocytic CSF1, and of spinal microglial versus DRG macrophage activation, may vary with the type of injury (traumatic, ischaemic, metabolic, inflammatory). Personalised or aetiology‑specific targeting may be required.
- **Redundancy between CSF1 and IL‑34**: Both ligands activate CSF1R, and their relative contributions in different pain conditions and anatomical compartments are not fully characterised. Blocking CSF1 alone may be insufficient if IL‑34 continues to drive CSF1R signalling.
- **Interactions with other pain pathways**: The CSF1/CSF1R axis is upstream of many of the pronociceptive systems previously discussed:
    - **BDNF/TrkB**: CSF1‑stimulated microglia release BDNF, which downregulates KCC2 and produces disinhibition.
    - **P2X4 receptor**: ATP‑induced P2X4 activation and CSF1‑induced CSF1R activation converge on the same microglial BDNF release pathway, representing parallel but independent mechanisms of microglial‑mediated central sensitization.
    - **TNF‑α and IL‑1β**: CSF1R signalling drives the expression of these key pro‑inflammatory cytokines, linking the pathway to broader neuroimmune networks.
    - **NLRP3 inflammasome**: CSF1R‑mediated ERK and NF‑κB activation promotes NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL‑1β maturation in the DRG.
    - **RhoA/ROCK**: CSF1R can activate RhoA in microglia, contributing to cytoskeletal changes that facilitate migration and phagocytosis.
    - **HIF1α/VEGFA**: Hypoxia‑driven HIF1α may upregulate CSF1 expression, linking metabolic stress to CSF1/CSF1R‑mediated neuroinflammation.
