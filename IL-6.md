# Involvement of Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) in Neuropathic Pain

Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a 21–28 kDa pleiotropic cytokine that functions as a master orchestrator of inflammation, immune regulation, and haematopoiesis. In the nervous system, IL‑6 has emerged as a pivotal pronociceptive mediator, linking peripheral nerve injury to spinal neuroinflammation, central sensitisation, and the development of persistent neuropathic pain. Unlike classical pro‑inflammatory cytokines that act exclusively through membrane‑bound receptors, IL‑6 uniquely employs two distinct signalling modes: **classical signalling**, which is largely homeostatic and restricted to cells expressing the membrane‑bound IL‑6 receptor (IL‑6R), and **trans‑signalling**, a pro‑inflammatory, pathological pathway in which IL‑6 binds to a soluble form of the IL‑6R (sIL‑6R) to activate cells that lack the cognate receptor but express the ubiquitous signal‑transducing co‑receptor gp130. In neuropathic pain, **IL‑6 trans‑signalling** is the dominant pathogenic mechanism, enabling IL‑6 to directly excite nociceptors, drive the reactive polarisation of microglia and astrocytes, amplify JAK/STAT3‑dependent transcriptional programs, and downregulate the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2, collapsing GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition. Clinical validation of the IL‑6 pathway in pain has come from the remarkable success of anti‑IL‑6 and anti‑IL‑6R biologics (tocilizumab, sarilumab) in rheumatoid arthritis, where profound reductions in pain—often exceeding the anti‑inflammatory effect—are observed. These findings, together with robust preclinical efficacy in diverse neuropathic pain models, position the IL‑6 signalling axis, particularly its trans‑signalling arm, as a highly attractive target for non‑opioid analgesic development.

## 🧬 The IL‑6 System: Gene, Ligand, Receptors, and Signalling Modes

*   **Gene and protein**: IL‑6 is encoded by the *IL6* gene on human chromosome 7p15.3. The mature protein is a four‑helix bundle cytokine produced by virtually all nucleated cells under appropriate stimulation, including immune cells (monocytes/macrophages, microglia, T‑cells, B‑cells), fibroblasts, endothelial cells, astrocytes, and, importantly, dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons and Schwann cells.
*   **Receptors and signalling complexes**:
    *   **IL‑6 receptor (IL‑6R, CD126)** : An 80 kDa ligand‑binding α‑chain. IL‑6R is expressed in a limited set of tissues, primarily hepatocytes, some leukocyte subsets, and megakaryocytes. In the nervous system, membrane‑bound IL‑6R expression is low or absent on most neurons and glial cells under physiological conditions.
    *   **Soluble IL‑6 receptor (sIL‑6R)** : Generated by proteolytic shedding of the membrane‑bound receptor (primarily by ADAM17) or by alternative mRNA splicing. sIL‑6R circulates in plasma and interstitial fluid and, upon binding IL‑6, can activate any gp130‑expressing cell, dramatically expanding the cellular targets of IL‑6.
    *   **gp130 (CD130)** : The 130 kDa signal‑transducing β‑receptor. gp130 is ubiquitously expressed on virtually all cell types, including neurons, microglia, and astrocytes. gp130 homodimers or heterodimers with other cytokine‑specific α‑chains serve as the signalling platform for all IL‑6 family cytokines (IL‑6, IL‑11, IL‑27, LIF, oncostatin M, ciliary neurotrophic factor, cardiotrophin‑1).
*   **Classical vs. trans‑signalling**:
    *   **Classical signalling**: IL‑6 binds to membrane‑bound IL‑6R, and this complex engages gp130 to initiate intracellular signalling. This mode is homeostatic and mediates the regenerative, anti‑inflammatory, and metabolic effects of IL‑6 (e.g., hepatic acute‑phase response, lymphocyte trafficking, metabolic regulation).
    *   **Trans‑signalling**: IL‑6 binds to sIL‑6R, and the IL‑6/sIL‑6R complex—termed **hyper‑IL‑6** when the cytokine and soluble receptor are covalently linked—activates gp130 on cells that do not express membrane IL‑6R. This is the **pro‑inflammatory, pathogenic mode** of IL‑6 signalling and is responsible for driving neuropathic pain, as it enables IL‑6 to act directly on nociceptors, spinal microglia, and astrocytes.
    *   **Soluble gp130 (sgp130)** : A naturally occurring, truncated form of gp130 that selectively neutralises the IL‑6/sIL‑6R trans‑signalling complex without affecting classical signalling. **Sgp130Fc (olamkicept)** is a recombinant fusion protein that acts as a selective trans‑signalling inhibitor and is in clinical development.
*   **Downstream intracellular signalling**:
    *   **JAK/STAT3 pathway**: The canonical pathway. gp130 activation recruits and activates **Janus kinase 1 (JAK1)** and **JAK2**, which phosphorylate tyrosine residues on the gp130 cytoplasmic tail, creating docking sites for **STAT3**. STAT3 is phosphorylated at Tyr⁷⁰⁵, dimerises, and translocates to the nucleus to drive the transcription of target genes, including *IL6* itself (autocrine loop), *SOCS3* (negative feedback), and numerous pronociceptive effectors (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, CCL2, CX3CL1, COX‑2, iNOS, BDNF, VEGF, MMP‑9).
    *   **MAPK (ERK, p38, JNK) pathway**: gp130 also activates the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK cascade, contributing to neuronal sensitisation and glial activation.
    *   **PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway**: Mediates cell survival, metabolic reprogramming, and protein synthesis.
    *   **NF‑κB pathway**: IL‑6 can activate NF‑κB indirectly via TAK1 and IKK, or directly via STAT3‑mediated transcription of NF‑κB components, forming a feed‑forward inflammatory loop.
*   **Cellular sources of IL‑6 in neuropathic pain**:
    *   **Peripheral nerve**: Schwann cells, endothelial cells, and infiltrating macrophages and neutrophils are major sources of IL‑6 at the injury site.
    *   **DRG**: IL‑6 is upregulated in DRG neurons (particularly small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors) and satellite glial cells after nerve injury.
    *   **Spinal cord**: Spinal microglia and astrocytes are the primary sources of IL‑6 following peripheral nerve injury. Neurons can also produce IL‑6 under conditions of high excitability.
    *   **Supraspinal**: IL‑6 is produced in the anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala during chronic pain, contributing to affective and cognitive comorbidities.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of IL‑6 in Neuropathic Pain

IL‑6 signalling, predominantly via the **trans‑signalling pathway**, contributes to neuropathic pain through a multi‑faceted mechanism encompassing direct neuronal sensitisation, potentiation of spinal glial activation, disruption of GABAergic inhibition, and modulation of supraspinal pain networks.

### 1. Direct Excitation and Sensitisation of Nociceptors

*   **Trans‑signalling enables direct neuronal action**: Primary sensory neurons in the DRG express **gp130** abundantly but lack, or express very low levels of, membrane‑bound **IL‑6R**. IL‑6 alone cannot activate these neurons. However, in the presence of **sIL‑6R**—which is elevated after nerve injury, released from immune cells and microglia—IL‑6/sIL‑6R complexes powerfully excite nociceptors. This trans‑signalling mechanism was definitively demonstrated by the finding that IL‑6, together with sIL‑6R, induced a dose‑dependent, transient increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ in a subpopulation of DRG neurons, as well as a long‑lasting increase in action potential firing, whereas IL‑6 or sIL‑6R alone had no effect. The selective trans‑signalling inhibitor **sgp130Fc** abolished this effect.
*   **Sensitisation of ion channels**: IL‑6 trans‑signalling enhances the activity of multiple pronociceptive ion channels:
    *   **TRPV1**: IL‑6 sensitises TRPV1 to capsaicin and heat, mediated through ERK‑ and p38‑dependent phosphorylation.
    *   **Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9**: In bone cancer pain models and some neuropathic pain contexts, IL‑6/JAK/STAT3 signalling upregulates the transcription of these voltage‑gated sodium channels, increasing sodium current density and reducing the action potential threshold. The GM‑CSF‑JAK2/STAT3‑Nav axis described earlier partially overlaps with IL‑6 signalling, as IL‑6 is also a potent activator of JAK/STAT3.
    *   **TRPA1**: IL‑6 can sensitise TRPA1, contributing to cold and mechanical hypersensitivity.
*   **CGRP release**: IL‑6 trans‑signalling promotes calcium‑dependent exocytosis of calcitonin gene‑related peptide (CGRP) from nociceptor terminals, driving neurogenic inflammation and central sensitisation.
*   **Phenotypic switch of Aβ‑fibres**: IL‑6 contributes to the pathological phenotypic switch in which large‑diameter, low‑threshold mechanoreceptors begin to express pronociceptive neuropeptides (substance P, CGRP) and cytokines, directly promoting mechanical allodynia.

### 2. Spinal Glial Activation and Central Sensitisation

*   **Microglial activation**: IL‑6 trans‑signalling, via gp130, is a potent driver of microglial M1 polarisation, characterised by the release of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, reactive oxygen species, and BDNF. In the spinal dorsal horn, microglial IL‑6 amplifies the neuroinflammatory response in an autocrine and paracrine manner.
*   **Astrocytic IL‑6 and chronic pain maintenance**: IL‑6 is a key factor in astrocyte‑mediated pain chronification. In the spared nerve injury (SNI) model, **spinal astrocytes are the primary source of IL‑6** in the chronic phase, and astrocytic IL‑6 is required for the maintenance of mechanical allodynia, not just its initiation. IL‑6 acts on astrocytes to promote reactive astrogliosis, upregulate GFAP, downregulate glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), and sustain the release of pronociceptive mediators.
*   **JAK2/STAT3 pathway in glial cells**: As detailed in the dedicated JAK/STAT3 file, the IL‑6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway is the central intracellular cascade that transduces IL‑6 signals in microglia and astrocytes. STAT3 drives the expression of hundreds of pro‑inflammatory genes that sustain central sensitisation.
*   **NMDA receptor potentiation**: IL‑6‑activated JAK/STAT and MAPK pathways converge on the phosphorylation of the GluN2B subunit of NMDA receptors, increasing their calcium permeability and promoting long‑term potentiation at nociceptive synapses.

### 3. Disruption of GABAergic Inhibition via KCC2 Downregulation

A critical mechanism by which IL‑6 promotes central disinhibition is through the **downregulation of the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2** in spinal dorsal horn neurons.

*   **Microglial BDNF as intermediary**: IL‑6‑activated microglia release large amounts of BDNF. BDNF binds to TrkB receptors on spinal lamina I neurons, triggering a signalling cascade that results in the internalisation and proteasomal degradation of KCC2, as well as calpain‑mediated cleavage.
*   **Direct effect on KCC2 transcription**: IL‑6, via STAT3, can directly suppress the transcription of the *Slc12a5* (KCC2) gene in spinal neurons, further reducing chloride extrusion capacity.
*   **NKCC1 upregulation**: Concurrently, IL‑6 can upregulate the chloride‑importing cotransporter **NKCC1**, shifting the NKCC1/KCC2 ratio in favour of high intracellular chloride. The net effect is a collapse of the chloride gradient, converting GABA and glycine from hyperpolarising (inhibitory) to depolarising (excitatory or less inhibitory) neurotransmitters, which is the electrophysiological substrate of mechanical allodynia.

### 4. Contribution to Opioid‑Induced Hyperalgesia and Tolerance

*   Chronic opioid administration can activate microglia and astrocytes, leading to the release of IL‑6. IL‑6 then drives neuroinflammation that opposes opioid analgesia and paradoxically enhances pain sensitivity (opioid‑induced hyperalgesia).
*   IL‑6 can desensitise μ‑opioid receptors (MORs) via PKC‑ and PKA‑mediated phosphorylation, reducing opioid efficacy and contributing to tolerance.
*   Blocking IL‑6 signalling with anti‑IL‑6R antibodies or sgp130Fc has been shown to prevent and reverse opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and to restore morphine potency in preclinical models.

### 5. Supraspinal IL‑6 and Pain Comorbidities

*   **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and amygdala**: IL‑6 is elevated in the ACC and amygdala in chronic neuropathic pain models, where it enhances excitatory synaptic transmission and contributes to anxiety‑ and depression‑like behaviours.
*   **Hippocampus**: IL‑6 impairs hippocampal long‑term potentiation (LTP) and neurogenesis, contributing to the cognitive deficits (memory impairment, reduced executive function) that frequently accompany chronic pain.
*   **Descending facilitation**: IL‑6 in the brainstem (periaqueductal grey, rostral ventromedial medulla) can shift descending pain‑modulatory pathways towards facilitation, amplifying spinal nociceptive transmission.

### 6. Human Genetic Evidence

*   **IL6 gene polymorphisms**: Several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the *IL6* gene promoter (e.g., **‑174G>C**, rs1800795) that affect IL‑6 production have been associated with an increased risk of developing chronic neuropathic pain, higher pain intensity, and poorer response to analgesics, including opioids. Meta‑analyses have confirmed that the ‑174G allele, associated with higher IL‑6 expression, is more frequent in chronic pain patients. This provides genetic evidence that IL‑6 is a human pain mediator.
*   **gp130 polymorphisms**: Rare polymorphisms in *IL6ST* (encoding gp130) that affect receptor signalling have been linked to altered pain sensitivity and inflammatory conditions, although the data are less extensive than for *IL6*.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

A comprehensive body of preclinical work using pharmacological inhibitors, biologics, and genetic models has established that IL‑6 signalling, especially trans‑signalling, is both necessary and sufficient for the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain.

### 1. Pharmacological and Biological IL‑6 Inhibitors

*   **Anti‑IL‑6 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)** :
    *   **Murine anti‑IL‑6 antibodies**: In the partial sciatic nerve ligation (PNL) model, systemic or intrathecal administration of neutralizing anti‑IL‑6 antibodies significantly reduced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
    *   **Rapamycin‑conjugated anti‑IL‑6**: A novel engineered antibody to enhance spinal cord targeting has shown efficacy in reducing neuropathic pain.
*   **Anti‑IL‑6R monoclonal antibodies (tocilizumab, sarilumab)** : These block both classical and trans‑signalling by targeting the IL‑6R α‑chain (both membrane‑bound and soluble forms).
    *   In preclinical neuropathic pain models, tocilizumab (administered systemically) reduces mechanical hypersensitivity, spinal microgliosis, and pro‑inflammatory cytokine levels. The analgesic effect is not always complete, likely because the antibody’s large size limits CNS penetration.
    *   Intrathecal anti‑IL‑6R antibodies produce more robust analgesia, confirming the importance of central IL‑6 signalling.
*   **Selective trans‑signalling inhibitors (sgp130Fc / olamkicept)** :
    *   **sgp130Fc** (soluble gp130‑Fc fusion protein) is the most definitive tool for dissecting classical vs. trans‑signalling roles. In multiple neuropathic pain models (CCI, SNI, SNL), systemic or intrathecal sgp130Fc:
        *   Profoundly reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
        *   Blocks the IL‑6‑induced direct excitation of DRG neurons.
        *   Suppresses spinal microglial and astrocytic activation.
        *   Restores KCC2 expression and GABAergic inhibition.
        *   Prevents and reverses opioid‑induced hyperalgesia.
    *   The efficacy of sgp130Fc, which leaves classical IL‑6 signalling intact, demonstrates that **IL‑6 trans‑signalling is the dominant pathogenic pathway in neuropathic pain**.

### 2. Genetic Models

*   **IL‑6 knockout mice**: *Il6*‑/‑ mice exhibit significantly attenuated mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia after chronic constriction injury (CCI) and spared nerve injury (SNI). They also show reduced spinal microgliosis and cytokine induction. However, pain is not completely abolished, indicating that IL‑6 is a major, but not the sole, mediator.
*   **gp130 conditional knockout**: Deletion of gp130 in sensory neurons (using Advillin‑Cre) or in astrocytes (GFAP‑Cre) reduces neuropathic pain, confirming that both neuronal and glial gp130 signalling is required for full pain development.
*   **sIL‑6R overexpression**: Transgenic mice overexpressing sIL‑6R exhibit enhanced pain sensitivity and exaggerated neuroinflammatory responses to nerve injury, directly demonstrating the pro‑nociceptive role of the trans‑signalling arm.
*   **SOCS3 deletion**: SOCS3 is the inducible negative regulator of IL‑6‑STAT3 signalling. Mice with SOCS3 deficiency in sensory neurons or glial cells show exacerbated neuropathic pain and prolonged STAT3 activation, highlighting the importance of endogenous braking mechanisms.

### 3. JAK/STAT3 Pathway Inhibition

Because the JAK/STAT3 pathway is the principal intracellular effector of IL‑6 signalling, JAK2 inhibitors (AG490, ruxolitinib, baricitinib) and STAT3 inhibitors (Stattic, S3I‑201) reliably reduce neuropathic pain, as detailed in the dedicated JAK/STAT file. The analgesic effects of these inhibitors are in part due to the blockade of IL‑6‑driven transcription.

### 4. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces spinal IL‑6 levels and IL‑6R expression in neuropathic pain models, contributing to its analgesic effect.
*   **Resveratrol, curcumin, and sulforaphane**: Suppress IL‑6 production by inhibiting NF‑κB and MAPK pathways and by activating Nrf2. They also reduce IL‑6‑induced STAT3 phosphorylation.
*   **Vitamin D**: Calcitriol can suppress IL‑6 expression in microglia and astrocytes, and vitamin D deficiency has been associated with increased chronic pain risk and elevated IL‑6 levels.

### 5. Endogenous Modulation of IL‑6 Activity

*   **Interleukin‑10 (IL‑10)** : The anti‑inflammatory cytokine IL‑10 is a physiological antagonist of IL‑6 production and signalling. IL‑10 suppresses IL‑6 synthesis in microglia and astrocytes and upregulates SOCS3. Preclinical administration of IL‑10 reduces neuropathic pain in part by inhibiting IL‑6.
*   **Vagus nerve stimulation**: The cholinergic anti‑inflammatory pathway, mediated by α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on macrophages and microglia, suppresses IL‑6 production. Vagus nerve stimulation reduces serum and spinal IL‑6 levels and alleviates neuropathic pain in some models.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Anti‑IL‑6 therapy in rheumatoid arthritis and beyond**:
    *   **Tocilizumab** (anti‑IL‑6R) and **sarilumab** (anti‑IL‑6R) are FDA‑approved for rheumatoid arthritis, giant cell arteritis, and cytokine release syndrome. In these conditions, they produce **rapid, substantial, and sustained reductions in pain**, often within days of administration, well before measurable improvements in inflammation (swollen/tender joint counts). This rapid analgesic effect is consistent with a direct anti‑nociceptive mechanism, independent of their anti‑inflammatory action.
    *   A **systematic review and meta‑analysis** of randomized controlled trials concluded that IL‑6 inhibitors (tocilizumab, sarilumab) are superior to TNF‑α inhibitors for relieving pain in rheumatoid arthritis, strongly supporting IL‑6 as a key human pain mediator.
    *   This clinical success has led to investigations of anti‑IL‑6 therapies in other chronic pain conditions, including **chronic low back pain with a neuropathic component**, **painful diabetic neuropathy**, and **complex regional pain syndrome**. Results from ongoing trials are eagerly awaited.
*   **Selective trans‑signalling inhibition (olamkicept / sgp130Fc)** :
    *   **Olamkicept (FE 999301)** is a recombinant sgp130Fc fusion protein that selectively blocks IL‑6 trans‑signalling. It has completed Phase 2a trials in inflammatory bowel disease, demonstrating a favourable safety profile and clinical efficacy.
    *   The selective blockade of trans‑signalling offers a potential **safety advantage** over pan‑IL‑6 or IL‑6R inhibition, as it preserves classical IL‑6 signalling, which is important for hepatic acute‑phase responses, metabolic regulation, and host defence. This makes olamkicept an especially attractive candidate for chronic, non‑life‑threatening pain conditions where long‑term immunosuppression is a concern.
*   **JAK inhibitors for neuropathic pain**: Baricitinib (JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor) and other JAK inhibitors that block the IL‑6‑STAT3 pathway are already approved for RA and are being tested in chronic pain. The clinical trial of baricitinib in chronic low back pain with a neuropathic component is a direct translational test of the IL‑6/JAK/STAT3 pathway in human pain.
*   **IL‑6 as a biomarker**:
    *   Elevated serum, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) IL‑6 levels have been consistently associated with the presence and severity of chronic neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, and post‑surgical pain. IL‑6 levels correlate with pain intensity and predict the transition from acute to chronic pain.
    *   IL‑6 could serve as a **theranostic biomarker** to identify patients with a dominant IL‑6‑driven inflammatory pain endotype who would be most likely to respond to anti‑IL‑6 therapies.
*   **Combination strategies**:
    *   **Anti‑IL‑6 + opioids**: Blocking IL‑6 may prevent opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and tolerance, allowing lower opioid doses and better long‑term pain control.
    *   **Anti‑IL‑6 + KCC2 enhancers**: IL‑6 is a major driver of KCC2 downregulation; combining IL‑6 blockade with KCC2‑activating drugs (e.g., CLP257) could synergistically restore GABAergic inhibition.
    *   **Anti‑IL‑6 + anti‑CGRP or anti‑SP**: IL‑6 promotes CGRP and substance P release; dual targeting may be especially effective for neurogenic inflammation‑associated pain.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Immunosuppression and infection risk**: IL‑6 signalling is critical for the host response to bacterial (especially Gram‑positive and Gram‑negative) and viral infections. Tocilizumab and sarilumab increase the risk of serious bacterial infections (e.g., pneumonia, cellulitis, tuberculosis reactivation) and have a black‑box warning for these events. The risk is dose‑ and duration‑dependent. Olamkicept, by sparing classical IL‑6 signalling, may carry a lower infectious risk, but this requires confirmation in large long‑term trials.
*   **Metabolic and hepatic effects**: IL‑6 regulates glucose and lipid metabolism, and IL‑6R blockade can cause mild increases in LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. IL‑6 also drives the hepatic synthesis of C‑reactive protein (CRP); IL‑6R blockade normalises CRP, complicating its use as a biomarker of infection.
*   **Dual role of IL‑6 in health and repair**: IL‑6 has important physiological roles in **exercise‑induced muscle repair**, **bone metabolism**, **liver regeneration**, and **neurogenesis**. The classical signalling pathway is largely responsible for these protective effects. Complete, long‑term blockade of all IL‑6 signalling could impair tissue repair after injury, delay wound healing, and negatively affect bone density. Selective trans‑signalling inhibition is designed to preserve these beneficial classical signalling functions.
*   **CNS penetration of biologics**: Anti‑IL‑6 and anti‑IL‑6R monoclonal antibodies are large proteins that do not readily cross the intact blood‑brain barrier (BBB). Their central effects are likely mediated by entry at circumventricular organs, the choroid plexus, or during periods of BBB disruption, which occurs in chronic pain. The degree to which the robust clinical analgesic effects of tocilizumab in RA are mediated centrally vs. peripherally is debated. Engineered antibodies with enhanced BBB penetration, or small‑molecule gp130 modulators, may be required for optimal efficacy in primary neuropathic pain conditions.
*   **Sex differences**:
    *   IL‑6 expression and signalling exhibit sex‑dependent differences. In preclinical models, spinal IL‑6 levels and IL‑6‑induced pain behaviours are often greater in female rodents, and microglial IL‑6 production may be differentially regulated by sex hormones.
    *   In humans, women with chronic pain have higher circulating IL‑6 levels than men, and the analgesic response to IL‑6 blockade may differ between sexes. Sex‑stratified analyses in clinical trials are essential.
*   **Redundancy with other gp130‑family cytokines**: IL‑6 is only one member of the gp130 cytokine family. LIF, oncostatin M, CNTF, and IL‑27 also activate gp130 and JAK/STAT3 and can compensate for IL‑6 loss. A pan‑gp130 blockade would be too toxic, but strategies that target the shared JAK/STAT pathway may inadvertently block multiple family members, increasing the risk of side effects.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**: The IL‑6 system is centrally integrated into the neuroimmune pain network.
    *   **JAK/STAT3**: The downstream effector pathway; nearly all pronociceptive effects of IL‑6 are transduced through JAK/STAT3.
    *   **TNF‑α and IL‑1β**: IL‑6 synthesis is stimulated by TNF‑α and IL‑1β, and IL‑6 in turn promotes their production, forming a feed‑forward cytokine loop.
    *   **NLRP3 inflammasome**: IL‑6 can prime the NLRP3 inflammasome via STAT3‑mediated upregulation of NLRP3 and pro‑IL‑1β.
    *   **BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2**: IL‑6‑driven microglial BDNF release and direct STAT3‑mediated KCC2 downregulation are central to disinhibition.
    *   **CCL2/CCR2, CX3CL1/CX3CR1**: IL‑6 induces the expression of these chemokines, promoting microglial and monocyte recruitment.
    *   **TRPV1, Nav1.7/1.8**: IL‑6 trans‑signalling directly sensitises these channels, increasing nociceptor excitability.
    *   **PPARγ**: PPARγ activation transrepresses the IL‑6 gene, providing a negative regulatory loop.
    *   **TLR4**: TLR4 activation by DAMPs induces IL‑6 expression; IL‑6 can upregulate TLR4, enhancing innate immune responses.
