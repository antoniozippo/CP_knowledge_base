# Involvement of the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End‑products (RAGE) in Neuropathic Pain

The receptor for advanced glycation end‑products (RAGE, encoded by the *AGER* gene) is a multiligand transmembrane receptor belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily. Originally characterised as the primary receptor for advanced glycation end‑products (AGEs) that accumulate in hyperglycaemia and oxidative stress, RAGE is now recognised as a pattern‑recognition receptor (PRR) capable of binding a diverse array of **damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)** —including **high‑mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)**, **members of the S100/calgranulin family (S100A8/A9, S100B, S100A12)** , **amyloid‑β**, **macrophage antigen‑1 (Mac‑1)** , and **phosphatidylserine**. In the context of neuropathic pain, RAGE is significantly upregulated in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, spinal dorsal horn glial cells, and peripheral nerves following traumatic, metabolic, or toxic injury. Ligand‑bound RAGE activates multiple intracellular signalling pathways—most prominently **NF‑κB**, **mitogen‑activated protein kinases (MAPKs: ERK1/2, p38, JNK)** , **Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT)** , and **reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via NADPH oxidase**—thereby driving a self‑sustaining cycle of neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal sensitisation that is central to the development and maintenance of mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain. Pharmacological blockade of RAGE with small‑molecule antagonists (e.g., **FPS‑ZM1**, **azeliragon/TTP488**), the soluble decoy receptor **sRAGE**, or neutralising anti‑RAGE antibodies consistently attenuates pain behaviours, reduces glial activation, and restores inhibitory neurotransmission in rodent models of diabetic neuropathy, chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy, traumatic nerve injury, and spinal cord injury. Clinically, the RAGE antagonist azeliragon has been investigated in Phase III trials for Alzheimer’s disease, providing a potential repurposing opportunity for chronic pain. However, the complex ligand repertoire of RAGE, its essential role in tissue repair and innate immunity, and the existence of multiple splice variants that modulate its function pose significant challenges that must be addressed for successful clinical translation.

## 🧬 The RAGE System: Gene, Receptor, and Ligands

- **Gene and protein structure**: RAGE is encoded by the *AGER* gene located on human chromosome 6p21.3 within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class III region. The full‑length, membrane‑bound receptor (flRAGE) is a type I transmembrane protein composed of an extracellular region containing one V‑type (variable) immunoglobulin domain and two C‑type (constant) immunoglobulin domains, a single transmembrane helix, and a short, highly charged cytoplasmic tail. The **V‑domain** is the primary ligand‑binding site for HMGB1, S100 proteins, and AGEs.
- **Splice variants and isoforms**: Alternative splicing of *AGER* mRNA generates several isoforms with distinct functions:
    - **Full‑length RAGE (flRAGE)** : The canonical, signal‑transducing transmembrane receptor.
    - **Soluble RAGE (sRAGE)** : Generated either by alternative splicing (endogenous soluble RAGE, esRAGE) or by proteolytic cleavage of the extracellular domain by ADAM10 and MMP‑9. sRAGE circulates in plasma and acts as a **decoy receptor** that binds RAGE ligands and prevents their interaction with flRAGE, thereby functioning as an endogenous anti‑inflammatory buffer.
    - **Dominant‑negative RAGE (dnRAGE)** : Lacks the intracellular domain; can heterodimerise with flRAGE and inhibit signalling.
- **Ligand repertoire**: RAGE is a multiligand receptor that recognises a diverse set of endogenous DAMPs and pathogens:
    - **Advanced glycation end‑products (AGEs)** : Formed by non‑enzymatic glycation and oxidation of proteins and lipids, particularly under hyperglycaemic and oxidative conditions. AGEs accumulate in peripheral nerves and the spinal cord in diabetes and ageing.
    - **High‑mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)** : A nuclear protein released passively from necrotic cells or actively secreted by activated immune cells and microglia. It is a major pro‑nociceptive RAGE ligand.
    - **S100/calgranulin proteins**: S100A8/A9 (calprotectin), S100B, and S100A12 are released from activated myeloid cells, microglia, and stressed neurons. The S100A8/A9–RAGE interaction is a key driver of neuroinflammation in pain.
    - **Amyloid‑β and other protein aggregates**: RAGE binds aggregated peptides and contributes to their cellular uptake and pro‑inflammatory signalling.
    - **Mac‑1 (CD11b/CD18)** : An integrin expressed on microglia and macrophages that mediates cell adhesion and activation.
    - **Phosphatidylserine**: A phospholipid exposed on the outer leaflet of apoptotic cells, linking RAGE to the clearance of dying cells.
- **Downstream signalling pathways**: Ligand binding induces RAGE dimerisation and the recruitment of the adaptor protein **diaphanous‑1 (mDia1)** to the cytoplasmic tail, which is essential for subsequent signalling. The major pathways activated include:
    - **NF‑κB pathway**: RAGE signalling leads to the persistent activation of NF‑κB by promoting the degradation of IκBα and by generating an auto‑amplificatory loop—RAGE activation increases NF‑κB, which in turn upregulates RAGE expression. This sustained NF‑κB activity drives the transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines, and inducible enzymes (iNOS, COX‑2).
    - **MAPK pathways (ERK1/2, p38, JNK)** : Contribute to neuronal sensitisation, glial activation, and the stabilisation of pro‑inflammatory mRNA transcripts.
    - **JAK/STAT pathway**: RAGE can activate JAK2, leading to STAT3 phosphorylation and the expression of genes that promote glial reactivity and pain.
    - **NADPH oxidase‑dependent ROS generation**: RAGE induces the production of reactive oxygen species, which in turn drive further AGE formation and RAGE ligand generation, creating a vicious cycle of oxidative stress and inflammation.
- **Tissue and cellular expression in the nociceptive system**:
    - **Primary sensory neurons (DRG)** : RAGE is constitutively expressed at low levels in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. After nerve injury or in diabetes, its expression is markedly upregulated, and it co‑localises with TRPV1, Nav1.8, and CGRP.
    - **Spinal dorsal horn**: RAGE is expressed on **microglia**, **astrocytes**, and some neurons. Its expression increases with age and in response to nerve injury, particularly on activated microglia and reactive astrocytes.
    - **Peripheral nerve**: RAGE is upregulated on **Schwann cells**, **endothelial cells**, and **infiltrating macrophages** after injury, contributing to local inflammation, axonal degeneration, and impaired regeneration.
    - **Supraspinal regions**: RAGE is present on microglia and neurons in the hippocampus, cortex, and amygdala, where it has been implicated in pain‑related cognitive decline and emotional dysregulation.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of RAGE in Neuropathic Pain

RAGE contributes to neuropathic pain through a multi‑tiered mechanism that amplifies neuroinflammation, promotes oxidative stress, and directly sensitises nociceptors. A hallmark of RAGE‑mediated pathology is the **auto‑amplificatory positive‑feedback loop** in which ligand‑induced RAGE signalling upregulates the expression of both the receptor and its ligands, sustaining the inflammatory response long after the initial injury.

### 1. Upregulation of RAGE and Its Ligands After Nerve Injury

- **Traumatic nerve injury (CCI, SNI, SNL)** : Within days after nerve injury, RAGE mRNA and protein levels are significantly increased in the ipsilateral DRG and spinal dorsal horn. This upregulation is paralleled by the release of the RAGE ligands HMGB1, S100A8/A9, and S100B from damaged neurons, reactive microglia, and astrocytes.
- **Diabetic neuropathy**: Hyperglycaemia‑driven AGE formation and oxidative stress lead to a profound upregulation of RAGE in the DRG, peripheral nerves, and spinal cord. AGE accumulation is a hallmark of diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and the AGE–RAGE axis is considered a central mediator of the degenerative and painful components of the disease.
- **Chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy (CIPN)** : Agents such as paclitaxel, oxaliplatin, and bortezomib induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage in DRG neurons, promoting the release of HMGB1 and the formation of AGEs. This is accompanied by RAGE upregulation, which drives the neuroinflammatory response that causes pain and axonal degeneration.
- **Ageing and pain chronicity**: RAGE expression naturally increases with age, which may contribute to the higher prevalence and severity of chronic pain in the elderly.

### 2. Sustained NF‑κB Activation and the Auto‑Amplificatory Loop

The defining feature of RAGE signalling is its ability to sustain **persistent NF‑κB activation**, in contrast to the transient, self‑limited activation induced by TLR4 or TNFR. This is achieved through several mechanisms:
- RAGE activation generates **de novo NF‑κB p65 mRNA and protein**, providing a continuous supply of the transcription factor.
- RAGE signalling simultaneously produces ROS that stabilise the newly synthesised NF‑κB, and it can directly sustain the nuclear activity of NF‑κB independently of IκB degradation.
- The activated NF‑κB drives the transcription of **RAGE itself** and of its ligands (**HMGB1**, **S100A8/A9**, **AGEs**), creating a self‑perpetuating, **auto‑amplificatory feedback loop** that maintains chronic inflammation and pain.
- Pharmacological blockade of RAGE with azeliragon breaks this loop by reducing NF‑κB activation and downregulating both RAGE and its ligands.

### 3. Microglial and Astrocytic Activation via RAGE

- **Microglial M1 polarisation**: HMGB1 and S100A8/A9 binding to RAGE on spinal microglia activates the NF‑κB and MAPK pathways, driving the pro‑inflammatory M1 phenotype. Microglial‑derived TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, and reactive oxygen species sensitise spinal nociceptive neurons and contribute to central sensitisation.
- **Astrogliosis**: RAGE activation on astrocytes promotes their reactive transformation, characterised by the upregulation of GFAP, the downregulation of glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), and the secretion of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1) that further recruit microglia and macrophages.
- **Glial–neuronal crosstalk**: RAGE‑activated microglia and astrocytes release BDNF, which acts on neuronal TrkB receptors to downregulate the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2, causing the disinhibition that underlies mechanical allodynia. They also release MMP‑9, which degrades perineuronal nets and contributes to the loss of fast‑spiking PV⁺ GABAergic interneurons.

### 4. Direct Sensitisation of Primary Afferent Neurons

RAGE is functionally expressed on nociceptor cell bodies and peripheral terminals, where its direct activation contributes to peripheral sensitisation.

- **Increased neuronal excitability**: Application of HMGB1 or S100A8/A9 to DRG neurons increases intracellular calcium and enhances the activity of TRPV1, TRPA1, Nav1.7, and Nav1.8 through PKA‑ and PKC‑dependent phosphorylation. This lowers the action potential threshold and promotes ectopic firing.
- **CGRP release**: RAGE activation stimulates calcium‑dependent exocytosis of calcitonin gene‑related peptide (CGRP) from nociceptor terminals, driving neurogenic inflammation and central sensitisation.
- **AGE‑mediated axonal dysfunction**: In diabetic neuropathy, AGE modification of axonal cytoskeletal proteins (e.g., neurofilaments, α‑tubulin) impairs axonal transport and contributes to sensory fibre degeneration and pain.

### 5. Spinal Central Sensitisation via NMDA Receptor Potentiation

RAGE‑driven neuroinflammation in the spinal cord promotes the development and maintenance of central sensitisation.

- The pro‑inflammatory cytokines TNF‑α and IL‑1β, released by RAGE‑activated microglia, phosphorylate the GluN2B subunit of NMDA receptors on dorsal horn neurons via Src and PKC, increasing their calcium conductance and promoting long‑term potentiation (LTP) at C‑fibre synapses.
- RAGE signalling also enhances the synthesis of prostaglandins (PGE₂) through the induction of COX‑2, which further sensitises spinal neurons.

### 6. Loss of GABAergic Inhibition and Interneuron Death

- **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2 disinhibition**: As described, RAGE‑activated microglia and astrocytes release BDNF, which downregulates KCC2 and converts GABA/glycine‑mediated inhibition into excitation.
- **Excitotoxic loss of inhibitory interneurons**: The sustained neuroinflammatory environment promotes the calcium overload → mitochondrial failure → caspase‑3 activation → apoptosis cascade in PV⁺ GABAergic interneurons, leading to their selective loss and the structural disinhibition of spinal nociceptive circuits. RAGE thus indirectly contributes to the reduction in inhibitory tone.

### 7. Peripheral Nerve Pathology and Impaired Regeneration

- **Wallerian degeneration**: In the injured nerve, RAGE on Schwann cells and macrophages binds HMGB1 and S100 proteins released from damaged axons, promoting the clearance of myelin debris and the recruitment of additional immune cells.
- **Impaired regeneration**: Chronic RAGE activation can impair axonal regeneration by sustaining a pro‑inflammatory environment and by directly modifying axonal proteins through AGE formation (glycation).

### 8. Supraspinal RAGE and Pain Comorbidities

- **Hippocampus**: RAGE is upregulated in hippocampal microglia in chronic neuropathic pain, where it contributes to neuroinflammation that impairs neurogenesis and long‑term potentiation, resulting in cognitive deficits.
- **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and amygdala**: RAGE‑mediated neuroinflammation in the ACC and amygdala enhances the emotional‑affective component of pain and contributes to anxiety‑ and depression‑like behaviours.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Small‑Molecule RAGE Antagonists

- **FPS‑ZM1**: A high‑affinity, non‑toxic, brain‑penetrant RAGE antagonist that specifically blocks the interaction between RAGE and its ligands (HMGB1, S100B, Aβ). Intraperitoneal or intrathecal administration of FPS‑ZM1:
    - Significantly reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNI, diabetic neuropathy, and paclitaxel‑induced CIPN models.
    - Suppresses spinal microglial and astrocytic activation, reduces NF‑κB nuclear translocation, and decreases the expression of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, iNOS, and COX‑2.
    - Restores KCC2 expression, preserves GABAergic interneuron numbers, and normalises inhibitory neurotransmission.
    - Breaks the RAGE auto‑amplificatory loop by reducing the expression of both RAGE and its ligands.
- **Azeliragon (TTP488)** : An orally bioavailable, small‑molecule RAGE antagonist that has been extensively tested in clinical trials for Alzheimer’s disease. Preclinically, azeliragon:
    - Attenuates mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in rodent models of traumatic nerve injury, diabetic neuropathy, and CIPN.
    - Reduces AGE‑mediated axonal degeneration in diabetic nerves.
    - Suppresses spinal microgliosis and neuroinflammation.

### 2. Soluble RAGE (sRAGE) as a Decoy Therapy

- **Recombinant sRAGE**: Systemic or intrathecal administration of recombinant sRAGE protein, which acts as a ligand trap by binding circulating and tissue DAMPs (HMGB1, S100A8/A9, AGEs), has been shown to:
    - Reduce mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in models of CCI, diabetic neuropathy, and arthritis‑associated pain.
    - Limit the infiltration of inflammatory cells into the spinal cord and DRG.
    - Suppress the pro‑inflammatory cytokine cascade and restore normal inhibitory tone.
- **Endogenous sRAGE**: Plasma levels of endogenous sRAGE are inversely correlated with pain severity in diabetic neuropathy patients, suggesting that sRAGE serves as a natural anti‑nociceptive buffer. Enhancing endogenous sRAGE production or reducing its degradation (by inhibiting MMP‑9 or ADAM10) may be a therapeutic strategy.

### 3. RAGE‑Neutralising Antibodies

- **Anti‑RAGE monoclonal antibodies**: Intrathecal or systemic administration of antibodies that block the ligand‑binding V‑domain of RAGE:
    - Reduces pain behaviours and spinal neuroinflammation in CCI and diabetic neuropathy models.
    - Prevents the development of opioid‑induced hyperalgesia by blocking RAGE‑mediated microglial activation.
- **Anti‑HMGB1 antibodies**: By neutralising a major RAGE ligand, these antibodies indirectly reduce RAGE signalling and produce analgesia, confirming the importance of the HMGB1–RAGE axis.

### 4. Genetic Models

- ***Rage*‑/‑ (Ager‑/‑) mice**: Mice lacking RAGE are viable, fertile, and exhibit normal acute pain sensitivity. In models of CCI, SNI, diabetic neuropathy, and CIPN, *Rage*‑/‑ mice show:
    - A profound reduction in mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
    - Significantly attenuated microglial and astrocytic activation, and lower levels of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, and CCL2.
    - Preservation of KCC2 expression and GABAergic interneuron numbers.
    - Reduced AGE accumulation and oxidative stress in diabetic nerves.
- **Conditional RAGE knockout**: Deletion of RAGE specifically in CX3CR1‑positive cells (microglia/macrophages) recapitulates much of the pain‑protective phenotype, confirming the critical role of microglial RAGE. Neuronal deletion also contributes, indicating that both glial and neuronal RAGE are involved.

### 5. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

- **Resveratrol**: Activates SIRT1, which suppresses RAGE expression and RAGE‑mediated NF‑κB activation, reducing neuroinflammation and pain.
- **Curcumin**: Inhibits RAGE signalling by directly binding to the receptor and by suppressing NF‑κB, with analgesic effects in CCI and diabetic neuropathy models.
- **Sulforaphane**: Activates Nrf2, which reduces oxidative stress and AGE formation, indirectly decreasing RAGE ligand availability.
- **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces RAGE expression and the levels of its ligands (HMGB1, S100B) in the spinal cord and DRG of nerve‑injured animals, contributing to its analgesic effect.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

- **Azeliragon (TTP488) repurposing for neuropathic pain**: Azeliragon is the most clinically advanced RAGE antagonist. It was investigated in a large Phase III trial for mild‑to‑moderate Alzheimer’s disease (the STEADFAST study). Although the trial did not meet its primary cognitive endpoints, azeliragon demonstrated a well‑characterised safety profile, including gastrointestinal adverse events (diarrhoea, nausea) and a possible risk of cognitive worsening at high doses. Its ability to cross the blood‑brain barrier and potently inhibit RAGE makes it an attractive candidate for repurposing in chronic pain, particularly in conditions with a strong neuroinflammatory and metabolic component (e.g., painful diabetic neuropathy, CIPN). A clinical trial of azeliragon for diabetic neuropathic pain would be a logical next step.
- **sRAGE as a biomarker**: Low plasma sRAGE levels are associated with increased pain severity in diabetic neuropathy and are predictive of the development of chronic pain after surgery. sRAGE could serve as a **theranostic biomarker** to identify patients with a depleted endogenous anti‑inflammatory buffer who would most benefit from RAGE‑targeted therapy.
- **Peripheral vs. central RAGE**: Because RAGE is expressed on both central glial cells and peripheral nerves/DRG, peripherally restricted RAGE antagonists could be effective for conditions where peripheral sensitisation predominates (e.g., diabetic neuropathy), while CNS‑penetrant antagonists are required for central neuropathic pain (e.g., spinal cord injury pain). Azeliragon is BBB‑penetrant; peripherally restricted molecules are also under investigation.
- **Combination strategies**:
    - **RAGE antagonist + anti‑TNF or anti‑IL‑6 biologic**: RAGE is an upstream driver of these cytokines; dual blockade could produce synergistic suppression of neuroinflammation.
    - **RAGE antagonist + KCC2 enhancer**: RAGE blockade reduces BDNF‑driven KCC2 downregulation; combining with a direct KCC2 activator could powerfully restore GABAergic inhibition.
    - **RAGE antagonist + opioid**: RAGE antagonists prevent opioid‑induced hyperalgesia by blocking microglial activation, potentially enabling more effective and sustained opioid analgesia.
    - **RAGE antagonist + TLR4 antagonist**: RAGE and TLR4 share many ligands (e.g., HMGB1, S100A8/A9) and signalling pathways. Combined blockade may be more effective than inhibiting either receptor alone, though with increased immunosuppressive risk.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

- **Physiological roles of RAGE**: RAGE is not solely a pathological receptor. It participates in normal physiological processes, including axonal outgrowth during development, innate immune surveillance, and the clearance of apoptotic cells. Complete, prolonged RAGE blockade could impair these functions. The auto‑amplificatory loop that sustains chronic inflammation also serves to initially contain injury and clear debris; interrupting this loop too early or too completely could delay tissue repair.
- **Multiple ligands and receptor redundancy**: RAGE shares its ligands with other receptors (e.g., HMGB1 also binds TLR2 and TLR4; S100A8/A9 binds TLR4). Blocking RAGE alone may shift ligand binding to these other receptors, which could compensate for the loss of RAGE signalling and maintain inflammation. This suggests that targeting common ligands (e.g., with neutralising antibodies) or combining RAGE with TLR antagonists may be more effective.
- **RAGE in the vasculature**: RAGE is expressed on endothelial cells and contributes to the regulation of vascular permeability. Chronic RAGE blockade could affect the blood‑brain barrier and blood‑spinal cord barrier, potentially disrupting the controlled entry of immune cells and molecules needed for surveillance and repair.
- **RAGE in cancer**: RAGE is implicated in tumour progression and metastasis for certain cancers. The long‑term effects of RAGE antagonism on cancer risk are not fully understood.
- **Sex differences**: The role of RAGE in neuropathic pain may differ between sexes, as has been observed for other innate immune receptors. Sex‑specific analyses in preclinical models and clinical trials are essential.
- **Interactions with the wider pain network**:
    - **TLR2, TLR4, TLR9**: RAGE and these Toll‑like receptors recognise overlapping ligands and engage similar downstream signalling pathways (MyD88‑dependent for TLRs, mDia1‑dependent for RAGE). They often synergise, and their simultaneous blockade may be required for maximal analgesia.
    - **NLRP3 inflammasome**: RAGE‑mediated NF‑κB activation provides the priming signal for NLRP3, and RAGE‑induced ROS contribute to inflammasome activation.
    - **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: RAGE‑activated microglia release BDNF, which drives the canonical disinhibition cascade.
    - **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: Major effectors of RAGE‑mediated neuroinflammation.
    - **Cav3.2, Nav1.7/Nav1.8, TRPV1**: RAGE signalling sensitises these pronociceptive ion channels through PKC‑ and PKA‑dependent phosphorylation.
    - **MMP‑9**: RAGE induces MMP‑9 expression in glial cells, which degrades perineuronal nets and contributes to interneuron dysfunction.
    - **PPARγ**: PPARγ activation can transrepress RAGE expression and break the RAGE‑NF‑κB feedback loop.
