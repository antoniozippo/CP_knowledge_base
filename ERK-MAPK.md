# Involvement of ERK/MAPK in Neuropathic Pain

The mitogen‑activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades are evolutionarily conserved intracellular signalling pathways that convert a wide variety of extracellular stimuli—growth factors, inflammatory cytokines, neurotransmitters, and cellular stressors—into coordinated cellular responses, including proliferation, differentiation, survival, and synaptic plasticity. Among the three major MAPK families (ERK, p38, and JNK), the **extracellular signal‑regulated kinase (ERK) pathway** (Raf–MEK–ERK) is the most extensively validated as a **central molecular hub** in the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain. ERK1 and ERK2 (ERK1/2) are expressed throughout the nociceptive neuraxis—dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, spinal dorsal horn neurons, microglia, and astrocytes—and are rapidly phosphorylated and activated after peripheral nerve injury. Once active, ERK phosphorylates a vast array of substrates, including ion channels (Nav1.7, Kv4.2, TRPV1), transcription factors (CREB, c‑Fos, Elk‑1), translational regulators (MNK1/eIF4E), and cytoskeletal proteins, thereby amplifying nociceptor excitability, driving central sensitisation and long‑term potentiation at spinal synapses, and orchestrating the pro‑inflammatory phenotype of glial cells. Pharmacological inhibition of MEK, the upstream kinase that activates ERK, or direct ERK blockade, consistently reverses mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain in diverse preclinical models of traumatic, metabolic, and chemotherapeutic neuropathy. The success of MEK inhibitors (e.g., trametinib) in oncology has provided clinical‑grade drugs that could be repurposed for chronic pain, but their widespread systemic actions and critical physiological roles—particularly in learning, memory, and cardiac function—pose significant challenges that demand cell‑type‑specific, temporally controlled, or locally delivered therapeutic strategies.

## 🧬 The ERK/MAPK Pathway: Components and Normal Function

*   **Core signalling module**: The canonical ERK pathway is a three‑tiered kinase cascade:
    1.  **MAP kinase kinase kinase (MAP3K)** : Members of the **Raf** family (A‑Raf, B‑Raf, C‑Raf/Raf‑1) are the primary MAP3Ks for ERK. They are activated by the small GTPase **Ras**, which in turn is recruited to activated receptor tyrosine kinases (e.g., TrkA, TrkB, EGFR) via the adaptor protein Grb2 and the guanine nucleotide exchange factor SOS.
    2.  **MAP kinase kinase (MAP2K)** : **MEK1** (encoded by *MAP2K1*) and **MEK2** (*MAP2K2*) are dual‑specificity kinases that phosphorylate ERK1/2 on both a threonine and a tyrosine residue (Thr¹⁸³ and Tyr¹⁸⁵ in ERK2). MEK1/2 are highly selective for ERK1/2.
    3.  **MAP kinase (MAPK)** : **ERK1** (p44, *MAPK3*) and **ERK2** (p42, *MAPK1*) are the effector serine/threonine kinases that phosphorylate hundreds of cytoplasmic and nuclear substrates.
*   **Scaffolding and subcellular localisation**: Specificity and efficiency are ensured by scaffold proteins (KSR1, MP1, IQGAP1) and by the spatial regulation of ERK. In quiescent cells, ERK is predominantly cytoplasmic, but upon activation it translocates to the nucleus. Sustained nuclear ERK activity is required for transcriptional reprogramming in pain.
*   **Negative regulators**: Dual‑specificity phosphatases (DUSPs, e.g., DUSP1/MKP‑1, DUSP6/MKP‑3) dephosphorylate ERK at its activation loop, terminating the signal. Sprouty (SPRY) and Spred proteins also inhibit the pathway at the level of Raf or MEK. After nerve injury, DUSP expression is often downregulated, contributing to sustained ERK activation.
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **DRG neurons**: ERK1/2 are highly expressed in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. MEK/ERK signalling in DRG neurons is activated by NGF, BDNF, and inflammatory mediators, and is essential for the phosphorylation and sensitisation of ion channels and for the induction of long‑term changes in gene expression.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: ERK1/2 are expressed in postsynaptic lamina I–II neurons and in presynaptic primary afferent terminals. ERK activation in spinal neurons is a hallmark of central sensitisation and is required for NMDA receptor‑dependent and ‑independent forms of pain‑related plasticity.
    *   **Microglia and astrocytes**: Under pathological conditions, ERK is robustly activated in spinal microglia and astrocytes, where it drives the expression and release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines, and growth factors (BDNF).
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: ERK is activated in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), central amygdala (CeA), and periaqueductal grey (PAG) in chronic pain, contributing to affective, cognitive, and descending modulatory components.
*   **Physiological functions in the adult CNS**: ERK signalling is indispensable for synaptic plasticity, learning and memory, neurogenesis, and neuronal survival. In nociceptors, physiological ERK activity maintains basal sensitivity and is involved in the early phase of acute pain signalling.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of ERK in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury, ERK is persistently activated at multiple levels of the pain pathway through a combination of growth factor, cytokine, and ionotropic receptor signalling. The downstream consequences are amplificatory, affecting both the sensitivity of ion channels and the transcription of pronociceptive genes.

### 1. Activation in Primary Afferent Neurons and Peripheral Sensitisation

*   **Upstream signals**: After nerve injury, **NGF** and **BDNF**, released from damaged tissue and Schwann cells, bind to TrkA and TrkB receptors on nociceptors, activating the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK cascade. Pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β) also activate ERK via their respective receptors and adaptor proteins (TRAF6). Additionally, increased intracellular Ca²⁺ through TRPV1, NMDA receptors, or CaV3.2 can stimulate Ca²⁺‑sensitive guanine nucleotide exchange factors (RasGRF) that activate Ras.
*   **Direct post‑translational modulation of ion channels**: ERK directly phosphorylates several pronociceptive ion channels at consensus sites, enhancing their activity:
    *   **Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9**: ERK‑mediated phosphorylation increases sodium current amplitude, shifts activation to more hyperpolarised potentials, and slows inactivation, leading to neuronal hyperexcitability and ectopic firing. In paclitaxel‑induced neuropathy, ERK‑dependent Nav1.7 upregulation is a key driver of pain.
    *   **Kv4.2 (A‑type potassium channel)** : ERK phosphorylates Kv4.2 at Ser⁶¹⁶, reducing its surface expression and current, thereby decreasing the rate of action potential repolarisation and increasing firing frequency. This mechanism is a major contributor to ERK‑induced nociceptor sensitisation.
    *   **TRPV1**: ERK‑mediated phosphorylation of TRPV1 enhances its response to capsaicin and protons, lowering the thermal threshold and promoting peripheral sensitisation.
*   **Transcriptional changes in DRG neurons**: ERK, through the activation of the transcription factor **CREB** (cAMP response element‑binding protein), induces the expression of pronociceptive genes, including *BDNF*, *Trpv1*, *Scn9a* (Nav1.7), *Scn10a* (Nav1.8), *CGRP* (*Calca*), and *substance P* (*Tac1*). This transcriptional reprogramming sustains the hyperexcitable phenotype of nociceptors.

### 2. Spinal Central Sensitisation and Regulation of Synaptic Plasticity

ERK activation in spinal dorsal horn neurons is a **core biochemical correlate of central sensitisation**.

*   **Time course**: After peripheral nerve injury (e.g., CCI, SNL), ERK1/2 phosphorylation in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn increases within minutes and can remain elevated for weeks, particularly in laminae I–II. The early phase (minutes to hours) is mediated by post‑translational mechanisms and local translation, while the late phase (days to weeks) reflects ERK‑dependent transcription.
*   **NMDA receptor‑mediated ERK activation**: Glutamate released from nociceptor central terminals activates postsynaptic NMDA receptors, leading to calcium influx and subsequent activation of CaMKII and PKA, which in turn stimulate the Ras–ERK pathway via Src family kinases and Pyk2. This provides a direct link between nociceptive input and ERK‑driven plasticity.
*   **CREB‑dependent transcription of pronociceptive genes**: Nuclear ERK phosphorylates **CREB** at Ser¹³³, as well as the transcription factors **Elk‑1** and **c‑Fos**, inducing the expression of a battery of genes that consolidate and maintain central sensitisation: **BDNF**, **COX‑2**, **prodynorphin** (precursor of the pronociceptive peptide dynorphin), **NK1 receptor**, and **c‑fos** itself. The induction of spinal COX‑2 leads to increased prostaglandin E₂ synthesis, which further sensitises neurons and amplifies ERK activation in a feed‑forward loop.
*   **Translation regulation via MNK1–eIF4E**: ERK phosphorylates and activates **MNK1** (MAP kinase‑interacting kinase 1), which in turn phosphorylates the cap‑binding protein **eIF4E**. This enhances the translation of a specific subset of mRNAs, including many pronociceptive mediators (e.g., IL‑6, BDNF) that contain complex 5′‑untranslated regions. Local translation in dendrites of spinal neurons is critical for the rapid synaptic changes underlying ERK‑dependent pain.
*   **Long‑term potentiation (LTP) at C‑fibre synapses**: ERK activation is necessary for the induction and maintenance of spinal LTP, a cellular model of chronic pain. Blocking MEK with U0126 or PD98059 prevents LTP at C‑fibre synapses and the associated mechanical hypersensitivity.

### 3. Glial ERK and the Amplification of Neuroinflammation

ERK is a central mediator of the reactive phenotypes of spinal microglia and astrocytes.

*   **Microglial ERK**: DAMPs (e.g., ATP, HMGB1) and chemokines (CCL2, CX3CL1) activate their respective receptors (P2X4/P2X7, TLR4, CCR2, CX3CR1) on microglia, leading to ERK activation. ERK then drives the transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines, and **BDNF**, via Elk‑1 and AP‑1 transcription factors. Microglial‑derived BDNF is a critical effector, as it diffuses to neurons and downregulates KCC2, causing disinhibition.
*   **Astrocytic ERK**: In the chronic phase of neuropathic pain, ERK is persistently activated in spinal astrocytes. Astrocytic ERK promotes reactive astrogliosis, downregulates glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), and sustains the release of CCL2 and CXCL1, which maintain central sensitisation and recruit additional immune cells.

### 4. ERK as a Molecular Integrator and Pain Memory Keeper

ERK activation is not merely a transient response to injury but a **sustained, maladaptive form of synaptic memory** that parallels the persistence of chronic pain.

*   In the ACC, ERK activation is required for the formation and recall of pain‑related aversive memories. Inhibiting ERK in the ACC with MEK inhibitors reduces pain‑related anxiety and conditioned place aversion.
*   ERK activity in the central amygdala contributes to the emotional‑affective dimension of pain.
*   Because of its role in long‑lasting synaptic plasticity, ERK is often described as a **“molecular memory” kinase** for pain, making it a target for disease‑modifying therapies aimed at erasing or attenuating the maladaptive memory trace of chronic pain.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Pharmacological MEK Inhibitors

The availability of potent and selective MEK1/2 inhibitors has enabled robust validation of the ERK pathway in pain models.

*   **U0126** and **PD98059**: First‑generation MEK inhibitors, widely used as tool compounds. Intrathecal or systemic administration significantly:
    *   Reverses mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in chronic constriction injury (CCI), spinal nerve ligation (SNL), spared nerve injury (SNI), and chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN, e.g., paclitaxel, oxaliplatin) models.
    *   Reduces capsaicin‑induced spontaneous pain and secondary mechanical hypersensitivity.
    *   Prevents the development of opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and tolerance.
    *   Suppresses spinal ERK phosphorylation, CREB activation, and the upregulation of pronociceptive genes (BDNF, COX‑2, c‑fos).
*   **SL327**: A brain‑penetrant MEK inhibitor that, when administered systemically, reduces both sensory and affective components of pain in rodent models.
*   **Trametinib (GSK1120212)** : An FDA‑approved allosteric MEK1/2 inhibitor for BRAF‑mutant melanoma. In preclinical pain models, systemic or intrathecal trametinib reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNL, and diabetic neuropathy, and it attenuates spinal microgliosis. Its clinical use in pain is facilitated by its established human pharmacokinetics and safety data.
*   **Cobimetinib, binimetinib**: Other approved MEK inhibitors that have shown analgesic effects in preclinical models but are less studied for pain.

### 2. Direct ERK Inhibitors and siRNA

*   **Small‑molecule ERK inhibitors** (e.g., **FR180204**, **SCH772984**): Directly bind ERK1/2 and prevent their catalytic activity and nuclear translocation. Intrathecal delivery reduces established mechanical allodynia in CCI models, confirming that the effect of MEK inhibitors is mediated through ERK.
*   **ERK1/2 antisense oligonucleotides or siRNA**: Knockdown of ERK1, ERK2, or both in the spinal cord or DRG produces sustained analgesia, with effects lasting several days after a single injection.

### 3. Genetic Models

*   **Cell‑specific ERK knockout**: Mice with deletion of *Erk2* specifically in Nav1.8‑positive nociceptors exhibit reduced inflammatory and neuropathic pain, including attenuated mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity after SNI. Deletion in CNS neurons (e.g., using CaMKIIα‑Cre) reduces pain affect but has less impact on spinal reflexes.
*   **DUSP1 overexpression**: Viral overexpression of the ERK‑selective phosphatase DUSP1 (MKP‑1) in the spinal cord or DRG reduces ERK phosphorylation, suppresses inflammatory cytokine production, and attenuates neuropathic pain, mimicking the effects of pharmacological ERK inhibition.
*   **MNK1 knockout mice**: *Mnk1*‑/‑ mice show reduced eIF4E phosphorylation in the spinal cord after nerve injury and develop less mechanical allodynia, confirming the importance of ERK‑MNK1‑dependent translational control in pain.

### 4. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Resveratrol**: Inhibits the ERK pathway in spinal microglia and neurons by activating SIRT1, which deacetylates and inactivates the upstream activator c‑Raf. This contributes to its analgesic effects in neuropathic pain.
*   **Curcumin**: Suppresses ERK activation in DRG and spinal cord by inhibiting the upstream kinases MEK and Raf, and by inducing DUSP1.
*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces spinal ERK phosphorylation in CCI and SNI models, partially mediating its analgesic effect.
*   **Cannabidiol (CBD)** : Attenuates ERK phosphorylation in microglia and neurons via CB2 receptor‑ and PPARγ‑dependent mechanisms.

### 5. Downstream Effector Targeting

Given that ERK itself is broadly essential, targeting specific downstream substrates may offer greater selectivity.
*   **CREB inhibitors** (e.g., 666‑15): Reduce CREB‑mediated transcription and have shown analgesic effects in some pain models.
*   **MNK inhibitors** (e.g., eFT508/tomivosertib): Block the translation of pronociceptive proteins without affecting all ERK‑dependent processes. These are in clinical trials for cancer and may be repurposed for pain.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Drug repurposing with approved MEK inhibitors**: Trametinib, cobimetinib, and binimetinib are FDA‑approved for various cancers. Case reports and small retrospective analyses have noted pain reduction in cancer patients receiving trametinib, including those with neuropathic pain due to tumour infiltration or chemotherapy. A prospective clinical trial of trametinib for neuropathic pain is not yet reported but is a logical next step. The oral bioavailability and known toxicity profile of these drugs facilitate rapid clinical translation, but the dose required for analgesia may differ from oncology doses.
*   **Localised delivery strategies**: Because systemic MEK inhibition carries significant toxicity (skin, cardiac, ocular), **intrathecal delivery** via implanted pumps or sustained‑release formulations could achieve high spinal cord concentrations while minimising systemic exposure. This approach is supported by robust preclinical data showing that intrathecal U0126 or trametinib produces potent analgesia without systemic side effects.
*   **Peripherally restricted ERK inhibitors**: Designing MEK or ERK inhibitors that do not cross the blood‑brain barrier could target peripheral nociceptor sensitisation and neurogenic inflammation while preserving CNS ERK function required for learning, memory, and cognition.
*   **Targeting ERK in specific pain endotypes**: Patients with chronic pain who have elevated phosphorylated ERK in skin biopsies, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), or cerebrospinal fluid may constitute a subgroup likely to respond to MEK/ERK‑targeted therapy. p‑ERK could serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for target engagement.
*   **Combination therapy**: Because ERK is a convergent downstream integrator of multiple pronociceptive pathways, its inhibition may synergise with:
    *   **Opioids**: ERK inhibition prevents opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and tolerance, allowing lower opioid doses and sustained efficacy.
    *   **Gabapentinoids**: Co‑targeting the calcium channel α₂δ‑1 subunit (which reduces presynaptic neurotransmitter release) and ERK (which blocks postsynaptic sensitisation) could provide multimodal analgesia.
    *   **Anti‑NGF antibodies**: Blocking the upstream activation of ERK by NGF while simultaneously inhibiting residual ERK activity could be synergistic.
    *   **IL‑6 or TNF‑α inhibitors**: Combining with cytokine blockade could break the positive‑feedback loops that sustain ERK‑dependent neuroinflammation.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Physiological essentiality**: ERK is indispensable for cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival in virtually all tissues. Systemic, chronic MEK inhibition in oncology patients is dose‑limited by predictable toxicities, including acneiform rash (due to inhibition of skin homeostasis), diarrhoea, peripheral oedema, fatigue, and, most seriously, cardiotoxicity (reduced ejection fraction, hypertension) and ocular toxicity (retinal vein occlusion, retinopathy). These toxicities would likely preclude the use of systemic MEK inhibitors for non‑life‑threatening chronic pain conditions at oncologic doses.
*   **Cognitive and memory impairment**: ERK is a core kinase for hippocampal long‑term potentiation, learning, and memory consolidation. Chronic systemic MEK/ERK inhibition could cause significant memory deficits and impair cognitive flexibility, a particularly concerning side effect for patients already suffering from pain‑related cognitive impairment.
*   **Dual role of ERK in pain resolution**: In some contexts, particularly in the late resolution phase of inflammation, ERK can drive anti‑inflammatory and pro‑resolving effects. For example, ERK activation in certain macrophage subtypes promotes the production of IL‑10 and specialised pro‑resolving mediators. Complete and prolonged ERK inhibition could therefore impede endogenous pain resolution and tissue repair.
*   **Isoform specificity and compensatory mechanisms**: ERK1 and ERK2 have overlapping but distinct functions. ERK2 is the dominant isoform in most cells, but ERK1 can partially compensate. MEK inhibitors block both, but the effects of deleting only one isoform are less severe. Drugs that selectively target ERK2 over ERK1 might offer a wider therapeutic window. Additionally, sustained ERK inhibition can lead to compensatory upregulation of the p38 and JNK MAPK pathways, which are themselves pronociceptive, potentially limiting the durability of ERK‑targeted monotherapy.
*   **Cell‑type and temporal specificity**: The role of ERK in pain changes over time and differs between cell types. In the acute phase, ERK in neurons drives protective sensitisation, while in the chronic phase, glial ERK maintains neuroinflammation. An ideal intervention would inhibit glial ERK in the chronic phase while sparing neuronal ERK‑dependent plasticity required for normal sensory function and memory. This is a significant challenge for small‑molecule inhibitors.
*   **Sex differences**: Preclinical data suggest that the contribution of the ERK pathway to neuropathic pain may be sexually dimorphic. In some models, spinal ERK activation and the analgesic efficacy of MEK inhibitors are more pronounced in male rodents, while females rely more on p38 or alternative signalling. This requires careful investigation in human clinical trials.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**: The ERK pathway is a **universal signalling integrator**, positioned downstream of virtually every receptor and upstream of nearly every effector in the pain network. Its activation is triggered by:
    *   **TrkA/TrkB** (NGF, BDNF)
    *   **TNFR** (TNF‑α)
    *   **IL‑1R** (IL‑1β)
    *   **TLR4** (HMGB1, S100A8/A9)
    *   **Chemokine receptors** (CCL2/CCR2, CX3CL1/CX3CR1)
    *   **P2X receptors** (ATP)
    *   **NMDA and AMPA receptors** (glutamate)
    *   **Cav3.2 T‑type calcium channels**
    *   **Notch, Wnt, and TGF‑β signalling pathways** (through non‑canonical routes)
    ERK, in turn, phosphorylates and sensitises **Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Kv4.2, TRPV1**, activates transcription of **BDNF, COX‑2, IL‑6, and chemokines**, and suppresses **KCC2** via BDNF‑TrkB‑dependent pathways. Thus, targeting ERK is a strategy to simultaneously interrupt multiple parallel pain‑driving pathways, but it also carries the highest risk of systemic and central nervous system toxicity.
