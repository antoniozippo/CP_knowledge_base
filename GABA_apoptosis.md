# Involvement of Autophagy and Apoptosis in GABAergic Hyperactive/Hyperexcitable Neurons Due to Increased Intracellular Calcium Accumulation (Probably Inherited by Aberrant Mitochondrial Activity) in Neuropathic Pain

GABAergic inhibitory interneurons in the spinal dorsal horn—particularly fast‑spiking parvalbumin (PV)‑expressing cells—are crucial gatekeepers that prevent tactile input from activating nociceptive circuits. In neuropathic pain, these neurons become pathologically hyperactive, driven by aberrant excitatory input and intrinsic plasticity. This sustained hyperactivity leads to an intracellular calcium (Ca²⁺) overload that originates largely from mitochondrial dysfunction. The resulting Ca²⁺ dyshomeostasis, in turn, triggers two intersecting cell‑death‑related programs—autophagy and apoptosis—that culminate in the functional silencing or outright loss of GABAergic interneurons, removing inhibition and driving mechanical allodynia.

### 🧬 The Vicious Cycle: Hyperactivity, Calcium Overload, and Mitochondrial Failure

A combination of molecular changes after nerve injury forces GABAergic interneurons into a state of excessive firing and Ca²⁺ accumulation.

- **Sources of Pathological Calcium Influx**
    - **Calcium‑permeable AMPA receptors (CP‑AMPARs)** : PV interneurons are enriched in GluA2‑lacking, Ca²⁺‑permeable AMPA receptors. Sustained glutamatergic drive from injured primary afferents and local excitatory interneurons causes massive Ca²⁺ entry through these channels.
    - **NMDA receptors and voltage‑gated Ca²⁺ channels (VGCCs)** : Enhanced NMDA receptor function and upregulation of Cav1.2/Cav1.3 L‑type channels, as well as T‑type channels, further exacerbate the Ca²⁺ load.
    - **TRPV1 and other TRP channels**: Aberrant expression or sensitization of transient receptor potential channels adds to the Ca²⁺ influx.
    - **Release from intracellular stores**: Ca²⁺‑induced Ca²⁺ release via ryanodine receptors (RyRs) and IP₃ receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) amplifies the overload.
    - **Loss of calcium buffers**: Parvalbumin itself, the defining calcium‑binding protein of this population, becomes depleted under sustained hyperactivity, weakening the neuron’s ability to chelate Ca²⁺ and further raising free cytosolic concentrations.

- **Mitochondrial Calcium Mishandling as the Central Hub**
    - Mitochondria normally take up Ca²⁺ through the **mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU)** to buffer cytosolic Ca²⁺ and stimulate ATP synthesis. In hyperactive GABAergic neurons, chronic Ca²⁺ flux overwhelms this system.
    - Excessive mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake leads to:
        - Opening of the **mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP)** , depolarising the inner membrane and collapsing ATP production.
        - Burst production of **reactive oxygen species (ROS)** , which damage mitochondrial DNA, lipids, and proteins, further impairing electron transport chain function.
        - Release of cytochrome *c* and other pro‑apoptotic factors into the cytosol.
    - Failed mitochondria can no longer buffer Ca²⁺ effectively, creating a self‑reinforcing loop: impaired mitochondrial uptake raises cytosolic Ca²⁺ further, which in turn poisons more mitochondria.
    - The phrase “inherited by aberrant mitochondrial activity” likely refers to the fact that the Ca²⁺ overload is initiated or perpetuated by these primary mitochondrial defects, not that it is genetically inherited in every case (though genetic vulnerabilities in mitochondrial quality control exist).

### 🔬 Autophagy Dysregulation: Failed Quality Control Meets Pro‑Death Signalling

Autophagy is the principal cellular pathway for degrading damaged organelles, including mitochondria (mitophagy), and aggregated proteins. In healthy neurons, basal autophagy is essential for survival. In the context of Ca²⁺‑overloaded GABAergic interneurons, autophagy is profoundly dysregulated, contributing to both functional collapse and cell death.

- **Mechanisms Linking Ca²⁺ to Autophagy Failure**
    - **Calpain‑mediated cleavage of autophagy proteins**: Sustained high Ca²⁺ activates the cysteine protease calpain, which cleaves ATG5, Beclin‑1, and other autophagy‑essential proteins, impairing autophagosome formation.
    - **mTORC1 hyperactivation**: Ca²⁺ influx through CP‑AMPARs and VGCCs can stimulate mTORC1, the master suppressor of autophagy initiation. Persistent mTORC1 activity shuts down autophagic turnover.
    - **Impaired autophagic flux**: Even when autophagosomes are formed, their fusion with lysosomes or lysosomal acidification is often inhibited by mitochondrial‑derived ROS and lipid peroxidation products, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional autophagosomes (increased LC3‑II with concomitant p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, indicating a block in degradation).
    - **Defective mitophagy**: The selective removal of damaged mitochondria is compromised. The PINK1/Parkin pathway, which labels depolarised mitochondria for degradation, is often downregulated or overwhelmed, leaving ROS‑producing mitochondria intact.

- **Consequences for GABAergic Interneuron Viability**
    - Accumulation of damaged mitochondria further feeds ROS production and Ca²⁺ leak, perpetuating the hyperexcitable state.
    - Inefficient autophagy can shift the cell toward **autophagy‑dependent cell death** (autosis) when the insult is severe. Excessive autophagosome accumulation can itself trigger apoptosis.
    - The loss of neuroprotective autophagy renders GABAergic neurons more vulnerable to apoptotic cascades.

### 🔬 Apoptotic Pathways: Execution of GABAergic Neuron Loss

Sustained Ca²⁺ overload and mitochondrial failure activate multiple, often parallel, apoptosis pathways that eliminate spinal GABAergic interneurons.

- **Intrinsic (Mitochondrial) Pathway**
    - Cytochrome *c* released through mPTP or BAX/BAK pores activates caspase‑9 and then the executioner caspase‑3. Caspase‑3‑cleaved substrates dismantle the neuron.
    - Mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation is facilitated by the Ca²⁺‑dependent translocation of BID to mitochondria and upregulation of pro‑apoptotic BCL‑2 family members (e.g., BIM, BAX).
    - Apoptosis‑inducing factor (AIF) and endonuclease G are released in a caspase‑independent manner, causing large‑scale DNA fragmentation.

- **ER Stress Pathway**
    - Ca²⁺ depletion from the ER (as a result of RyR/IP₃R‑mediated release) triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR). If unresolved, the UPR activates caspase‑12 (in rodents; caspase‑4/‑5 in humans) and the transcription factor CHOP, both of which drive apoptosis.
    - ER–mitochondria contact sites (MAMs) facilitate direct Ca²⁺ transfer, and their disruption or over‑activation amplifies both UPR and mitochondrial apoptosis.

- **Calpain‑Mediated Necroptosis and Programmed Necrosis**
    - Beyond apoptosis, extreme Ca²⁺ overload and ATP depletion can push neurons into necroptotic death via RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL, or into parthanatos through hyperactivation of PARP‑1 by DNA damage. These non‑apoptotic pathways are also observed in spinal GABAergic neurons after severe nerve injury.

### 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

Directly targeting autophagy, apoptosis, or mitochondrial Ca²⁺ handling in the spinal cord has been shown to influence GABAergic neuron survival and pain behaviour.

- **Anti‑Apoptotic Strategies**
    - **Caspase inhibitors**: Intrathecal delivery of the pan‑caspase inhibitor z‑VAD‑fmk or caspase‑3‑specific inhibitors reduces GABAergic neuron loss in the dorsal horn and attenuates mechanical allodynia in CCI and SNL models.
    - **Calpain inhibition**: The calpain inhibitor MDL‑28170 or calpastatin overexpression preserves GABAergic neurons and reduces pain behaviours, underscoring the role of Ca²⁺‑activated proteolysis.
    - **Overexpression of anti‑apoptotic proteins**: AAV‑mediated delivery of BCL‑2 or BCL‑xL specifically into spinal interneurons protects against nerve‑injury‑induced apoptosis and allodynia.

- **Autophagy Modulation**
    - **Enhancing autophagy**: Early treatment with the mTOR inhibitor **rapamycin** or its analogues can restore autophagic flux, clear damaged mitochondria, reduce ROS, and protect GABAergic neurons from progressing to apoptosis. In some studies, rapamycin reduces mechanical allodynia.
    - **Inhibiting autophagy can be detrimental or beneficial**: Late‑stage blockade of autophagy with 3‑methyladenine (3‑MA) or bafilomycin A1 in hyperactive neurons often exacerbates cell death by preventing clearance of toxic cargo. However, in contexts where autophagy is excessively upregulated and contributes to autosis, transient inhibition may paradoxically prevent cell loss. The effect is highly context‑ and timing‑dependent.
    - **Mitophagy enhancers**: Urolithin A, a natural compound that stimulates PINK1/Parkin‑mediated mitophagy, has been shown to preserve mitochondrial function in spinal neurons and alleviate neuropathic pain in animal models.

- **Mitochondrial‑Targeted Interventions**
    - **MCU blockade**: The MCU inhibitor **Ru360** or knockdown of MCU expression reduces mitochondrial Ca²⁺ accumulation, prevents mPTP opening, and protects GABAergic neurons from apoptosis, reducing pain.
    - **Mitochondrial ROS scavengers**: MitoTEMPO and MitoQ, antioxidants targeted to mitochondria, decrease oxidative damage, restore ATP levels, and reduce GABAergic neuron hyperexcitability and loss.
    - **mPTP inhibitors**: Cyclosporin A (CsA) or the non‑immunosuppressive analogue NIM811 inhibit cyclophilin D‑dependent mPTP opening, preserve mitochondrial membrane potential, and limit both apoptosis and autophagy dysfunction. Intrathecal CsA reduces neuropathic pain in rodent models.

- **Genetic Models**
    - Mice with conditional deletion of *Bax* or *Bak* in spinal interneurons show reduced apoptosis and allodynia.
    - DRP1 (a mitochondrial fission protein) knockout in GABAergic neurons prevents excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and partially rescues them from Ca²⁺‑induced death.

### 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

- **Mitochondrial Stabilizers**: Repurposing drugs like cyclosporin A (mPTP inhibitor) or novel mitochondrial‑targeted antioxidants for neuropathic pain, especially via intrathecal delivery to minimise systemic side effects.
- **Autophagy Enhancers**: Rapamycin and its analogues (rapalogs) are approved for other indications and could be repurposed if the timing of intervention can be optimised to protect, rather than harm, GABAergic neurons.
- **Calpain and Caspase Inhibitors**: While systemic use carries risks, local delivery to the spinal cord is conceivable; some calpain inhibitors have reached clinical trials for neurodegenerative diseases.
- **MCU and Mitochondrial Calcium Modulators**: As drugs that specifically modulate the MCU become available, they may offer a new class of analgesics targeting the root of calcium overload.
- **Biomarkers**: Elevated ROS markers, cytochrome *c* or cleaved caspase‑3 in cerebrospinal fluid, or altered autophagy markers (LC3‑II, p62) in circulating exosomes could indicate ongoing GABAergic neuron distress and help guide therapy.

### ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

- **The Double‑Edged Sword of Autophagy**: Basal autophagy is pro‑survival; excessive or blocked autophagy is pro‑death. The timing, duration, and degree of autophagy modulation are critical, and a therapeutic window may be narrow.
- **Selectivity**: The pathways described are not unique to GABAergic interneurons; systemic modulation of apoptosis, autophagy, or mitochondrial function could affect other neuronal populations and peripheral organs, causing neurotoxicity, impaired cognition, or immunosuppression.
- **Compensatory Inhibitory Circuits**: Not all GABAergic neurons are equally vulnerable. Some subtypes (e.g., those expressing somatostatin) may be less affected, and surviving inhibitory neurons might compensate, making it difficult to predict the net effect of protecting PV neurons alone.
- **Sex Differences**: Male rodents often show greater PV interneuron loss and apoptosis in neuropathic pain models, while females may rely more on functional silencing (KCC2 downregulation) rather than cell death. Therapies targeting apoptosis may thus be more effective in males, but this requires rigorous testing.
- **Interactions with the Wider Pain Network**:
    - **TNF‑α** and **BDNF/TrkB** signalling can directly stimulate mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload and ROS production, feeding into the apoptosis/autophagy axis.
    - **RhoA/ROCK** activation can disrupt mitochondrial trafficking and fission, linking cytoskeletal changes to mitochondrial dysfunction.
    - **HIF1α** drives a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, potentially depriving GABAergic neurons of efficient mitochondrial ATP production and worsening their vulnerability.
    - **Perineuronal net (PNN) loss** exacerbates hyperactivity, pushing more Ca²⁺ into the neuron and accelerating mitochondrial failure.
    Thus, interventions that dampen upstream drivers (e.g., anti‑TNF agents, KCC2 enhancers, ROCK inhibitors) may indirectly protect GABAergic mitochondrial health and reduce the need for direct pro‑survival autophagy/apoptosis modulation.
