# Involvement of Heme Oxygenase‑1 (HO‑1) and Carbon Monoxide (CO) in Neuropathic Pain

Heme oxygenase‑1 (HO‑1, encoded by the *HMOX1* gene) is a stress‑inducible microsomal enzyme that catalyses the rate‑limiting step in heme degradation, generating equimolar amounts of **biliverdin** (which is rapidly reduced to the potent antioxidant **bilirubin**), **carbon monoxide (CO)** , and **free ferrous iron (Fe²⁺)** . In the context of neuropathic pain, HO‑1 functions primarily as an **anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective** system that is rapidly upregulated in the spinal cord, dorsal root ganglia (DRG), and peripheral nerves in response to oxidative stress, pro‑inflammatory cytokines, and electrophilic compounds. Its induction—mainly through the activation of the transcription factor **nuclear factor erythroid 2‑related factor 2 (Nrf2)** —represents a key endogenous defence mechanism that counteracts the mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and central sensitisation that drive chronic pain. The gaseous product **CO**, acting at nanomolar concentrations as a signalling molecule, mediates many of the analgesic effects of HO‑1 by activating soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) to produce cGMP, opening calcium‑activated potassium channels, inhibiting NADPH oxidase, and suppressing pro‑inflammatory cytokine production. Pharmacological induction of HO‑1 (with hemin, cobalt protoporphyrin, dimethyl fumarate, resveratrol, curcumin) or the administration of exogenous CO (via CO‑releasing molecules, CORMs) consistently alleviates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and neuroinflammation across diverse preclinical models of traumatic, metabolic, and chemotherapeutic neuropathy. The clinical development of HO‑1/CO‑based therapies leverages the approved Nrf2 activator **dimethyl fumarate** (Tecfidera) and inhaled CO in clinical trials for inflammatory conditions, positioning this pathway as a promising avenue for non‑opioid analgesic development, provided that the delicate balance between cytoprotection and potential toxicity (iron overload, CO poisoning) is carefully managed.

## 🧬 The HO‑1/CO System: Gene, Enzyme, and Products

*   **Gene and isoforms**: The *HMOX* gene family comprises two functional isoforms:
    *   **HO‑1** (*HMOX1*, 32 kDa): The inducible isoform, transcriptionally regulated by a wide array of stressors and protective signals. It is the primary mediator of the adaptive response to oxidative and inflammatory injury.
    *   **HO‑2** (*HMOX2*, 36 kDa): The constitutively expressed isoform, which maintains basal CO production and is involved in physiological neuromodulation. HO‑2 is not typically a target for pain therapy but provides tonic anti‑nociceptive tone in some contexts.
*   **Enzymatic reaction**: HO catalyses the oxidative cleavage of the heme porphyrin ring at the α‑methene bridge, consuming three molecules of oxygen and seven electrons donated by NADPH‑cytochrome P450 reductase. The reaction yields:
    *   **Biliverdin**: Converted to **bilirubin** by biliverdin reductase. Bilirubin is a potent peroxyl radical scavenger and chain‑breaking antioxidant that protects membrane lipids from peroxidation.
    *   **Carbon monoxide (CO)** : A diatomic gas that, at physiological concentrations (nanomolar to low micromolar), functions as an endogenous signalling molecule with vasodilatory, anti‑inflammatory, anti‑apoptotic, and neurotransmitter‑like properties. Unlike nitric oxide (NO), CO does not react with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, making it a relatively “clean” gasotransmitter under physiological conditions.
    *   **Ferrous iron (Fe²⁺)** : Free iron is pro‑oxidative and potentially toxic. HO‑1 concomitantly upregulates the iron‑sequestering protein **ferritin** (via an iron‑responsive element/iron‑regulatory protein mechanism), which safely stores the released iron and contributes an additional layer of cytoprotection.
*   **Regulation of HO‑1 expression**: The *HMOX1* promoter contains multiple stress‑responsive elements, most notably **antioxidant response elements (AREs)** that are bound by the transcription factor **Nrf2**. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm by Keap1 and targeted for proteasomal degradation. Electrophiles, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and certain drugs (dimethyl fumarate, sulforaphane) modify Keap1 cysteine residues, releasing Nrf2 to translocate to the nucleus, where it heterodimerises with small Maf proteins and binds to AREs to drive *HMOX1* transcription. Additional regulatory elements include binding sites for AP‑1, NF‑κB, and STAT3, allowing fine‑tuning by inflammatory and stress signals.
*   **CO signalling mechanisms**: Exogenous or HO‑derived CO diffuses rapidly through membranes and binds to the heme moiety of target proteins, modulating their activity:
    *   **Soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC)** : At low nanomolar concentrations, CO binds to the heme of sGC, stimulating the production of cGMP. The cGMP–PKG pathway mediates many of the vasodilatory and anti‑nociceptive effects of CO.
    *   **Calcium‑activated potassium channels (BK<sub>Ca</sub>, IK<sub>Ca</sub>)** : CO directly activates large‑conductance BK<sub>Ca</sub> channels in vascular smooth muscle and neurons, leading to membrane hyperpolarisation and reduced excitability.
    *   **NADPH oxidase (NOX)** : CO inhibits NOX2 and NOX4, reducing the production of superoxide anion and thereby limiting peroxynitrite formation and oxidative damage.
    *   **Mitogen‑activated protein kinases (MAPKs)** : CO modulates the activity of p38 MAPK, ERK, and JNK, generally suppressing pro‑inflammatory signalling while preserving cytoprotective pathways.
    *   **Mitochondria**: Low concentrations of CO bind to cytochrome *c* oxidase (Complex IV), modulating mitochondrial respiration, reducing excessive ROS production, and triggering mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC‑1α.
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution of HO‑1**: Under basal conditions, HO‑1 is expressed at very low levels in most tissues, with the notable exception of the spleen (where it recycles heme from senescent red blood cells). In the nervous system, HO‑1 is rapidly and robustly induced in:
    *   **Spinal microglia and astrocytes**: HO‑1 induction in glial cells is a hallmark of the adaptive response to nerve injury. It promotes the resolution of neuroinflammation and a shift towards an M2‑like, anti‑inflammatory phenotype.
    *   **DRG neurons and satellite glial cells**: HO‑1 is upregulated in primary sensory neurons and their supporting cells after nerve injury, where it protects against oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction.
    *   **Peripheral nerve**: Schwann cells and infiltrating macrophages express HO‑1 in injured peripheral nerves, facilitating Wallerian degeneration, myelin clearance, and axonal regeneration.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: HO‑1 is induced in the hippocampus, cortex, and amygdala in chronic pain models, where it may protect against cognitive and affective comorbidities.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of HO‑1/CO in Neuropathic Pain

The HO‑1/CO system exerts its anti‑nociceptive effects through multiple, complementary mechanisms that collectively resolve neuroinflammation, reduce oxidative stress, restore mitochondrial homeostasis, protect GABAergic inhibition, and directly dampen neuronal excitability.

### 1. Activation of the Nrf2/HO‑1 Axis and Resolution of Neuroinflammation

The Nrf2/HO‑1 pathway is the principal endogenous defence system against oxidative and inflammatory injury in the nervous system. In neuropathic pain, this axis is initially induced as a compensatory response, but its magnitude is often insufficient to fully counteract the severity of the injury. Pharmacological activation of Nrf2 or direct upregulation of HO‑1 powerfully suppresses neuroinflammation:

*   **HO‑1 induction in glial cells**: Nrf2‑driven HO‑1 expression in spinal microglia and astrocytes reduces the production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and cyclooxygenase‑2 (COX‑2). Simultaneously, it promotes the synthesis of the anti‑inflammatory cytokine IL‑10 and the pro‑resolving lipid mediator lipoxin A₄.
*   **Inhibition of NF‑κB**: HO‑1‑derived CO can directly inhibit NF‑κB by blocking the phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, preventing NF‑κB nuclear translocation and the transcription of its target genes. Additionally, bilirubin can scavenge ROS that would otherwise activate the IKK complex.
*   **Microglial M1‑to‑M2 polarisation**: Nrf2/HO‑1 activation shifts microglia from a pro‑inflammatory M1 phenotype (characterised by iNOS, TNF‑α, and ROS production) to an anti‑inflammatory, neuroprotective M2 phenotype (characterised by arginase‑1, IL‑10, and CD206 expression). This phenotypic switch is a central mechanism of pain resolution.
*   **Restoration of autophagic flux**: CO and bilirubin can restore impaired autophagy by reducing oxidative stress‑mediated inhibition of autophagosome‑lysosome fusion and by promoting the transcription of autophagy genes (e.g., via TFEB). Improved autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates limits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL‑1β release.

### 2. Direct Neuronal Anti‑Nociceptive Effects of CO

*   **Activation of BK<sub>Ca</sub> channels and membrane hyperpolarisation**: CO directly binds to and activates large‑conductance calcium‑activated potassium (BK<sub>Ca</sub>) channels on DRG neurons and spinal dorsal horn neurons. The resulting potassium efflux hyperpolarises the membrane, reducing action potential firing and nociceptor excitability. This effect is rapid and independent of transcription.
*   **Inhibition of voltage‑gated sodium and calcium channels**: CO can S‑nitrosylate (or carbonylate) specific cysteine residues on Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Ca<sub>V</sub>3.2 channels, reducing their activity and thereby dampening nociceptor hyperexcitability and ectopic firing.
*   **Reduction of TRPV1 sensitisation**: By inhibiting NADPH oxidase‑derived ROS and the associated oxidative sensitisation of TRPV1, CO indirectly reduces the thermal and chemical responsiveness of nociceptors.
*   **Preservation of KCC2 and GABAergic inhibition**: The Nrf2/HO‑1 pathway suppresses the glial production of BDNF, a major driver of KCC2 downregulation. CO itself can also directly inhibit the PKCε‑ and Src‑mediated phosphorylation of KCC2 that triggers its internalisation. By maintaining KCC2 function, HO‑1/CO preserves the inhibitory efficacy of GABA and glycine, counteracting the disinhibition that underlies mechanical allodynia.

### 3. Mitochondrial Protection and Bioenergetics

*   **Mitochondrial biogenesis**: CO, at low concentrations, binds to cytochrome *c* oxidase, triggering a mild, transient increase in mitochondrial ROS that acts as a signalling molecule to activate **PGC‑1α** and **TFAM**, driving mitochondrial biogenesis. This restores the mitochondrial network in injured neurons and glial cells, improving ATP production and calcium buffering capacity.
*   **Inhibition of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP)** : CO and bilirubin both inhibit the opening of the mPTP, preventing mitochondrial membrane depolarisation, the release of cytochrome *c*, and the initiation of intrinsic apoptosis. This directly protects GABAergic interneurons from the calcium‑overload‑induced apoptosis that contributes to loss of inhibition.
*   **Reduction of mitochondrial ROS**: HO‑1‑derived bilirubin is a potent, chain‑breaking lipophilic antioxidant that intercepts lipid peroxyl radicals within mitochondrial membranes. By reducing mitochondrial ROS, the HO‑1 system prevents the oxidative sensitisation of TRPV1/TRPA1 and the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

### 4. Promotion of Peripheral Nerve Repair and Resolution of Neuroinflammation

*   **Schwann cell function and remyelination**: In the injured peripheral nerve, HO‑1 induction in Schwann cells facilitates the phagocytosis and clearance of myelin debris, promotes the expression of neurotrophic factors (NGF, BDNF), and supports axonal regeneration and remyelination. CO contributes to this by modulating the M1‑to‑M2 shift in infiltrating macrophages, which is essential for the transition from the inflammatory to the reparative phase of Wallerian degeneration.
*   **Inhibition of perineural fibrosis**: CO can suppress TGF‑β‑induced myofibroblast differentiation and collagen deposition, potentially limiting the development of painful perineural fibrosis in the chronic phase after nerve injury.

### 5. Supraspinal HO‑1/CO and Pain Comorbidities

*   **Hippocampus and prefrontal cortex**: Induction of HO‑1 in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) in chronic pain models is associated with improved cognitive performance and reduced anxiety‑ and depression‑like behaviours. The mechanisms include reduced neuroinflammation, protection of neurogenesis, and preservation of long‑term potentiation.
*   **Periaqueductal grey (PAG)** : HO‑2‑derived CO in the PAG tonically modulates descending pain pathways. Enhancing CO production in the PAG can activate descending inhibition, providing an additional supraspinal analgesic mechanism.

### 6. Interaction with the Endocannabinoid System

There is emerging crosstalk between the HO‑1/CO system and the endocannabinoid system. CO can modulate the activity of fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), the enzyme that degrades anandamide, potentially increasing local endocannabinoid tone. Some of the analgesic effects of HO‑1 inducers may be partially mediated by this interaction.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Pharmacological Induction of HO‑1

*   **Hemin (heme arginate)** : Hemin is a potent inducer of HO‑1. Systemic or intrathecal administration of hemin in models of chronic constriction injury (CCI), spinal nerve ligation (SNL), diabetic neuropathy, and chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) consistently:
    *   Attenuates mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in a dose‑ and time‑dependent manner.
    *   Increases spinal HO‑1 protein and activity, accompanied by reduced markers of oxidative stress (malondialdehyde, protein carbonyls) and nitrosative stress (nitrotyrosine).
    *   Suppresses spinal NF‑κB activation and reduces the expression of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, iNOS, and COX‑2.
    *   Restores the activity of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase).
    *   The analgesic effects of hemin are **blocked by the HO‑1 inhibitor zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPP IX)** , confirming that HO‑1 induction is the primary mechanism.
*   **Cobalt protoporphyrin IX (CoPP)** : A synthetic metalloporphyrin that is one of the most potent and long‑lasting inducers of HO‑1. CoPP administration produces profound analgesia in neuropathic pain models, with effects persisting for days after a single injection.
*   **Dimethyl fumarate (DMF, Tecfidera)** : DMF is an electrophilic Nrf2 activator that potently induces HO‑1. Systemic DMF treatment reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNL, and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE)‑associated pain models. The analgesic effects are associated with increased spinal Nrf2 nuclear translocation, HO‑1 expression, reduced microgliosis, and suppressed NF‑κB signalling. The effects are abolished in Nrf2‑knockout mice or by co‑administration of the HO‑1 inhibitor SnPP.
*   **Sulforaphane**: An isothiocyanate derived from cruciferous vegetables that is a potent Nrf2 activator. Sulforaphane administration increases spinal HO‑1 expression and reduces neuropathic pain in rodent models of diabetes and traumatic nerve injury.
*   **Resveratrol, curcumin, and cannabidiol**: These natural compounds activate the Nrf2/HO‑1 axis, among other targets, and their analgesic effects in neuropathic pain are partially dependent on HO‑1 induction.

### 2. Administration of CO or CO‑Releasing Molecules (CORMs)

Direct delivery of CO, bypassing the need for HO‑1 enzymatic activity, has been achieved using CO‑releasing molecules (CORMs) and inhaled CO.

*   **CORM‑2** (tricarbonyldichlororuthenium(II) dimer) and **CORM‑3** (a water‑soluble, ruthenium‑based CORM): Intraperitoneal or intrathecal administration of CORM‑2 or CORM‑3 in CCI, SNI, and spinal cord injury models:
    *   Significantly reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.
    *   Suppresses spinal microglial and astrocytic activation, reduces iNOS and COX‑2 expression, and normalises the pro‑/anti‑inflammatory cytokine balance.
    *   Activates the cGMP–PKG pathway and opens BK<sub>Ca</sub> channels, effects that are reversed by the sGC inhibitor ODQ or the BK<sub>Ca</sub> blocker iberiotoxin.
    *   Improves mitochondrial respiration and reduces mitochondrial ROS production.
*   **CORM‑A1**: A slow‑releasing, boranocarbonate‑based CORM that more closely mimics the sustained, low‑level CO production by HO‑1. It has shown analgesic efficacy in inflammatory and neuropathic pain models.
*   **Inhaled CO gas (250–500 ppm)** : Inhalation of low concentrations of CO has been investigated in preclinical models of spinal cord injury and peripheral nerve injury, with results showing reduced neuropathic pain and improved functional recovery.

### 3. Genetic Models

*   **HO‑1 knockout mice (*Hmox1*‑/‑)** : Mice lacking HO‑1 exhibit exacerbated neuropathic pain after nerve injury, with enhanced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, increased spinal microgliosis and astrogliosis, elevated pro‑inflammatory cytokine levels, and more severe oxidative and nitrosative damage. This demonstrates that endogenous HO‑1 provides tonic protection against pain pathology.
*   **HO‑1 overexpressing transgenic mice**: Mice with conditional or global overexpression of HO‑1 show attenuated neuropathic pain and reduced neuroinflammation after nerve injury, confirming that enhancing the HO‑1 pathway is analgesic.
*   **Nrf2 knockout mice**: Mice lacking Nrf2 fail to upregulate HO‑1 in response to injury or pharmacological inducers (e.g., DMF) and exhibit increased pain sensitivity, supporting the upstream role of Nrf2 in the protective HO‑1 response.

### 4. Pharmacological Inhibition of HO‑1

*   **Zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPP IX)** and **tin protoporphyrin IX (SnPP IX)** : These competitive inhibitors of HO activity, when administered alone, **exacerbate or unmask neuropathic pain behaviours** in some models, confirming the tonic protective role of endogenous HO‑1. They also abolish the analgesic effects of HO‑1 inducers (hemin, CoPP, DMF) and of CORMs (partially, for effects that require downstream HO‑1 products beyond CO).

### 5. Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Electroacupuncture**: Enhances the Nrf2/HO‑1 pathway in the spinal cord and DRG, contributing to its analgesic and anti‑inflammatory effects in neuropathic pain models.
*   **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)** : Increases spinal HO‑1 expression and CO production, which may be one of the mechanisms by which HBOT alleviates neuropathic pain in some models.
*   **Exercise and caloric restriction**: Both activate Nrf2 and upregulate HO‑1, and have been associated with reduced pain sensitivity in preclinical models.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Dimethyl fumarate (DMF, Tecfidera)**: DMF is FDA‑approved for the treatment of relapsing‑remitting multiple sclerosis (MS). In MS patients, DMF significantly reduces pain—including neuropathic pain—independently of its effects on disease activity (relapse rate, MRI lesions). This clinical evidence directly supports the analgesic potential of Nrf2/HO‑1 activation. A clinical trial of DMF in painful diabetic neuropathy or other neuropathic pain conditions is a logical next step.
*   **Inhaled CO for pain**: Inhaled CO is in Phase I/II clinical trials for various inflammatory conditions (e.g., acute respiratory distress syndrome, sickle cell disease). Its established safety at carefully controlled low doses and its potent analgesic effects in preclinical models support its investigation for neuropathic pain, particularly in conditions where a short‑term, intensive anti‑inflammatory effect is desirable (e.g., acute spinal cord injury, post‑surgical neuropathic pain).
*   **Oral CO‑releasing molecules (CORMs)** : The development of orally bioavailable, safe, and tissue‑targeted CORMs is an active area of pharmaceutical research. An ideal CORM for chronic pain would produce a sustained, low‑level CO release, preferentially targeting the central nervous system or inflamed peripheral nerves, without producing systemic carboxyhaemoglobin levels that impair oxygen delivery. Several next‑generation CORMs are in preclinical development.
*   **Nrf2 activators (beyond DMF)** : Sulforaphane (oral, derived from broccoli sprout extract) is widely available as a dietary supplement and has shown preliminary evidence of pain relief in small clinical studies of chronic pain conditions, though large, placebo‑controlled trials are lacking. Bardoxolone methyl and other synthetic Nrf2 activators are in clinical trials for other indications and could be repurposed for pain if CNS penetration and safety allow.
*   **Biomarker potential**: Bilirubin (the end‑product of heme degradation by HO‑1) is readily measurable in serum and has been associated with lower pain sensitivity in some clinical studies. HO‑1 protein levels in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or the ratio of carboxyhaemoglobin to met‑haemoglobin could serve as pharmacodynamic biomarkers for target engagement in clinical trials of HO‑1/CO‑based therapies.
*   **Combination strategies**: The HO‑1/CO system is a master anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant hub that can synergise with:
    *   **Anti‑TNF‑α or anti‑IL‑6 biologics**: HO‑1 induction can potentiate the anti‑inflammatory effects of these therapies while countering their potential to exacerbate oxidative stress.
    *   **KCC2 enhancers**: By preserving KCC2, CO and bilirubin complement the direct actions of KCC2 activators to restore GABAergic inhibition.
    *   **Opioids**: HO‑1 activation can prevent and reverse opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and tolerance, allowing sustained analgesia at lower opioid doses.
    *   **COX‑2 inhibitors**: The combination of an HO‑1 inducer (which suppresses COX‑2 and iNOS) with a low‑dose COX‑2 inhibitor may provide broad‑spectrum analgesia with reduced gastrointestinal and cardiovascular side effects.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **CO toxicity and dosing window**: CO binds to haemoglobin with ~240 times greater affinity than oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb). Elevated COHb levels (>10‑15%) impair oxygen delivery, causing tissue hypoxia, headache, dizziness, and, at high levels, death. The therapeutic window for CO‑based therapies is narrow, and dosing must be carefully controlled to avoid systemic toxicity. Tissue‑targeted CORMs that release CO locally, or inhaled CO at very low concentrations (250 ppm), aim to exploit the signalling benefits of CO while avoiding systemic hypoxia.
*   **Iron release and Fenton chemistry**: HO‑1 activity releases free Fe²⁺, which, if not properly sequestered by ferritin, can catalyse the Fenton reaction, generating highly toxic hydroxyl radicals. The concomitant upregulation of ferritin by HO‑1 normally mitigates this risk, but under conditions of severe or sustained HO‑1 induction, iron‑dependent oxidative injury can occur. Therapeutic strategies must ensure that iron‑handling capacity keeps pace with heme degradation.
*   **HO‑1 and cancer**: HO‑1 is overexpressed in many cancers and is associated with tumour progression, angiogenesis, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Chronically high HO‑1 activity in a patient with an undiagnosed malignancy could theoretically promote tumour growth. This is a concern for long‑term, systemic HO‑1 induction, though it is less relevant for short‑term or localised interventions for pain.
*   **Dual role in acute vs. chronic phase**: While HO‑1 is protective and analgesic in the acute and subacute phases of nerve injury, its role in the very late, chronic phase of established neuropathic pain is less well defined. Some studies suggest that the endogenous HO‑1 response may become exhausted or that sustained high HO‑1 levels could contribute to the pathology, although this is not a consistent finding.
*   **Redundancy with other antioxidant systems**: The Nrf2/HO‑1 axis is part of a network of antioxidant and anti‑inflammatory pathways, including the thioredoxin system, glutathione synthesis, and superoxide dismutases. Compensatory upregulation of these systems can partially mask the effects of HO‑1 deficiency or inhibition.
*   **Sex differences**: HO‑1 expression and induction may be influenced by sex hormones (oestrogen, in particular, can upregulate HO‑1). Preclinical studies have not always rigorously evaluated sex as a biological variable for HO‑1/CO‑mediated analgesia, and potential sex‑specific differences in efficacy and safety warrant investigation.
*   **Interactions with the wider pain network**: The HO‑1/CO system intersects with many of the targets discussed in this series:
    *   **Nrf2**: HO‑1 is the primary cytoprotective effector of the Nrf2 pathway, linking it directly to PPARγ and other antioxidant systems.
    *   **NF‑κB and NLRP3**: CO and bilirubin suppress NF‑κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, providing a broad anti‑inflammatory effect.
    *   **KCC2**: CO and bilirubin preserve KCC2 function by inhibiting BDNF release and PKC‑mediated KCC2 phosphorylation, maintaining GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition.
    *   **BDNF‑TrkB**: HO‑1 activation reduces microglial BDNF production, attenuating the BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2 disinhibition cascade.
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: HO‑1 induction suppresses the production of these cytokines, while CO directly inhibits their signalling.
    *   **iNOS and NO**: HO‑1/CO reciprocally regulates iNOS/NO; CO can inhibit iNOS expression and activity, while NO can induce HO‑1.
    *   **mTOR and autophagy**: CO and bilirubin restore autophagic flux by inhibiting mTORC1 (via AMPK activation) and by reducing oxidative stress‑mediated autophagy impairment.
    *   **Wnt/β‑catenin**: HO‑1/CO can inhibit Wnt/β‑catenin signalling, reducing the expression of pronociceptive genes.
    *   **TRPV1 and Nav channels**: CO suppresses the sensitisation of TRPV1 and voltage‑gated sodium channels via antioxidant effects and direct protein modification.
    *   **TSPO**: Both TSPO and HO‑1 are mitochondrial‑protective systems that converge on maintaining mitochondrial integrity, reducing mPTP opening, and preventing pyroptosis.
    *   **CB2 receptor**: HO‑1 induction is one of the downstream effectors of CB2 receptor‑mediated anti‑inflammation, and CB2 agonists can upregulate HO‑1.
