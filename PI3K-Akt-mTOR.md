# Involvement of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR Pathway in Neuropathic Pain

The phosphoinositide 3‑kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling axis is a highly conserved intracellular cascade that governs cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, survival, and synaptic plasticity. In the adult nervous system, this pathway is largely quiescent but is rapidly and persistently reactivated in nociceptive circuits following peripheral nerve injury. Its hyperactivation in primary sensory neurons, spinal microglia and astrocytes, and supraspinal pain‑processing regions is now recognized as a core driver of neuropathic pain. The principal effector of this cascade, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), promotes the translation of a specific set of pronociceptive mRNAs, suppresses the clearance of damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates by blocking autophagy, and drives the pro‑inflammatory phenotype of glial cells. Pharmacological inhibition of PI3K, Akt, or mTOR with repurposed clinical drugs—most notably rapamycin (sirolimus) and its analogues—consistently attenuates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and neuroinflammatory markers across diverse preclinical models of traumatic, metabolic, and chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy. These findings position the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway as a high‑value therapeutic target, but the profound physiological roles of mTOR in systemic metabolism, immune function, and cognitive processes demand carefully tailored, locally delivered, or isoform‑specific interventions.

## 🧬 The PI3K/Akt/mTOR Pathway: Components and Normal Function

- **Core signalling cascade**:
    1. **Class I PI3K**: Activated by receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs: TrkA, TrkB, insulin receptor) or G‑protein‑coupled receptors, PI3K phosphorylates the membrane phospholipid phosphatidylinositol‑4,5‑bisphosphate (PIP₂) to generate phosphatidylinositol‑3,4,5‑trisphosphate (PIP₃). The p110α/p110β catalytic and p85/p101 regulatory subunits define distinct PI3K isoforms; **PI3Kγ and PI3Kδ** are enriched in immune cells and microglia, while **PI3Kα and PI3Kβ** are broadly expressed.
    2. **Akt (PKB)** : PIP₃ recruits Akt to the plasma membrane, where it is phosphorylated at Thr³⁰⁸ by **PDK1** (phosphoinositide‑dependent kinase 1) and at Ser⁴⁷³ by **mTORC2** (mTOR complex 2). Fully activated Akt phosphorylates a vast array of substrates to promote cell survival, growth, and metabolism.
    3. **mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1)** : Akt phosphorylates and inactivates the **TSC1/TSC2** (tuberous sclerosis complex) tumour suppressor complex, relieving its inhibition of the small GTPase **Rheb**. Rheb directly activates mTORC1, a multiprotein assembly comprising mTOR, Raptor, mLST8, PRAS40, and Deptor. mTORC1 phosphorylates:
        - **S6 kinase 1 (S6K1)** and **S6K2**, which promote ribosome biogenesis and mRNA translation.
        - **4E‑binding protein (4E‑BP)** , releasing its inhibition of the cap‑dependent translation initiation factor eIF4E.
        - **ULK1** (Unc‑51‑like autophagy‑activating kinase 1), inhibiting autophagy induction.
        - **TFEB**, blocking lysosomal biogenesis.
    4. **mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2)** : Contains mTOR, Rictor, mSIN1, Protor, and mLST8. It is activated downstream of growth factors and PI3K, but is resistant to acute rapamycin treatment. mTORC2 phosphorylates Akt at Ser⁴⁷³, enhancing its full activation, and also regulates cytoskeletal organisation via PKCα and Rho GTPases.
- **Negative regulators**:
    - **PTEN** (phosphatase and tensin homologue): Dephosphorylates PIP₃ back to PIP₂, terminating PI3K signalling. PTEN is often downregulated or lost after nerve injury, contributing to sustained pathway activation.
    - **AMP‑activated protein kinase (AMPK)** : Phosphorylates and activates TSC2, and directly phosphorylates Raptor to inhibit mTORC1, thereby coupling cellular energy status to growth.
    - **REDD1**: Induced by hypoxia and DNA damage, it releases TSC2 from its inhibitor, inhibiting mTORC1.
- **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    - **DRG neurons**: mTORC1 and its upstream regulators are highly expressed in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors. Following nerve injury, PI3K/Akt/mTOR is persistently activated in DRG neurons, driving the translation of ion channels and neuropeptides.
    - **Spinal dorsal horn**: mTORC1 is present in postsynaptic neurons, presynaptic terminals, and glial cells. Its activation is a hallmark of central sensitisation.
    - **Microglia and astrocytes**: mTORC1 activity is markedly increased in activated microglia and reactive astrocytes, where it orchestrates metabolic reprogramming and pro‑inflammatory cytokine synthesis.
    - **Supraspinal regions**: mTORC1 is activated in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), hippocampus, and amygdala in chronic pain, contributing to cognitive deficits, anxiety, and depression.
- **Physiological functions**: The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is essential for embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, immune cell proliferation and differentiation, learning and memory (via synaptic protein synthesis), and the regulation of organismal metabolism. Its activity is tightly controlled and is primarily engaged by growth factors and nutrients.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of PI3K/Akt/mTOR Dysregulation in Neuropathic Pain

Peripheral nerve injury leads to a chronic, maladaptive overactivation of mTORC1 at multiple nodes of the pain pathway, converting a tightly regulated anabolic and survival pathway into a driver of hyperexcitability, neuroinflammation, and impaired quality control.

### 1. Neuronal mTORC1 Hyperactivation and Translation of Pronociceptive Proteins

This is the most direct and extensively characterised mechanism by which mTOR promotes neuropathic pain.

- **Upstream activation**: Injury‑induced release of **NGF**, **BDNF**, and **insulin‑like growth factor‑1 (IGF‑1)** from damaged tissue, Schwann cells, and glial cells activates their cognate receptors (TrkA, TrkB, IGFR) on nociceptors, which engage the PI3K/Akt pathway. Additionally, pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β) can activate PI3K via their receptors. Increased intracellular Ca²⁺ also activates Akt via Ca²⁺/calmodulin‑dependent protein kinase kinase (CaMKK) and PDK1.
- **Loss of PTEN**: Nerve injury reduces the expression and activity of the lipid phosphatase PTEN in DRG and spinal cord neurons, removing a critical brake on PI3K signalling and resulting in sustained PIP₃ accumulation and Akt activation.
- **Enhanced cap‑dependent translation**: mTORC1‑mediated phosphorylation of **4E‑BP** releases eIF4E, allowing the assembly of the eIF4F cap‑binding complex. This selectively increases the translation of a subset of mRNAs with complex 5′‑untranslated regions, many of which encode pronociceptive proteins, including:
    - **Nav1.7** (*SCN9A*), **Nav1.8** (*SCN10A*), and **Nav1.9** (*SCN11A*) voltage‑gated sodium channels
    - **TRPV1**, **TRPA1** transient receptor potential channels
    - **CGRP** (*CALCA*) and **substance P** (*TAC1*)
    - **BDNF** itself, creating a positive‑feedback loop via TrkB
    - **Ca<sub>V</sub>α2δ‑1** (*CACNA2D1*), the gabapentin‑sensitive calcium channel subunit
- **Increased neuronal excitability and ectopic firing**: The net result of this translational reprogramming is a profound increase in nociceptor intrinsic excitability, reduced action potential threshold, enhanced repetitive firing, and spontaneous ectopic discharges that drive spontaneous pain and stimulus‑evoked hypersensitivity.
- **Synaptic plasticity and LTP**: In the spinal dorsal horn, mTORC1‑dependent translation of AMPA receptor subunits (GluA1) and NMDA receptor subunits (GluN2B) at synaptic sites contributes to the long‑term potentiation (LTP) at C‑fibre synapses, a cellular substrate of central sensitisation.

### 2. mTORC1‑Mediated Suppression of Autophagy and Accumulation of Damaged Mitochondria

A critical consequence of mTORC1 hyperactivation is the potent inhibition of autophagy, a lysosome‑dependent degradation pathway that clears damaged organelles, protein aggregates, and intracellular pathogens.

- **Phosphorylation of ULK1 and ATG13**: Active mTORC1 directly phosphorylates and inactivates the **ULK1** kinase complex (ULK1‑ATG13‑FIP200), the initiating kinase for autophagosome formation. It also phosphorylates and inhibits **TFEB**, a transcription factor that drives the expression of autophagy and lysosomal genes.
- **Blocked autophagic flux**: In DRG neurons and spinal dorsal horn cells after nerve injury, autophagic markers indicate a block in flux: **LC3‑II** accumulates, but **p62/SQSTM1** also accumulates, indicating that autophagosomes are formed but not efficiently degraded.
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production**: The failure to clear damaged mitochondria (mitophagy) results in the accumulation of depolarised, ROS‑producing mitochondria. Mitochondrial ROS oxidise and sensitise TRPV1, TRPA1, and Nav channels, further enhancing nociceptor excitability, and also activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, linking mTOR‑driven autophagy failure to neuroinflammation.
- **Calpain‑mediated KCC2 cleavage**: Impaired autophagy leads to sustained intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and calpain activation, which cleaves the potassium‑chloride cotransporter **KCC2**, disrupting GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition and contributing to mechanical allodynia.

### 3. mTORC1 in Microglial and Astrocytic Activation

- **Microglial M1 polarisation**: mTORC1 is a key driver of the metabolic switch that supports the M1 pro‑inflammatory phenotype in microglia. It promotes glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway, providing the biosynthetic precursors needed for cytokine production. Inhibition of mTORC1 with rapamycin shifts microglia towards an M2, anti‑inflammatory, pro‑resolving phenotype.
- **Astrogliosis**: In reactive astrocytes, mTORC1 activity sustains the expression of GFAP, the release of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1), and the downregulation of glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), thereby maintaining central sensitisation.
- **Glial BDNF release**: mTORC1‑activated microglia and astrocytes release BDNF, which acts on neuronal TrkB to downregulate KCC2 and produce disinhibition—a canonical pronociceptive mechanism.

### 4. The AMPK–mTOR Axis as a Pain Rheostat

AMPK is a cellular energy sensor that becomes activated when the AMP:ATP ratio rises (energy stress). AMPK directly inhibits mTORC1 by phosphorylating TSC2 and Raptor. In neuropathic pain, AMPK activity is often suppressed, releasing mTORC1 from tonic inhibition. Pharmacological activation of AMPK (e.g., with metformin, AICAR, or resveratrol) reduces mTORC1 activity, restores autophagy, and attenuates neuropathic pain in multiple models. Conversely, AMPK knockout or inhibition exacerbates mTORC1‑driven pain hypersensitivity.

### 5. Supraspinal mTOR and Pain Comorbidities

- **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** and **hippocampus**: mTORC1 is critical for the formation of pain‑related aversive memories and for the cognitive deficits associated with chronic pain. Rapamycin infused into the ACC or hippocampus reduces pain‑related anxiety and improves memory performance in neuropathic pain models.
- **Amygdala**: mTORC1 in the central amygdala contributes to the emotional‑affective component of persistent pain.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. mTORC1 Inhibitors

- **Rapamycin (sirolimus) and its analogues (rapalogs)** :
    - **Rapamycin**: Systemic, intraperitoneal, or intrathecal administration of rapamycin—an allosteric inhibitor of mTORC1—consistently and robustly reverses mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in a wide range of neuropathic pain models, including:
        - Chronic constriction injury (CCI)
        - Spared nerve injury (SNI)
        - Spinal nerve ligation (SNL)
        - Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (streptozotocin‑induced)
        - Chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN: paclitaxel, oxaliplatin, vincristine)
        - Spinal cord injury (SCI)‑induced central neuropathic pain
    - The analgesic effects are dose‑dependent and accompanied by reduced mTORC1 activity (decreased phospho‑S6 ribosomal protein), restored autophagic flux (normalised LC3‑II/p62 ratio), decreased spinal microgliosis and astrogliosis, and reduced expression of pro‑inflammatory cytokines.
    - Rapamycin also prevents and reverses **opioid‑induced hyperalgesia** and tolerance, by blocking mTOR‑driven glial activation that opposes opioid analgesia.
- **Everolimus, temsirolimus, deforolimus**: Rapamycin analogues with improved pharmacokinetics. They have shown similar analgesic efficacy in preclinical pain models.

### 2. PI3K and Akt Inhibitors

- **PI3K inhibitors**:
    - **Wortmannin** and **LY294002**: Classic, non‑isoform‑selective PI3K inhibitors. Intrathecal delivery reduces pain behaviours in CCI and SNL models, with associated reductions in Akt phosphorylation and spinal neuroinflammation. Their clinical utility is limited by toxicity and poor pharmacokinetics.
    - **Isoform‑selective PI3K inhibitors**: In preclinical use, PI3Kγ‑ and PI3Kδ‑selective inhibitors have shown analgesic effects in inflammatory and neuropathic pain models by targeting immune cell signalling while sparing the broadly expressed PI3Kα isoform.
- **Akt inhibitors** (e.g., **MK‑2206**, **perifosine**): Inhibit all Akt isoforms. Intrathecal or systemic administration reduces pain behaviours and normalises mTORC1 activity in rodent neuropathic pain models. However, Akt is essential for neuronal survival, and systemic Akt inhibition carries risks of neurotoxicity and immunosuppression.

### 3. AMPK Activators (Indirect mTORC1 Inhibition)

- **Metformin**: The first‑line antidiabetic drug activates AMPK, inhibits mTORC1, and restores autophagy. Systemic metformin treatment reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in diabetic neuropathy, CCI, and paclitaxel‑induced CIPN models. Some retrospective clinical studies suggest a lower incidence of neuropathic pain in diabetic patients on metformin, but prospective pain trials are lacking.
- **AICAR**: A direct AMPK activator that inhibits mTORC1 and produces analgesia in neuropathic pain models.
- **Resveratrol**, **curcumin**, and **sulforaphane**: Natural compounds that activate AMPK, thereby inhibiting mTORC1 and restoring autophagy. Their analgesic effects are partially attributed to this mechanism.

### 4. Genetic Models

- **Neuronal‑specific TSC1/2 deletion**: Mice lacking TSC2 in sensory neurons, leading to constitutive mTORC1 activation, spontaneously develop mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity, confirming that mTORC1 hyperactivation is sufficient to cause pain. Rapamycin reverses this phenotype.
- **PTEN deletion**: Loss of PTEN in sensory neurons or spinal cord neurons results in sustained Akt/mTOR activity and neuropathic pain.
- **DRG‑specific Raptor knockout**: Deletion of the essential mTORC1 component Raptor in DRG neurons prevents nerve injury‑induced mTORC1 activation, restores autophagy, and significantly attenuates mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia.

### 5. Natural Compounds

- **Cannabidiol (CBD)** : Activates PPARγ and inhibits the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in microglia and neurons, reducing neuroinflammation and promoting autophagy.
- **Curcumin** and **resveratrol**: Inhibit mTORC1 by activating AMPK and by directly modulating mTOR and PI3K activity.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

- **Drug repurposing with rapamycin and rapalogs**: Rapamycin (sirolimus) and everolimus are FDA‑approved for immunosuppression in transplant recipients, coronary stent coatings, and the treatment of certain cancers (renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, neuroendocrine tumours). Their well‑characterised human pharmacokinetics, oral bioavailability, and established, though significant, toxicity profiles make them immediately available for clinical testing in neuropathic pain. Case reports and small series have noted pain improvement in patients receiving rapalogs, and a Phase II trial of rapamycin for fibromyalgia is ongoing. A major limitation is the side‑effect profile: immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, hyperlipidaemia, stomatitis, and interstitial pneumonitis.
- **Metformin repurposing**: Metformin is a safe, inexpensive, and widely used oral antidiabetic drug with a favourable side‑effect profile. Its analgesic effects in preclinical models and its ability to activate AMPK and inhibit mTORC1 make it a strong candidate for clinical trials in painful diabetic neuropathy and other chronic pain conditions. Some epidemiological studies suggest a lower incidence of neuropathic pain in metformin‑using diabetic patients, supporting this approach.
- **Localised delivery**: Intrathecal administration of rapamycin or other mTORC1 inhibitors could achieve high spinal cord concentrations while avoiding systemic exposure, thereby mitigating immunosuppression and metabolic side effects. Preclinical data show that intrathecal rapamycin produces potent analgesia without systemic toxicity.
- **Isoform‑specific PI3K inhibitors**: PI3Kγ‑ and PI3Kδ‑selective inhibitors, which target immune cells and microglia, may allow blockade of neuroinflammation while sparing the broadly expressed and physiologically essential PI3Kα and PI3Kβ isoforms. This approach is still in preclinical exploration for pain.
- **Biomarker‑guided therapy**: Phosphorylated S6 (p‑S6, a marker of mTORC1 activity) in skin biopsies, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, or cerebrospinal fluid could identify patients with elevated mTOR signalling and may predict response to mTOR‑targeted therapy.
- **Combination strategies**:
    - **mTORC1 inhibitors + opioids**: Rapamycin prevents opioid‑induced hyperalgesia and tolerance, allowing sustained analgesia at lower opioid doses.
    - **mTORC1 inhibitors + KCC2 enhancers**: Restoring autophagy and reducing calpain activity protects KCC2; combining an mTOR inhibitor with a KCC2 enhancer could synergistically restore GABAergic inhibition.
    - **mTORC1 inhibitors + anti‑NGF antibodies**: Blocking the upstream NGF‑TrkA‑PI3K signal while directly inhibiting mTOR could provide a powerful two‑pronged attack on pain‑related translational reprogramming.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

- **Essential physiological roles and systemic toxicity**: mTORC1 is indispensable for cell growth and proliferation, immune function, wound healing, and development. Chronic systemic mTORC1 inhibition leads to immunosuppression, increased infection risk, impaired wound and bone healing, metabolic disturbances (hyperglycaemia, hyperlipidaemia), stomatitis, and pneumonitis. These toxicities represent a significant barrier to the use of rapalogs for chronic, non‑life‑threatening pain conditions.
- **Cognitive and memory impairment**: mTORC1‑dependent protein synthesis is critical for hippocampal long‑term potentiation, learning, and memory consolidation. Chronic systemic rapamycin treatment impairs memory formation and cognitive flexibility. This is a serious concern for a pain‑relieving therapy, particularly given that chronic pain itself impairs cognition.
- **Dual role of autophagy in pain**: While mTOR inhibition and autophagy induction are generally analgesic, excessive or prolonged autophagy can lead to autosis (autophagic cell death), which could damage irreplaceable neurons. The therapeutic window for mTOR inhibition must balance restoration of quality control with the risk of excessive autophagy.
- **mTORC2 and off‑target effects**: Rapamycin primarily inhibits mTORC1; prolonged treatment can also indirectly inhibit mTORC2 in some tissues, which may contribute to metabolic side effects. mTORC2 is essential for Akt Ser⁴⁷³ phosphorylation and for cytoskeletal regulation, and its inhibition could have additional adverse effects.
- **Compensatory pathways**: Long‑term mTORC1 inhibition can lead to compensatory activation of the ERK/MAPK pathway, which itself is pronociceptive. This suggests that monotherapy may not be durably effective and that combined mTOR/ERK inhibition could be more efficacious but also more toxic.
- **Sex differences**: Preclinical studies suggest that the mTOR pathway may be differentially regulated in males and females. Some data indicate that rapamycin’s analgesic effect is more pronounced in male rodents, but this is inconsistent across models. Sex‑stratified analysis in clinical trials is necessary.
- **Interactions with the wider pain network**:
    - **ERK/MAPK**: mTORC1 and ERK are often co‑activated and can reciprocally regulate each other. Combined inhibition may be synergistic.
    - **AMPK**: The AMPK‑mTOR axis is a central energy‑sensing rheostat; AMPK activators (metformin) indirectly inhibit mTOR and are a clinically feasible strategy.
    - **BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2**: mTORC1‑driven glial BDNF release, coupled with mTOR‑mediated suppression of KCC2 translation, powerfully disinhibits nociceptive circuits.
    - **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: mTORC1 promotes the translation of these cytokines, and they in turn activate PI3K/Akt/mTOR, forming a feed‑forward loop.
    - **NLRP3 inflammasome**: mTORC1 inhibits autophagy, leading to mitochondrial ROS accumulation that activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL‑1β maturation.
    - **Autophagy–Apoptosis balance**: mTORC1 sits at the centre of the decision between cell survival and programmed cell death. Its inhibition restores autophagy, clears damaged mitochondria, and reduces apoptosis in GABAergic interneurons.
    - **Wnt/β‑catenin**: Wnt signalling activates mTORC1, and mTORC1 can potentiate Wnt‑driven translation and inflammation.
    - **PPARγ**: PPARγ activation inhibits mTORC1 via AMPK‑dependent and ‑independent mechanisms.
    - **CB2 receptor**: CB2 agonists can inhibit the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in immune cells, linking cannabinoid signalling to mTOR‑mediated analgesia.
