# Involvement of GM-CSF (Granulocyte-Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor) in Neuropathic Pain

Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, also known as CSF2) is a monomeric glycoprotein cytokine that was originally identified as a hematopoietic growth factor essential for the proliferation, differentiation, and survival of myeloid progenitor cells. Over the past decade, a convergence of preclinical and clinical evidence has repositioned GM-CSF as a key **pro‑nociceptive cytokine** and a central upstream mediator in the neuroimmune cascade that drives chronic neuropathic pain. Unlike classical pain mediators that act directly on nociceptors, GM-CSF operates principally through **indirect mechanisms**—orchestrating the expansion, mobilization, and activation of myeloid cells (neutrophils and monocytes) that infiltrate pain‑relevant compartments, where they release a secondary wave of pronociceptive mediators (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, reactive oxygen species, prostaglandins). A paradigm‑shifting discovery in 2025 revealed that after peripheral nerve injury, meningeal group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) surrounding the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) produce GM-CSF, which instructs **emergency myelopoiesis in the vertebral bone marrow**, fueling a local supply of myeloid cells to the DRG meninges that is both necessary and sufficient for pain chronicity. At the spinal level, GM-CSF acts on microglia and astrocytes to potentiate their pro‑inflammatory responses. In sensory neurons, GM-CSF sensitises nociceptors indirectly via immune cell‑derived factors, though direct neuronal effects via GM-CSF receptor (GM‑CSFR, also known as CSF2R)‑dependent JAK2/STAT3‑mediated upregulation of voltage‑gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9) have also been documented in some pain states. Pharmacological neutralization of GM-CSF or genetic deletion of its receptor consistently alleviates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and pain‑related behaviours in diverse preclinical models, and anti‑GM‑CSF monoclonal antibodies (e.g., otilimab, namilumab) have demonstrated significant analgesic efficacy in Phase II/III clinical trials for rheumatoid arthritis. These findings position the GM‑CSF/CSF2R axis as a high‑priority target for non‑opioid, disease‑modifying analgesic development.

## 🧬 The GM‑CSF/CSF2R System: Gene, Ligand, Receptor, and Physiological Functions

*   **Gene and protein**: GM‑CSF is encoded by the *CSF2* gene located on human chromosome 5q31.1. The mature protein is a 14.6 kDa monomeric glycoprotein that belongs to the β‑common (βc) cytokine family, along with interleukin‑3 (IL‑3) and interleukin‑5 (IL‑5). GM‑CSF is produced by a wide variety of cell types, including activated T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and, as recently discovered, group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) in the meninges.
*   **Receptor (GM‑CSFR/CSF2R)** : The GM‑CSF receptor is a heterodimer composed of:
    *   **CSF2RA (GM‑CSFRα, CD116)** : The ligand‑binding α‑subunit that binds GM‑CSF with high specificity but low affinity.
    *   **CSF2RB (β‑common chain, βc, CD131)** : A shared signal‑transducing β‑chain that is also used by the IL‑3 and IL‑5 receptors. Upon GM‑CSF binding, CSF2RB is recruited, leading to receptor dimerization and activation of intracellular signaling cascades.
*   **Downstream signaling pathways**: The GM‑CSFR signals primarily through:
    *   **JAK2/STAT3 pathway**: Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) is constitutively associated with the β‑chain. Ligand binding activates JAK2, which phosphorylates the receptor and recruits STAT3, STAT5, and other STATs. STAT3 is the dominant STAT activated in nociceptive contexts and drives the transcription of pronociceptive genes.
    *   **MAPK (ERK, p38, JNK) pathway**: The adaptor protein Shc and Grb2 couple the receptor to the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK cascade.
    *   **PI3K/Akt pathway**: Regulates cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic reprogramming.
    *   **NF‑κB pathway**: Indirectly activated via IKK, contributing to pro‑inflammatory gene expression.
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution of GM‑CSFR**:
    *   **Myeloid cells (highest expression)** : Monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and microglia. In the CNS, GM‑CSFR is prominently expressed on spinal microglia and astrocytes.
    *   **Sensory neurons**: Expression in DRG neurons has been reported but is controversial. Some studies using RNA‑seq and functional assays found no functional GM‑CSFR in murine nociceptors, while others reported GM‑CSFR expression in a subset (17‑30%) of small‑/medium‑sized DRG neurons, with colocalization with TRPV1. In human DRG, GM‑CSFR is expressed in ~30% of small/medium neurons and ~10% of large neurons, with no significant change after avulsion injury.
    *   **Peripheral nerves**: GM‑CSFR is expressed on axons of healthy peripheral nerves but is markedly decreased after chronic painful nerve injury and in painful neuromas. The infiltrating CD68⁺ macrophages in these tissues do not appear to express GM‑CSFR.
    *   **Synovium**: GM‑CSFR is absent in control synovium but markedly upregulated in macrophages of painful osteoarthritic knee synovium, providing a peripheral site of action in joint pain.
*   **Physiological functions**: GM‑CSF is a hematopoietic growth factor that stimulates the production, differentiation, and activation of granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils) and macrophages from bone marrow precursors. It is also a key regulator of emergency myelopoiesis—the rapid, demand‑driven production of myeloid cells in response to infection or injury. Beyond hematopoiesis, GM‑CSF promotes the survival, activation, and pro‑inflammatory polarization of mature myeloid cells, enhances antigen presentation, and participates in tissue repair and fibrosis.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of GM‑CSF in Neuropathic Pain

GM‑CSF contributes to neuropathic pain through a multi‑tiered mechanism that spans the meninges, vertebral bone marrow, peripheral nerves, dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord, and brain. The overarching theme is that GM‑CSF acts as an **instructive upstream signal** that mobilizes and activates the myeloid cell compartment, which then releases a secondary cocktail of pronociceptive mediators that ultimately sensitise nociceptors and drive central sensitization.

### 1. The Meningeal ILC2–GM‑CSF–Vertebral Emergency Myelopoiesis Axis

A landmark 2025 study by Pigatto, Cunha, and colleagues (bioRxiv preprint) uncovered a previously unrecognized neuroimmune axis that is both necessary and sufficient for the development of neuropathic pain.

*   **DRG meningeal immunity**: The meninges surrounding the DRG form an immunologically active niche. Following peripheral nerve injury (spared nerve injury, SNI; chronic constriction injury, CCI), there is a robust and persistent accumulation of leukocytes—predominantly myeloid cells (neutrophils and classical monocytes)—in the ipsilateral lumbar DRG meninges, peaking 10–14 days post‑injury and persisting for at least 28 days. This response is localized to the injured side and is not observed in the contralateral DRG meninges or brain.
*   **Meningeal ILC2s as the source of GM‑CSF**: Single‑cell RNA sequencing of DRG meningeal leukocytes after SNI revealed that **group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s)** —a subset of tissue‑resident innate lymphocytes—are the predominant source of GM‑CSF (*Csf2*). Among all leukocyte populations in the DRG meninges, *Csf2* was almost exclusively expressed in ILC2s, and its expression was dramatically upregulated after nerve injury.
*   **Human translational evidence**: Reanalysis of spatial RNA‑sequencing data from DRGs of organ donors with a history of **painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN)** —the most common form of neuropathic pain in humans—revealed an increased number of ILC2‑associated barcodes in PDN DRGs, located at the edge of the DRG or in meningeal regions, consistent with the mouse findings. This provides a direct translational link between meningeal ILC2–GM‑CSF signaling and human neuropathic pain.
*   **Vertebral emergency myelopoiesis**: Meningeal ILC2‑derived GM‑CSF does not act locally at the DRG parenchyma. Instead, it diffuses into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and travels through ossified channels in the vertebral bone to reach the **vertebral bone marrow (BM)** , where it binds to CSF2R on hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and stimulates **emergency myelopoiesis**—the rapid expansion of common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), granulocyte‑monocyte progenitors (GMPs), and mature myeloid cells. This effect is **localized to the vertebral BM**; the tibial BM is unaffected, demonstrating a compartmentalized, spinal‑segment‑specific hematopoietic response.
*   **Migration of myeloid cells to the DRG meninges**: The newly generated myeloid cells (primarily neutrophils, ~55%, and classical monocytes, ~38%) exit the vertebral BM and migrate through ossified vertebral channels to accumulate in the DRG meninges. Here, they promote pain hypersensitivity through the release of pronociceptive mediators.
*   **Causality**: Intrathecal injection of recombinant GM‑CSF alone is sufficient to induce vertebral emergency myelopoiesis, leukocyte accumulation in the DRG meninges, and pain hypersensitivity. This is completely abolished by JAK2 inhibition or by genetic deletion of *Csf2ra* (GM‑CSFRα). Conversely, blockade of GM‑CSF with a neutralizing antibody disrupts the entire cascade—preventing vertebral myelopoiesis, reducing myeloid cell accumulation in the DRG meninges, and **abolishing neuropathic pain**. *Csf2ra*‑/‑ mice exhibit reduced vertebral emergency myelopoiesis, decreased myeloid cell accumulation, and attenuated pain hypersensitivity after SNI, with no changes in basal pain sensitivity or motor function.

This mechanism redefines neuropathic pain as, in part, a disorder of **local vertebral bone marrow‑driven neuroinflammation**, with meningeal GM‑CSF as the instructive signal.

### 2. Spinal Glial Modulation and Central Sensitization

Beyond the meningeal‑vertebral axis, GM‑CSF acts directly on the spinal cord to promote central sensitization through glial cell modulation.

*   **Microglial and astrocytic GM‑CSFR**: GM‑CSFR is expressed on both spinal microglia and astrocytes. Following nerve injury, GM‑CSF levels in the spinal cord are elevated, and GM‑CSF signaling contributes to the pro‑inflammatory phenotype of these glial cells.
*   **Potentiation of glial pro‑inflammatory responses**: *In vitro*, pretreatment of glial cells with GM‑CSF potentiates the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‑induced release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6). This suggests that GM‑CSF primes glial cells to respond more vigorously to DAMPs and other injury signals, amplifying the neuroinflammatory cascade.
*   **Contribution to nociceptive signaling**: In the spared nerve injury (SNI) model, central inhibition of GM‑CSF signaling—either through genetic deletion of the GM‑CSFR β‑chain or treatment with an antagonizing monoclonal antibody—significantly alleviated mechanical allodynia. The analgesic effect was enhanced by using an engineered antibody construct with greater blood‑brain barrier penetration.
*   **Indirect mechanism via glial‑derived mediators**: GM‑CSF itself does not directly activate nociceptors (at least not via a functional receptor on murine nociceptors). Instead, GM‑CSF‑activated microglia and macrophages secrete a cocktail of pronociceptive factors—including TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, reactive oxygen species, and prostaglandins—that in turn sensitise nociceptor terminals and spinal dorsal horn neurons. This indirect mechanism places GM‑CSF upstream of many of the pronociceptive pathways discussed in this series.

### 3. JAK2/STAT3‑Mediated Upregulation of Voltage‑Gated Sodium Channels in DRG Neurons

In bone cancer pain models, GM‑CSF has been shown to have a **direct effect** on DRG neurons through the activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, leading to the transcriptional upregulation of nociceptor‑specific voltage‑gated sodium channels.

*   **GM‑CSF expression in tumors**: GM‑CSF is highly expressed in human malignant osteosarcoma and other tumors that cause bone cancer pain. Tumor‑derived GM‑CSF acts on DRG neurons that innervate the tumor microenvironment.
*   **JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation**: GM‑CSF binding to GM‑CSFR on DRG neurons activates JAK2, which phosphorylates and activates STAT3. Phosphorylated STAT3 translocates to the nucleus and promotes the transcription of **Nav1.7 (SCN9A)**, **Nav1.8 (SCN10A)**, and **Nav1.9 (SCN11A)** —three TTX‑resistant or TTX‑sensitive sodium channels that are critical for nociceptor excitability.
*   **Enhanced neuronal excitability**: The upregulation of these sodium channels results in increased sodium current density, a lower action potential threshold, and enhanced repetitive firing in DRG neurons. This sensitisation contributes to both spontaneous pain and evoked mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity.
*   **Therapeutic validation**: Targeted knockdown of Nav1.7–Nav1.9 or JAK2/STAT3 in DRG neurons *in vivo* significantly alleviated hyperalgesia in bone cancer pain models. Antagonizing GM‑CSF in tumor‑bearing animals also reduced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity.
*   **Note on species and model differences**: This direct neuronal mechanism appears to be more prominent in **bone cancer pain** models and possibly in **male animals**. In other neuropathic pain models (e.g., traumatic nerve injury), the indirect immune‑mediated mechanism appears dominant, and the presence of a functional GM‑CSFR on nociceptors has been questioned.

### 4. Peripheral Actions: Sensitization of Nociceptor Terminals and Neurogenic Inflammation

In the periphery, GM‑CSF acts on tissue‑resident and recruited immune cells to create a pro‑nociceptive environment.

*   **Macrophage‑nociceptor crosstalk**: GM‑CSF applied to cultured macrophages induces the secretion of factors that, when transferred as conditioned medium to purified nociceptors, upregulate pronociceptive genes (e.g., *Trpv1*, *Scn9a*) and downregulate anti‑nociceptive genes. This is a bidirectional crosstalk: conditioned medium from NGF‑treated nociceptors also modifies macrophage gene transcription. This paracrine communication is a key peripheral mechanism of GM‑CSF‑mediated pain.
*   **Absence of direct nociceptor activation**: Importantly, GM‑CSF applied **directly** to purified murine nociceptors does **not** induce any transcriptional changes in nociceptive genes, establishing the **absence of a functional GM‑CSF receptor in murine nociceptors** in the context of traumatic neuropathic pain. This finding highlights the fundamental importance of the indirect, immune‑mediated mechanism.
*   **TRPV1 sensitization**: In cultured rat DRG neurons, GM‑CSF treatment sensitizes capsaicin (TRPV1) responses, an effect that is diminished by p38 MAPK or TrkA inhibitors. This suggests that GM‑CSF can prime nociceptors to respond more vigorously to TRPV1 agonists, likely through immune cell intermediaries or co‑signaling pathways.
*   **Cyclo‑oxygenase (COX) products**: The pronociceptive effects of GM‑CSF in inflammatory pain models are partly mediated by COX‑dependent prostaglandins, as the non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drug (NSAID) indomethacin can reverse GM‑CSF‑induced pain without affecting the underlying inflammatory pathology.

### 5. Clinical Human Tissue Evidence

Studies of human tissues provide important translational support for the role of GM‑CSF/CSF2R in clinical pain conditions.

*   **Multiple sclerosis (MS) spinal cord**: GM‑CSFR is markedly increased in microglia at lesional sites of MS spinal cords, where it co‑localises with the macrophage marker CD68. This suggests a role in the neuroinflammatory pain that often accompanies MS.
*   **Painful peripheral nerve injury**: In chronically painful injured peripheral nerves and painful neuromas, **axonal GM‑CSFR is significantly decreased**, while CD68‑positive macrophages are increased. The macrophages, however, do not appear to express GM‑CSFR, suggesting that the receptor loss on axons may be a compensatory response to chronic inflammation or that the main site of GM‑CSF action in these conditions is elsewhere (e.g., spinal cord, meninges).
*   **Osteoarthritis synovium**: GM‑CSFR is markedly upregulated in macrophages of painful osteoarthritic knee synovium, providing a direct target tissue for anti‑GM‑CSF therapy in osteoarthritic pain.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Pharmacological and Biological Antagonists

*   **Anti‑GM‑CSF monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)** : Neutralizing antibodies against GM‑CSF (e.g., **CAM3003** in mice) have been shown to alleviate mechanical allodynia in the partial sciatic nerve ligation (PNL) model of neuropathic pain. Both systemic (subcutaneous) and intrathecal administration are effective, with intrathecal delivery providing direct central nervous system access.
    *   **Engineered BBB‑penetrant constructs**: Conjugating the anti‑GM‑CSFR mAb to a blood‑brain barrier (BBB) technology platform to enhance CNS penetration resulted in **superior analgesic efficacy** compared to the unmodified antibody, highlighting the importance of central compartment targeting for maximal pain relief.
    *   In bone cancer pain models, systemic antagonism of GM‑CSF significantly reduced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity.
*   **Anti‑GM‑CSFR monoclonal antibodies**: Blocking the receptor (CAM3003 anti‑mouse GM‑CSFR) also produces analgesia, confirming that the pronociceptive effects are mediated through the canonical CSF2R.
*   **GM‑CSF neutralization in arthritis models**: In collagen‑induced and antigen‑induced arthritis models, anti‑GM‑CSF treatment not only reduced joint inflammation and cartilage destruction but also produced a **robust analgesic effect that was dissociable from the anti‑inflammatory effect**. The NSAID indomethacin reversed the pain but not the arthritis, demonstrating that GM‑CSF drives pain through distinct downstream pathways.

### 2. Genetic Models

*   **GM‑CSFR β‑chain knockout mice**: Mice lacking the β‑common chain of the GM‑CSFR (shared with IL‑3 and IL‑5 receptors) exhibit significantly reduced mechanical allodynia after PNL compared to wild‑type littermates. The gliosis response (Iba1⁺ microglia, GFAP⁺ astrocytes) in the dorsal horn is not altered in these knockout mice, suggesting that GM‑CSF modulates glial **function** (e.g., cytokine secretion) rather than glial proliferation in neuropathic pain.
*   **CSF2RA knockout mice (*Csf2ra*‑/‑)** : Mice lacking the GM‑CSFR α‑chain are viable, fertile, and exhibit normal basal pain sensitivity and motor function. After SNI, *Csf2ra*‑/‑ mice show:
    *   Reduced vertebral BM emergency myelopoiesis.
    *   Decreased accumulation of myeloid cells (neutrophils and classical monocytes) in the DRG meninges.
    *   Attenuated pain hypersensitivity.
    *   No response to intrathecal GM‑CSF‑induced pain.
*   **Conditional GM‑CSF deletion**: siRNA‑mediated knockdown of GM‑CSF or its receptor in the spinal cord or DRG is analgesic in inflammatory and bone cancer pain models.

### 3. JAK2/STAT3 Pathway Inhibition

*   **JAK2 inhibitors (e.g., AG490)** : Blocking the JAK2/STAT3 pathway downstream of the GM‑CSFR prevents GM‑CSF‑induced upregulation of Nav1.7–Nav1.9 in DRG neurons and alleviates hyperalgesia in bone cancer pain models. Intrathecal JAK2 inhibition also abolishes GM‑CSF‑induced vertebral emergency myelopoiesis and pain hypersensitivity, confirming the necessity of this pathway for the meningeal‑vertebral axis.

### 4. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Resveratrol** and **curcumin**: Suppress GM‑CSF production by inhibiting NF‑κB and MAPK pathways, contributing to their analgesic effects in neuropathic pain models.
*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces GM‑CSF levels in inflamed tissues and may modulate GM‑CSFR expression in the spinal cord.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Anti‑GM‑CSF monoclonal antibodies in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) pain**: The most clinically advanced validation of GM‑CSF as a pain target comes from Phase II/III clinical trials of anti‑GM‑CSF and anti‑GM‑CSFR monoclonal antibodies in RA.
    *   **Otilimab (GSK3196165)** : A human anti‑GM‑CSF mAb. In a Phase 2b trial in patients with active RA and inadequate response to conventional synthetic disease‑modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), otilimab produced **significant, rapid, and sustained reductions in pain scores**, with improvements in physical function. The analgesic effect was clinically meaningful and occurred independently of the anti‑inflammatory effect. The Phase III contRAst program (contRAst 1, 2, and 3) further evaluated otilimab versus placebo or active comparators (tofacitinib, sarilumab). While the primary endpoint (ACR20 response) was met in contRAst 1 and 2 but not contRAst 3, otilimab consistently demonstrated significant improvements in patient‑reported pain outcomes.
    *   **Namilumab (AMG203)** : Another human anti‑GM‑CSF mAb that has shown analgesic efficacy in RA and is being investigated in axial spondyloarthritis (NAMASTE trial).
    *   **Mavrilimumab**: A human anti‑GM‑CSFRα mAb that blocks GM‑CSF signaling at the receptor level. Phase II trials in RA reported significant reductions in pain and disease activity, with a rapid onset of analgesia.
*   **Dissociation of analgesic and anti‑inflammatory effects**: A key clinical observation from the RA trials is that the analgesic effect of anti‑GM‑CSF therapy occurs **independently and more rapidly** than the anti‑inflammatory effect (reduction in swollen/tender joint counts, acute phase reactants). This supports the preclinical evidence that GM‑CSF drives pain through separate downstream pathways (e.g., direct effects on nociceptors, mobilization of pronociceptive myeloid cells) that are distinct from those mediating synovitis. This is a critical proof of concept for the development of GM‑CSF‑targeted therapies specifically for chronic pain, even in the absence of overt inflammation.
*   **Safety profile**: Anti‑GM‑CSF therapies have been generally well‑tolerated in clinical trials. The most common adverse events are mild to moderate injection‑site reactions and transient decreases in neutrophil counts, which are typically asymptomatic and reversible. Unlike broad‑spectrum immunosuppressants (e.g., JAK inhibitors), anti‑GM‑CSF agents do not appear to significantly increase the risk of serious infections, likely because GM‑CSF is only one of several redundant pathways for myeloid cell production and function.
*   **Extension to neuropathic pain**: The robust analgesic efficacy observed in RA, a condition with a significant neuropathic‑like pain component (e.g., central sensitization), together with the powerful preclinical data in traumatic and metabolic neuropathic pain models, provides a strong rationale for investigating anti‑GM‑CSF therapies in primary neuropathic pain conditions. Clinical trials of anti‑GM‑CSF or anti‑GM‑CSFR biologics for painful diabetic neuropathy, post‑herpetic neuralgia, or chemotherapy‑induced neuropathic pain have not yet been reported but are a logical next step.
*   **Targeting the vertebral meningeal axis**: The discovery of the meningeal ILC2–GM‑CSF–vertebral emergency myelopoiesis axis opens new therapeutic possibilities. Intrathecal or systemic anti‑GM‑CSF therapy could interrupt this pathway, and the localized nature of the response (limited to vertebral BM) suggests that peripheral biomarkers (e.g., circulating myeloid cells, CSF GM‑CSF levels) could be used to monitor target engagement and treatment response.
*   **GM‑CSF as a biomarker**: Elevated GM‑CSF levels have been detected in the CSF, synovial fluid, and serum of patients with chronic pain conditions. GM‑CSF levels could serve as a biomarker for patient stratification, predicting those most likely to respond to anti‑GM‑CSF therapy.
*   **Combination strategies**: Given that GM‑CSF acts upstream of multiple pronociceptive pathways, its neutralization could be synergistically combined with:
    *   **Low‑dose opioids**: Anti‑GM‑CSF could reduce the inflammatory drive that opposes opioid analgesia and contributes to tolerance.
    *   **COX‑2 inhibitors or NSAIDs**: As GM‑CSF‑induced pain is partly COX‑dependent, combination with NSAIDs could allow lower doses of each and reduce side effects.
    *   **Gabapentinoids or Nav channel blockers**: Co‑targeting the upstream immune drive (GM‑CSF) and the downstream neuronal hyperexcitability (α₂δ‑1, Nav channels) may produce synergistic analgesia.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Indirect mechanism and species/cell‑type differences**: The finding that functional GM‑CSFR is absent from murine nociceptors in some studies, but present on human DRG neurons and in bone cancer pain models, highlights significant species, model, and context‑dependent differences. The relative contribution of direct neuronal versus indirect immune‑mediated effects of GM‑CSF may vary depending on the pain aetiology, the tissue compartment, and the sex of the individual.
*   **Receptor downregulation in chronic injury**: The marked decrease in axonal GM‑CSFR in chronically painful human nerves and neuromas, despite increased macrophage infiltration, suggests a compensatory downregulation or that the receptor is shed or internalized under sustained inflammatory conditions. This could limit the efficacy of receptor‑targeted (as opposed to ligand‑targeted) therapies in chronic established neuropathic pain.
*   **Redundancy in hematopoietic growth factors**: GM‑CSF is one of several colony‑stimulating factors (G‑CSF, M‑CSF, IL‑3) that regulate myelopoiesis. M‑CSF (CSF1), acting on CSF1R, also drives microglial proliferation and macrophage expansion in neuropathic pain. The functional redundancy between these systems means that blocking GM‑CSF alone may be partially compensated by M‑CSF and other factors. Dual inhibition of GM‑CSF and M‑CSF pathways could be more effective but carries greater risk of myelosuppression.
*   **Physiological roles of GM‑CSF**: GM‑CSF is an important component of host defence against bacterial and fungal pathogens. It is also critical for surfactant homeostasis in the lung (GM‑CSF‑deficient mice and humans develop pulmonary alveolar proteinosis). Complete, chronic neutralization of GM‑CSF could increase the risk of pulmonary infections and impair mucosal immunity. The safety data from RA trials are reassuring for the short‑to‑medium term, but the long‑term risks of chronic GM‑CSF blockade remain to be fully defined.
*   **G‑CSF vs. GM‑CSF confusion**: It is essential to distinguish **GM‑CSF** from **G‑CSF** (granulocyte colony‑stimulating factor, filgrastim). G‑CSF has been reported to have **neuroprotective** and **analgesic** effects in some spinal cord injury and compression myelopathy models. Case reports and small clinical trials have noted that intravenous G‑CSF dramatically reduces neuropathic pain in patients with thoracic compression myelopathy. These opposite effects of G‑CSF and GM‑CSF on pain highlight the distinct biology of these two colony‑stimulating factors and the importance of not conflating them.
*   **Sex differences**: The preclinical studies on GM‑CSF in neuropathic pain have used both male and female animals, and the meningeal ILC2–GM‑CSF axis does not appear to differ between sexes. However, the direct JAK2/STAT3‑mediated neuronal mechanism was demonstrated in male rats only. The potential for sex‑specific differences in GM‑CSF biology and response to anti‑GM‑CSF therapy warrants further investigation.
*   **Interactions with the wider pain network**: GM‑CSF sits at a very upstream position in the neuroimmune pain cascade:
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: GM‑CSF‑activated myeloid cells and glial cells release these cytokines, which directly sensitise nociceptors and drive central sensitization.
    *   **Prostaglandins (PGE₂)** : GM‑CSF‑induced pain is partly mediated by COX‑2‑dependent prostaglandins.
    *   **Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9**: GM‑CSF, via JAK2/STAT3, upregulates these sodium channels in DRG neurons in bone cancer pain.
    *   **M‑CSF/CSF1R**: GM‑CSF and M‑CSF have overlapping but distinct roles in myelopoiesis and microglial/macrophage activation. They may act synergistically in neuropathic pain.
    *   **JAK/STAT3**: GM‑CSF is a potent activator of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, providing a direct link to the broader JAK/STAT signaling network that sustains neuroinflammation and glial activation.
    *   **BDNF/TrkB/KCC2**: By promoting the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines from glial cells, GM‑CSF indirectly facilitates the BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2 disinhibition cascade.
    *   **CCL2/CCR2 and CX3CL1/CX3CR1**: GM‑CSF can synergize with chemokine systems to recruit and activate monocytes and microglia.
    *   **NLRP3 inflammasome**: GM‑CSF‑primed macrophages and microglia may exhibit enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in response to DAMPs, amplifying IL‑1β‑mediated neuroinflammation.
