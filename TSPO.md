# Involvement of TSPO in Neuropathic Pain

The 18 kDa translocator protein (TSPO) is a mitochondrial outer membrane protein that acts as a key regulator of mitochondrial health, neurosteroid synthesis, inflammation, and cell survival. In neuropathic pain, TSPO is dynamically upregulated in both the spinal cord and specific brain regions following peripheral nerve injury, where it serves a protective, anti‑nociceptive role. Pharmacological activation of TSPO—for example, by its ligands etifoxine, Ro5‑4864, or the repurposed drug zopiclone—alleviates pain hypersensitivity, reduces neuroinflammation, limits apoptosis and pyroptosis, and restores mitochondrial function. Importantly, TSPO is also a validated clinical biomarker of neuroinflammation: its brain expression is increased in chronic pain patients as measured by PET imaging and is reduced by effective antinociceptive interventions.

### 🧬 TSPO Structure, Localization, and Normal Function

*   **Location and structure**: TSPO is a 169‑amino acid protein located predominantly on the outer mitochondrial membrane. It was previously known as the "peripheral benzodiazepine receptor" but was renamed to reflect its broader role in mitochondrial biology.[reference:0]
*   **Cellular expression**: In the nervous system, TSPO is expressed mainly in glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) and, to a lesser extent, in neurons. Under healthy conditions, its expression in the CNS is low, but it is dramatically upregulated following injury or inflammation.[reference:1]
*   **Physiological roles**:
    *   **Cholesterol transport and neurosteroid synthesis**: TSPO mediates the transport of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane, the rate‑limiting step in the synthesis of neurosteroids, which are potent positive allosteric modulators of GABAA receptors.[reference:2]
    *   **Mitochondrial homeostasis**: TSPO interacts with the voltage‑dependent anion channel (VDAC) and adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT) to form the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). It regulates mitochondrial respiration, membrane potential, and calcium handling.[reference:3]
    *   **Regulation of programmed cell death**: TSPO modulates both apoptosis and pyroptosis through its control of mitochondrial integrity, ROS production, and the mPTP.[reference:4]

### 🔬 Mechanisms of TSPO in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury, TSPO expression is increased in a time‑dependent manner in the spinal dorsal horn and dorsal root ganglia (DRG), as well as in discrete brain regions. This upregulation is thought to represent an endogenous protective response aimed at restoring cellular homeostasis.

*   **Spinal TSPO upregulation**: After L5 spinal nerve ligation (SNL), spared nerve injury (SNI), or chronic constriction injury (CCI), TSPO levels increase in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn. This upregulation persists for weeks and correlates with the duration of pain behaviors.[reference:5][reference:6][reference:7]
*   **Cellular source in the spinal cord**: Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry studies demonstrate that TSPO is primarily localized in **astrocytes** in the spinal dorsal horn after injury, with some expression in microglia.[reference:8][reference:9][reference:10]
*   **Brain TSPO upregulation**: PET‑CT imaging with the radioligand [¹⁸F]‑PBR06 in rats with CCI revealed that TSPO is broadly upregulated across multiple brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex, somatosensory cortex, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, motor cortex, ventral tegmental area, amygdala, midbrain, pons, medulla, and nucleus accumbens. Upregulation occurred at 4 weeks post‑CCI in males and 10 weeks in females, with regional sex differences. Immunohistochemistry confirmed TSPO expression principally in microglia in the nucleus accumbens core, and in astrocytes and endothelial cells in the nucleus accumbens shell.[reference:11]
*   **Mitochondrial dysfunction and pyroptosis**: Nerve injury disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, impairing biogenesis and fusion while promoting fission. TSPO upregulation counteracts these pathological changes. If the protective TSPO response is insufficient, mitochondrial damage triggers pyroptosis—a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death—in spinal astrocytes. Pyroptosis releases additional damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pro‑inflammatory cytokines, further amplifying neuroinflammation and central sensitization.[reference:12][reference:13]
*   **AMPK‑PGC‑1α pathway**: TSPO exerts its mitochondrial protective effects largely through the AMP‑activated protein kinase (AMPK)–PGC‑1α axis. AMPK phosphorylation activates PGC‑1α, the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense. This restores mitochondrial number, promotes fusion over fission, and enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes. Inhibition of the AMPK‑PGC‑1α pathway abolishes the protective effects of TSPO activation on mitochondrial balance and pyroptosis suppression.[reference:14]
*   **Autophagy restoration**: In neuropathic pain, autophagic flux is impaired—autophagosomes accumulate but their clearance is blocked. TSPO activation (e.g., by Ro5‑4864) restores autophagic flux by upregulating ATG7, Beclin‑1, and LC3‑II, while decreasing the autophagy substrate p62/SQSTM1. This improved autophagic clearance eliminates damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates, reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation.[reference:15]
*   **Nrf2‑dependent antioxidant system**: In diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), TSPO activation stimulates the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, a transcription factor that drives the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as HO‑1 and NQO1. This Nrf2‑mediated antioxidant defense reduces oxidative damage in Schwann cells and peripheral nerves, preserving myelin integrity and improving nerve conduction.[reference:16][reference:17]
*   **Modulation of neuroinflammation**: TSPO ligands powerfully reduce neuroinflammation at multiple levels:
    *   Reduced microglial and astrocytic activation in the spinal cord and DRG.[reference:18][reference:19]
    *   Decreased transcription and release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6, IL‑1β).[reference:20][reference:21]
    *   Inhibition of the CXCL1‑CXCR2‑dependent astrocyte‑to‑neuron signaling pathway, which contributes to central sensitization.[reference:22]
*   **Neurosteroid synthesis and GABAergic potentiation**: TSPO activation stimulates the synthesis of 3α‑reduced neurosteroids (e.g., allopregnanolone), which are the most potent endogenous positive allosteric modulators of GABAA receptors. By enhancing GABAergic and glycinergic inhibition in the spinal dorsal horn, TSPO‑derived neurosteroids counteract the disinhibition that underlies mechanical allodynia. The analgesic effects of TSPO agonists are abolished by pretreatment with the neurosteroid synthesis inhibitor AMG, confirming this pathway's importance.[reference:23][reference:24]
*   **Protection against prostaglandin E₂‑induced disinhibition**: Etifoxine not only amplifies spinal GABAergic inhibition but also prevents prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂)‑induced glycinergic disinhibition, thereby restoring "normal" spinal pain processing. This dual action—enhancing inhibition and preventing its breakdown—is particularly effective against central sensitization.[reference:25]

### 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

*   **Pharmacological TSPO Agonists**:
    *   **Ro5‑4864**: The classical TSPO agonist. A single intrathecal injection of Ro5‑4864 at days 7 and 21 after L5 SNL dramatically suppresses established mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia. The effect is dose‑dependent and abolished by the neurosteroid synthesis inhibitor AMG.[reference:26][reference:27]
    *   **FGIN‑1‑27**: Another TSPO agonist that, when injected intrathecally, reverses established mechanical allodynia induced by L5 SNL.[reference:28]
    *   **Zopiclone**: Identified through drug screening and molecular docking as a promising TSPO‑targeting drug. Zopiclone, already clinically used as a hypnotic, was shown to alleviate neuropathic pain in the SNI model by upregulating TSPO and preventing pyroptosis via the AMPK‑PGC‑1α pathway.[reference:29]
*   **Etifoxine (Stresam)** :
    *   Etifoxine (50 mg/kg, twice daily) post‑sciatic nerve crush significantly alleviated mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity and improved motor dysfunction in rats.[reference:30]
    *   Etifoxine treatment reduced microglial presence and pro‑inflammatory cytokine transcription (TNF‑α, IL‑6, IL‑1β) in the DRG and spinal cord.[reference:31]
    *   Etifoxine prevented the decline in mitochondrial respiration (non‑phosphorylation, ATP‑linked, and maximal respiration) after mPTP induction by Ca²⁺, indicating protection of mitochondrial function under stress.[reference:32]
    *   In a monoarthritis model, curative and preventive treatment with etifoxine (50 mg/kg) efficiently reduced neuropathic pain symptoms, accompanied by reduced microglial activation, lower pro‑inflammatory mediator levels, amplified GABAergic inhibition, and prevention of PGE₂‑induced glycinergic disinhibition.[reference:33]
*   **Genetic Approaches**:
    *   **TSPO overexpression lentivirus (LV‑TSPO)** : Intraspinal injection of LV‑TSPO to overexpress TSPO in the spinal dorsal horn relieved the development of chronic neuropathic pain in the SNL model, while TSPO‑inhibited lentivirus (LV‑shTSPO) did not.[reference:34]
    *   **Mendelian randomization**: Analysis of GWAS data indicated that genetically predicted increased TSPO expression is linked to pain relief, providing human genetic evidence for TSPO's protective role.[reference:35]
*   **Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy**: In DPN rats, TSPO agonist Ro5‑4864 alleviated mechanical allodynia, increased myelin sheath thickness, improved Schwann cell function and regeneration, restored autophagic flux, and activated the Nrf2‑dependent antioxidant system. All beneficial effects were abrogated by the autophagy inhibitor 3‑MA or the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385.[reference:36]
*   **Diroximel Fumarate**: This Nrf2‑activating drug, used clinically for multiple sclerosis, alleviated neuropathic pain and reduced TSPO upregulation in the brain after CCI. This demonstrates that TSPO upregulation is sensitive to antinociceptive interventions and supports the concept of TSPO as a treatment‑responsive biomarker.[reference:37]
*   **TSPO Antagonists**: The TSPO antagonist PK11195 does not alleviate mechanical allodynia and may block the beneficial effects of endogenous TSPO activation. This indicates that TSPO *activation*, not simply its presence, is required for analgesia.[reference:38]
*   **Inhibition of Downstream Pathways**: The analgesic effects of TSPO activation can be blocked by:
    *   **AMG** (neurosteroid synthesis inhibitor)[reference:39]
    *   **3‑MA** (autophagy inhibitor)[reference:40]
    *   **ML385** (Nrf2 inhibitor)[reference:41]
    *   **AMPK‑PGC‑1α pathway inhibition**[reference:42]

### 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **TSPO PET Imaging as a Pain Biomarker**: Multiple human PET studies using TSPO radioligands (e.g., [¹¹C]PBR28, [¹¹C]PK11195, [¹⁸F]‑PBR06) have demonstrated that brain and spinal cord TSPO signals are elevated in patients with chronic low back pain, fibromyalgia, migraine, and other persistent pain conditions, compared to healthy volunteers. The TSPO signal shows a degree of disorder‑specific spatial distribution and is parametrically linked to pain characteristics and comorbid symptoms (e.g., nociplastic pain, fatigue, depression). This positions TSPO PET as a potential objective biomarker for neuroinflammation in human pain and a tool for patient stratification and treatment monitoring.[reference:43]
*   **Spinal Cord TSPO Imaging**: In patients with cervical spinal disorders and neuropathic pain, (R)‑[¹¹C]PK11195 PET/MRI of the cervical spinal cord visualized TSPO uptake in cases up to 10 months after injury or surgery. A positive correlation was found between the standardized uptake value ratio and the severity of neuropathic pain, suggesting clinical utility for objective evaluation of chronic pain.[reference:44]
*   **Drug Repurposing Opportunities**:
    *   **Etifoxine (Stresam)** : Already marketed in several countries as a non‑benzodiazepine anxiolytic. Its dual action as a TSPO ligand and GABAA receptor positive allosteric modulator makes it an attractive candidate for clinical trials in chronic pain. New clinical trials are required to extend its therapeutic indications to pain management.[reference:45]
    *   **Zopiclone**: A widely prescribed hypnotic identified as a TSPO‑targeting drug. Its safety profile is well‑known, facilitating clinical translation for pain.[reference:46]
    *   **Diroximel Fumarate**: An approved drug for multiple sclerosis that activates Nrf2 and reduces TSPO upregulation. Its analgesic effects in preclinical models support investigation in pain patients with a neuroinflammatory component.[reference:47]
*   **Therapeutic Strategies Targeting the TSPO Pathway**:
    *   **TSPO agonists**: Direct activation with Ro5‑4864 analogues or etifoxine derivatives optimized for CNS penetration and chronic administration.
    *   **Downstream pathway activators**: Targeting AMPK, PGC‑1α, Nrf2, or autophagic machinery may replicate TSPO's protective effects without directly binding TSPO, potentially avoiding TSPO‑related genetic issues (see below).
    *   **Combination therapies**: Pairing TSPO ligands with drugs that enhance GABAergic tone, reduce oxidative stress, or promote mitochondrial health could produce synergistic analgesia.

### ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **TSPO Genetic Polymorphism**: A common single nucleotide polymorphism (rs6971) in the human *TSPO* gene results in a non‑conservative amino acid substitution (Ala147Thr) that significantly affects the binding affinity of several TSPO radioligands and potentially the response to TSPO‑targeting drugs. Approximately 30% of the population are low‑affinity binders. This genetic variability must be accounted for in PET imaging studies and may influence the clinical efficacy of TSPO ligands.[reference:48]
*   **Conditional TSPO Knockout Controversy**: Global TSPO knockout mice are viable and fertile, challenging the earlier notion that TSPO is essential for life. Some conditional TSPO knockout studies have questioned whether TSPO is absolutely required for steroidogenesis, leading to ongoing debate about its precise molecular function. This controversy highlights that the field is still evolving in its understanding of TSPO biology.
*   **Dual Role of TSPO Expression**: TSPO upregulation is a protective, compensatory response to nerve injury. Pharmacological *downregulation* of TSPO is not beneficial; only *upregulation* or *activation* of TSPO alleviates pain. TSPO expression returns to control levels or decreases substantially when neuropathic pain heals naturally or is reversed by treatment, suggesting that the endogenous upregulation is a pro‑recovery mechanism. Therapeutic strategies should therefore aim to enhance, not suppress, TSPO function.[reference:49][reference:50]
*   **Cell‑Type Specificity**: The cellular source of TSPO differs by CNS region—astrocytes in the spinal cord versus microglia and endothelial cells in certain brain regions. This heterogeneity implies that TSPO‑targeting drugs may have region‑ and cell‑type‑specific effects, which could be therapeutically exploited or could complicate dosing.[reference:51][reference:52]
*   **Sex Differences**: TSPO upregulation shows different temporal dynamics between males and females after nerve injury, with broader upregulation at 4 weeks in males and at 10 weeks in females. The functional significance of this temporal difference is not yet fully understood but may influence the timing of TSPO‑targeted interventions in a sex‑specific manner.[reference:53]
*   **Off‑Target Effects**: TSPO is expressed in peripheral tissues (adrenal glands, kidney, heart, immune cells). Systemic TSPO agonists could affect steroid hormone synthesis in the adrenal cortex, cardiovascular function, and immune responses. Localized delivery (intrathecal) or peripherally restricted ligands may be necessary to limit side effects.
*   **Interactions with the Wider Pain Network**:
    *   **TLR4**: The upregulation of TSPO after L5 SNL is prevented by pharmacological blockade of Toll‑like receptor 4 (TLR4), indicating that TLR4‑mediated neuroinflammatory signaling drives TSPO expression. This links TSPO to the broader neuroimmune activation cascade initiated by DAMPs such as HMGB1 and S100A8/A9.[reference:54]
    *   **TNF‑α**: TSPO activation reduces TNF‑α production by astrocytes and microglia, interrupting one of the key pronociceptive cytokine loops. Conversely, sustained TNF‑α signaling may suppress TSPO‑mediated protective responses, contributing to pain chronicity.[reference:55][reference:56]
    *   **Autophagy–Apoptosis–Pyroptosis**: TSPO sits at the intersection of these cell death and quality‑control pathways. By restoring autophagic flux and preventing mPTP opening, TSPO blocks the progression from mitochondrial dysfunction to pyroptosis and apoptosis in spinal glial cells, directly linking to the GABAergic interneuron health and calcium overload mechanisms discussed in previous sections.[reference:57][reference:58]
    *   **GABAergic/Glycinergic Inhibition**: TSPO‑derived neurosteroids potentiate both GABAA and glycine receptor function, directly counteracting the disinhibition that arises from KCC2 downregulation, PNN degradation, and PV interneuron loss. Thus, TSPO activation targets a convergent point of multiple pronociceptive pathways.[reference:59]
    *   **Prostaglandins**: Etifoxine prevents PGE₂‑induced glycinergic disinhibition, linking TSPO signaling to the modulation of prostaglandin‑driven central sensitization.[reference:60]
    *   **NLRP3 Inflammasome**: By preserving mitochondrial integrity and reducing ROS, TSPO limits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent IL‑1β maturation, connecting mitochondrial protection to the suppression of sterile neuroinflammation.
