# Involvement of Protein Kinase C (PKC) in Neuropathic Pain

Protein kinase C (PKC) comprises a family of serine/threonine kinases that function as central intracellular transducers of pronociceptive signalling in the peripheral and central nervous systems. In neuropathic pain, specific PKC isoforms—particularly **PKCε**, **PKCγ**, **PKCα**, and **PKCβII**—are chronically activated downstream of inflammatory mediators, growth factors, and metabotropic receptors. Once active, PKC phosphorylates a broad array of substrates that together amplify neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission, including TRPV1, voltage‑gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8), NMDA receptors (GluN2B), and the potassium‑chloride cotransporter KCC2, while also driving transcriptional programmes that sustain neuroinflammation and central sensitisation. Pharmacological inhibition of specific PKC isoforms, or genetic deletion of *Prkce* (PKCε), consistently attenuates mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain in multiple preclinical models. Despite robust validation, the clinical development of isoform‑selective PKC inhibitors has been hampered by the highly conserved ATP‑binding pocket across the PKC family and by the broad physiological roles of PKCs in cardiovascular, immune, and cognitive functions. Nonetheless, PKC remains a high‑value node in the pain network, and strategies to target its specific anchoring proteins, regulatory domains, or downstream substrates are emerging as alternative paths to harness its therapeutic potential.

## 🧬 The PKC Family: Classification, Activation, and Normal Function

*   **Gene family**: The mammalian PKC family comprises nine genes that are classified into three groups based on their regulatory domains and co‑factor requirements:
    *   **Conventional (cPKCs)**: **PKCα** (*PRKCA*), **PKCβI** and **PKCβII** (splice variants of *PRKCB*), and **PKCγ** (*PRKCC*). They require calcium (Ca²⁺) and diacylglycerol (DAG) for activation.
    *   **Novel (nPKCs)**: **PKCδ** (*PRKCD*), **PKCε** (*PRKCE*), **PKCη** (*PRKCH*), and **PKCθ** (*PRKCQ*). They are calcium‑independent but require DAG.
    *   **Atypical (aPKCs)**: **PKCζ** (*PRKCZ*) and **PKCι/λ** (*PRKCI*). They are independent of both Ca²⁺ and DAG and are regulated by protein–protein interactions (e.g., with PAR‑3/PAR‑6).
*   **Activation mechanism**: Under basal conditions, PKC resides in the cytosol in an autoinhibited conformation. Receptor‑mediated activation of phospholipase C (PLCβ or PLCγ) hydrolyses phosphatidylinositol‑4,5‑bisphosphate (PIP₂) to generate **DAG** and **IP₃**. IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores. DAG, together with Ca²⁺ (for cPKCs), recruits PKC to the plasma membrane and induces a conformational change that relieves autoinhibition, allowing substrate phosphorylation. Sustained activation can lead to PKC translocation to other membranes (e.g., mitochondrial, nuclear) and prolonged signalling.
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **DRG nociceptors**: PKCε and PKCα are highly expressed in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptive neurons. PKCε is particularly enriched in the peripheral terminals and is a critical mediator of inflammatory and neuropathic sensitisation. PKCγ is expressed in a subset of medium‑sized DRG neurons and in spinal cord interneurons.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: PKCγ is heavily expressed in a specific population of excitatory interneurons in lamina II inner (IIi), where it is required for the development of mechanical allodynia after nerve injury. PKCα and PKCβII are expressed in lamina I–II neurons and glial cells.
    *   **Supraspinal**: PKCγ and PKCε are present in the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and periaqueductal grey, where they modulate the affective and descending components of pain.
*   **Physiological functions**: PKC isozymes regulate diverse processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, neurotransmitter release, ion channel function, synaptic plasticity, and immune responses. In the adult nervous system, PKC is essential for certain forms of learning and memory (e.g., PKCγ in cerebellar long‑term depression, PKCε in hippocampal LTP).

## 🔬 Mechanisms of PKC in Neuropathic Pain

Peripheral nerve injury leads to a sustained increase in DAG levels and intracellular Ca²⁺, resulting in the chronic activation of specific PKC isoforms at multiple anatomical levels. The downstream consequences are overwhelmingly pronociceptive.

### 1. PKCε as a Master Regulator of Primary Afferent Sensitisation

PKCε is the most extensively validated isoform in nociceptor sensitisation and peripheral pain amplification.

*   **Upstream activation**: Pro‑inflammatory mediators released after nerve injury—**bradykinin**, **prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂)** , **nerve growth factor (NGF)** , **proteases (PAR2 agonists)** , and **TNF‑α**—bind to their respective G‑protein‑coupled receptors or TrkA receptors on nociceptor terminals, activating PLC and thereby generating DAG. This directly activates PKCε. Additionally, increased intracellular Ca²⁺ enhances its membrane translocation.
*   **TRPV1 phosphorylation**: PKCε directly phosphorylates the TRPV1 channel at **Ser⁸⁰⁰** (and other sites), dramatically sensitising it to heat, capsaicin, and protons. This phosphorylation lowers the thermal activation threshold of TRPV1 into the physiological range (~37 °C), enabling ongoing activity at body temperature and contributing to spontaneous burning pain and thermal hyperalgesia.
*   **TRPA1 sensitisation**: PKCε similarly phosphorylates TRPA1, enhancing its response to reactive oxygen species and endogenous lipid agonists, and contributing to mechanical and cold hypersensitivity.
*   **Voltage‑gated sodium channel modulation**: PKCε phosphorylates **Nav1.8** (at multiple cytoplasmic loop sites), increasing its current amplitude and producing a hyperpolarising shift in activation voltage, which dramatically increases nociceptor excitability. PKCε also enhances the activity of **Nav1.7** and **Nav1.9**, promoting ectopic firing.
*   **Kv channel suppression**: PKCε phosphorylates and downregulates certain voltage‑gated potassium channels (e.g., Kv1.2, Kv4.x), reducing the delayed rectifier and A‑type K⁺ currents that normally limit action potential firing. The loss of these brakes further amplifies nociceptor hyperexcitability.
*   **Membrane trafficking**: PKCε promotes the insertion of TRPV1, Nav1.8, and P2X3 receptors into the plasma membrane of nociceptor terminals, increasing the density of functional pronociceptive channels.

### 2. PKCγ in Spinal Mechanical Allodynia and Central Sensitisation

PKCγ is uniquely positioned in the spinal cord to drive the mechanical allodynia that characterises neuropathic pain.

*   **Lamina II inner (IIi) interneuron expression**: PKCγ is selectively expressed in a subset of excitatory interneurons in the inner part of lamina II, a region that normally receives input from low‑threshold Aβ mechanoreceptors. Under physiological conditions, these PKCγ⁺ interneurons are kept under tight GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition from surrounding interneurons and receive minimal direct Aβ input.
*   **Disinhibition and gate opening after nerve injury**: Following peripheral nerve injury, the inhibition onto PKCγ⁺ interneurons is weakened (due to KCC2 downregulation and loss of inhibitory neuron function). Simultaneously, Aβ‑fibres that normally terminate in deeper laminae sprout into lamina II and form aberrant excitatory synapses onto PKCγ⁺ neurons. Thus, light tactile stimuli now directly activate PKCγ⁺ neurons, which in turn synapse onto lamina I nociceptive projection neurons, opening a gate for tactile allodynia.
*   **PKCγ‑dependent phosphorylation**: Once activated by the combined DAG and Ca²⁺ signals from enhanced synaptic input, PKCγ phosphorylates postsynaptic **GluN2B‑containing NMDA receptors**, increasing their conductance and calcium permeability and promoting long‑term potentiation at these aberrant synapses. It also phosphorylates **GluA1** AMPA receptors, enhancing their trafficking to synapses.
*   **Genetic validation**: Mice with a global knockout of *Prkcc* (PKCγ) fail to develop mechanical allodynia after nerve injury, while their responses to acute thermal and mechanical stimuli remain normal. This phenotype is identical to that of NK1‑expressing lamina I neuron ablation, establishing PKCγ⁺ interneurons as a critical link in the allodynia circuit.

### 3. PKCα/β and Glial‑Mediated Neuroinflammation

*   **Microglial PKC**: PKCα and PKCδ are activated in spinal microglia after nerve injury. PKCα contributes to the activation of ERK and p38 MAPK, driving the production of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and BDNF. PKCδ has been implicated in the phagocytic activity of microglia and the release of reactive oxygen species.
*   **Astrocytic PKC**: PKCβII and PKCε are upregulated in reactive astrocytes, where they regulate the release of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1) and the downregulation of glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), thereby sustaining central sensitisation.

### 4. PKC and the Regulation of KCC2

A critical link between PKC and the disruption of GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition is the **PKC‑dependent phosphorylation of KCC2**.

*   PKC (primarily PKCε and PKCγ) directly phosphorylates KCC2 at **Thr⁹⁰⁶** and **Thr¹⁰⁰⁷** in the cytoplasmic C‑terminus. This phosphorylation reduces KCC2’s transport activity, promotes its endocytosis, and targets it for lysosomal degradation.
*   Under conditions of sustained PKC activation, this leads to a significant reduction in membrane KCC2 protein, a rise in intracellular chloride, and the weakening of GABA/glycine‑mediated inhibition—a hallmark of neuropathic pain. This provides a direct, BDNF‑independent pathway by which PKC drives disinhibition.

### 5. Crosstalk with ERK and Other Kinases

PKC and ERK pathways are intimately linked and often act in concert.

*   PKC can directly phosphorylate and activate **Raf‑1** (a MAP3K for ERK), providing a route for PKC to engage the MEK/ERK cascade independently of Ras. This PKC‑ERK crosstalk is important for the transcriptional effects of PKC, such as the induction of c‑Fos, COX‑2, and BDNF.
*   PKC and ERK can phosphorylate each other’s substrates; for example, both PKC and ERK phosphorylate Ser⁸⁰⁰ of TRPV1, integrating multiple upstream signals.
*   PKC also activates **S6 kinase** and **eIF4E**, linking PKC to mTOR‑dependent translational control of pronociceptive gene expression.

### 6. Supraspinal PKC and Pain Affect

*   **Amygdala**: PKCε in the central amygdala is required for the formation of pain‑related aversive memories and anxiety‑like behaviour in chronic pain models.
*   **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** : PKCγ and PKCα contribute to NMDA receptor‑dependent LTP and the emotional‑affective encoding of pain in the ACC.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Isoform‑Selective PKC Inhibitors

*   **PKCε‑specific inhibitors**:
    *   **εV1‑2 peptide**: A peptide derived from the first variable region (V1) of PKCε that blocks its binding to its anchoring protein, RACK2. Intrathecal or local peripheral administration of εV1‑2 significantly reduces mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and capsaicin‑induced pain in models of CCI, SNL, diabetic neuropathy, and CIPN. It normalises TRPV1 and Nav1.8 function in nociceptors without affecting acute nociception.
    *   **KAI‑1678**: A rationally designed epsilon‑PKC inhibitor that reached clinical trials for neuropathic pain (see below).
    *   **Small‑molecule PKCε inhibitors**: Several small molecules with relative selectivity for PKCε over other isoforms are in preclinical development.
*   **PKCγ inhibitors**:
    *   **γV5‑3 peptide**: A PKCγ‑selective inhibitory peptide that blocks its interaction with RACK1. Intrathecal γV5‑3 attenuates mechanical allodynia in CCI models by normalising the activity of PKCγ⁺ interneurons in lamina II.
    *   Genetic knockout of PKCγ in mice completely prevents the development of nerve injury‑induced tactile allodynia.
*   **PKCα/β inhibitors** (e.g., **enzastaurin**, **ruboxistaurin/LY333531**):
    *   **Ruboxistaurin** is a PKCβ‑selective inhibitor originally developed for diabetic retinopathy. In preclinical diabetic neuropathy models, it improves nerve conduction velocity, reduces mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity, and normalises endoneurial blood flow. Clinical trials in diabetic neuropathy showed mixed results, with some improvement in sensory symptoms but not meeting primary pain endpoints.
    *   **Enzastaurin**, a PKCβ/α inhibitor, has shown some analgesic effects in cancer pain models, but its use is limited by toxicity (cardiotoxicity, immunosuppression).

### 2. Broad‑Spectrum PKC Inhibitors

*   **Chelerythrine**: A widely used pan‑PKC inhibitor tool compound. Intrathecal chelerythrine blocks the maintenance of mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI and SNL models, and prevents NMDAR‑dependent wind‑up. However, its use is limited by non‑specific effects and toxicity.
*   **Calphostin C**, **GF109203X**: Other pan‑PKC inhibitors that reduce pain behaviours when delivered intrathecally.

### 3. Genetic Models

*   **PKCε global knockout (*Prkce*‑/‑)** : Mice lacking PKCε exhibit profoundly reduced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia after nerve injury and inflammation. They also show reduced anxiety‑like behaviour and attenuated pain‑related aversive memory. The phenotype is attributable primarily to the loss of nociceptor‑specific PKCε.
*   **PKCγ global knockout (*Prkcc*‑/‑)** : Mice lacking PKCγ are protected from neuropathic mechanical allodynia, with a specific deficit in the Aβ‑fibre‑mediated gate‑opening mechanism in the spinal cord.
*   **Conditional PKC knockouts**: Inducible, cell‑type‑specific deletion of PKCε in sensory neurons (using Advillin‑Cre) recapitulates the pain‑protective phenotype, confirming the nociceptor as the major site of action.

### 4. Natural Compounds and Dietary Interventions

*   **Resveratrol**: Inhibits PKC activity in DRG neurons and spinal cord, reducing TRPV1 and NMDA receptor phosphorylation, and contributing to its analgesic effects.
*   **Curcumin**: Suppresses PKC expression and activation, and reduces downstream ERK and NF‑κB signalling.
*   **Gingerol** (from ginger): A direct PKC inhibitor that reduces inflammatory and neuropathic pain in animal models.

### 5. Endogenous PKC Modulators

*   **Diacylglycerol kinases (DGKs)** : Enzymes that phosphorylate DAG and convert it to phosphatidic acid, thereby terminating DAG‑mediated PKC activation. Upregulation of DGK activity in the spinal cord is analgesic in some models, while loss of DGK enhances PKC‑dependent pain.
*   **RACK proteins**: The receptors for activated C‑kinase (RACK1 and RACK2) are anchoring proteins that localise specific PKC isoforms to their substrates. Targeting the PKC–RACK interaction (as with εV1‑2 and γV5‑3 peptides) represents a highly selective way to inhibit specific PKC functions without blocking the ATP‑binding pocket.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **PKCε as a validated, but challenging, analgesic target**: The robust preclinical efficacy of PKCε inhibition and the availability of selective peptide inhibitors (εV1‑2) and small molecules have made PKCε a high‑priority target.
    *   **KAI‑1678**, a first‑in‑class PKCε‑selective inhibitor, entered Phase II clinical trials for chronic post‑herpetic neuralgia and chronic low back pain with a neuropathic component, but **failed to demonstrate significant analgesic efficacy** compared to placebo. The reasons for the translational failure remain debated, but may include insufficient target engagement in the human DRG, rapid clearance of the peptide, or redundancy with other PKC isoforms.
*   **Ruboxistaurin in diabetic neuropathy**: Phase III trials for diabetic retinopathy did not meet their primary endpoints for vision loss, but post‑hoc analyses and smaller neuropathy trials indicated some improvement in vibration perception thresholds and sensory symptoms. The drug was generally well‑tolerated but lacked a sufficiently strong analgesic signal for regulatory approval.
*   **Local and perineural delivery**: Peptide inhibitors of PKCε (e.g., εV1‑2) are too large and hydrophilic to cross the blood‑brain barrier efficiently. Local delivery—perineural, intrathecal, or topical—could maximise target engagement in primary sensory neurons while minimising systemic exposure and central side effects. Sustained‑release formulations are under investigation.
*   **Targeting PKC anchoring proteins**: The PKC–RACK interaction is essential for the localisation of activated PKC isoforms to their specific substrates. Peptide inhibitors that disrupt PKCε–RACK2 binding selectively block the pronociceptive functions of PKCε without inhibiting its global catalytic activity. This represents a novel strategy to achieve isoform and pathway specificity.
*   **Biomarker potential**: PKC activity, as measured by the phosphorylation of its substrates (e.g., TRPV1 pSer⁸⁰⁰, KCC2 pThr⁹⁰⁶), in skin biopsies, CSF, or peripheral blood cells, could serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for target engagement and patient stratification.
*   **Combination strategies**:
    *   **PKCε inhibition + TrkB antagonists**: Combining PKCε and BDNF‑TrkB blockade would target both parallel and convergent pathways driving KCC2 downregulation and ion channel sensitisation.
    *   **PKCε inhibition + TRPV1 desensitisation**: Using low‑dose capsaicin (to desensitise TRPV1) and a PKCε inhibitor (to prevent re‑sensitisation) could produce sustained analgesia.
    *   **PKCγ inhibition + KCC2 enhancers**: PKCγ drives the allodynia circuit; combining with KCC2 enhancers could directly restore inhibition and synergise with the blockade of the gate‑opening mechanism.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Isoform conservation and selectivity challenge**: The ATP‑binding site is highly conserved across all PKC isoforms and other kinases (e.g., PKA, PKG), making ATP‑competitive inhibitors that are truly isoform‑selective extremely difficult to design. Most small‑molecule “selective” PKC inhibitors have significant off‑target activity, limiting their utility and increasing toxicity.
*   **Physiological essentiality**: PKC isozymes are essential for cardiac contraction (PKCα, PKCε), immune cell function (PKCθ, PKCδ), platelet aggregation (PKCα, PKCβ), and synaptic plasticity and memory (PKCγ, PKCε). Systemic, chronic PKC inhibition carries risks of cardiotoxicity, immunosuppression, bleeding, and cognitive impairment.
*   **Dual role of PKCε in cardioprotection**: PKCε is a critical mediator of ischaemic preconditioning and cardioprotection in the heart. Systemic inhibition of PKCε could increase the risk of myocardial infarction or cardiac dysfunction, particularly in patients with pre‑existing cardiovascular disease.
*   **PKCδ and apoptotic signalling**: PKCδ is generally pro‑apoptotic and its inhibition could impair the clearance of damaged cells or promote tumourigenesis. Pan‑PKC inhibitors that block PKCδ are particularly problematic.
*   **Redundancy among PKC isoforms**: In nociceptors, PKCε, PKCα, and PKCδ can all phosphorylate overlapping substrates (e.g., TRPV1 at multiple sites). Blocking PKCε alone may lead to compensatory activation of PKCα and sustained channel sensitisation.
*   **Sex differences**: PKCε‑mediated nociceptor sensitisation has been reported to be more pronounced in male rodents in some pain models, with females potentially relying more on PKA‑driven or adaptive immune mechanisms. PKC‑targeted therapies may therefore have sex‑dependent efficacy.
*   **Interactions with the wider pain network**:
    *   **ERK/MAPK**: PKC and ERK pathways converge on TRPV1, Nav1.8, and KCC2, and mutually activate each other. Combined inhibition may be necessary.
    *   **BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2**: PKC directly phosphorylates KCC2, and BDNF‑TrkB signalling activates PKC via PLCγ, providing a parallel pathway for disinhibition.
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, PGE₂**: These are all potent upstream activators of PKC in nociceptors, linking neuroinflammation to PKC‑dependent sensitisation.
    *   **RhoA/ROCK**: PKC can activate RhoA, linking cytokine signalling to cytoskeletal changes and synaptic remodelling.
    *   **mTOR**: PKC can phosphorylate TSC2 and activate mTORC1, promoting translational control of pronociceptive genes.
    *   **Nav1.7/Nav1.8 and TRPV1**: These are the key ion channels through which PKC exerts its pronociceptive effects, making them the downstream effectors of this signalling hub.
