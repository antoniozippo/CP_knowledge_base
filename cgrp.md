# Involvement of CGRP in Neuropathic Pain

Calcitonin gene‑related peptide (CGRP) is a 37‑amino acid neuropeptide that is widely expressed in primary sensory neurons and is a key mediator of neurogenic inflammation and nociceptive signaling. Although traditionally studied in migraine, CGRP is now firmly established as a significant pronociceptive modulator in neuropathic pain. Following peripheral nerve injury, CGRP synthesis, release, and receptor‑mediated signaling are markedly dysregulated in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), spinal dorsal horn, and supraspinal pain‑processing regions. CGRP acts at its receptor—a heterodimer of the calcitonin receptor‑like receptor (CLR) and receptor activity‑modifying protein 1 (RAMP1)—to enhance glutamatergic transmission, promote glial activation, facilitate central sensitization, and amplify neuroinflammation. Intriguingly, recent evidence reveals that in chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy, it is not simply an increase in CGRP expression but rather a profound **sensitization** of the CGRP signaling system—via upregulation of RAMP1 and enhanced release—that drives pain. Clinically, anti‑CGRP monoclonal antibodies and small‑molecule CGRP receptor antagonists (gepants) have revolutionised migraine treatment and are now being actively explored for other chronic pain conditions, including neuropathic pain, with emerging efficacy signals.

## 🧬 The CGRP System: Peptide, Receptor, and Physiological Functions

*   **CGRP isoforms**: Two isoforms exist in humans, α‑CGRP (encoded by *CALCA* via tissue‑specific splicing of the calcitonin gene) and β‑CGRP (encoded by *CALCB*). α‑CGRP is the predominant form in the central and peripheral nervous systems and is the focus of pain research.
*   **Synthesis and storage**: CGRP is synthesised in the cell bodies of small‑ and medium‑diameter DRG neurons (C‑fibre and Aδ‑nociceptors) and packaged into large dense‑core vesicles. It is co‑stored and co‑released with substance P, glutamate, and other nociceptive transmitters.
*   **CGRP receptor**: The canonical CGRP receptor is a heterodimer composed of:
    *   **Calcitonin receptor‑like receptor (CLR)** : A G‑protein‑coupled receptor (Gs‑coupled) that signals through cAMP.
    *   **Receptor activity‑modifying protein 1 (RAMP1)** : Essential for CLR trafficking to the plasma membrane and for conferring CGRP binding specificity. RAMP1 expression levels critically determine CGRP responsiveness.
    *   Additional intracellular signalling involves ERK, p38 MAPK, and PKA pathways.
*   **Amylin receptors**: CGRP can also cross‑react with amylin receptors (CTR/RAMP1 complexes), which may contribute to its biological effects, particularly at high concentrations.
*   **Distribution in the nociceptive pathway**:
    *   **Peripheral terminals**: CGRP is released from nociceptor endings in the skin, joints, and injured nerves, where it induces local vasodilation and immune cell recruitment (neurogenic inflammation).
    *   **DRG and trigeminal ganglia**: CGRP is expressed in neuronal cell bodies, and CGRP receptors are found on satellite glial cells, facilitating neuron–glia crosstalk.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: CGRP is densely localised in laminae I–II of the spinal cord, released from the central terminals of primary afferent nociceptors. CGRP receptors are present on postsynaptic neurons, astrocytes, and microglia.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: CGRP and its receptor are found in the periaqueductal grey (PAG), amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and hypothalamus, where they modulate the emotional and autonomic dimensions of pain.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of CGRP in Neuropathic Pain

After peripheral nerve injury, CGRP signalling is persistently amplified at multiple levels—peripheral, spinal, and supraspinal—through a combination of increased release, enhanced receptor function, and altered intracellular coupling.

### 1. Peripheral Sensitization and Neurogenic Inflammation

*   In the injured nerve and DRG, CGRP synthesis is initially upregulated in small nociceptors and, interestingly, *de novo* in some large myelinated Aβ‑fibres, which begin to express CGRP and substance P—a phenotypic switch that contributes to mechanical allodynia.
*   CGRP released from peripheral terminals acts on CGRP receptors on vascular smooth muscle to produce potent vasodilation, and on immune cells (mast cells, macrophages, dendritic cells) to promote the release of pro‑inflammatory mediators (histamine, TNF‑α, IL‑1β, NGF). This neurogenic inflammation sensitises nociceptor endings and sustains a cycle of pain.
*   CGRP can also directly sensitise nociceptors by enhancing TRPV1 and TRPA1 currents and by modulating voltage‑gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8) via PKA‑mediated phosphorylation.

### 2. Spinal Central Sensitization and Glial Activation

*   **Enhanced neurotransmitter release**: CGRP released from central afferent terminals facilitates the presynaptic release of glutamate and substance P, potentiating excitatory synaptic transmission onto lamina I projection neurons. CGRP also enhances postsynaptic NMDA receptor function through PKA‑ and PKC‑dependent phosphorylation of the GluN2B subunit, promoting long‑term potentiation (LTP) at nociceptive synapses.
*   **Glial activation**: CGRP receptors are expressed on spinal microglia and astrocytes. CGRP binding induces glial activation, resulting in the production and release of BDNF, TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and reactive oxygen species. This glial‑derived cocktail further drives central sensitization and disinhibition through the BDNF–TrkB–KCC2 cascade.
*   **Disinhibition**: By promoting microglial BDNF release, CGRP indirectly downregulates KCC2 in spinal neurons, collapsing the chloride gradient and weakening GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition—a hallmark of neuropathic pain.
*   **RAMP1 upregulation**: In rodent models of neuropathic pain (e.g., spinal nerve ligation, chronic constriction injury), RAMP1—the rate‑limiting subunit for CGRP receptor function—is significantly upregulated in the spinal dorsal horn. This sensitises the spinal cord to CGRP, meaning that even normal levels of CGRP release can produce exaggerated pain responses.

### 3. Chemotherapy‑Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN): Sensitization, Not Just Expression

A paradigm‑shifting discovery in CGRP biology is that in oxaliplatin‑ and paclitaxel‑induced neuropathy, **CGRP‑mediated pain is driven primarily by sensitization of the CGRP signaling pathway, not by increased CGRP peptide expression**.

*   In oxaliplatin‑treated mice, CGRP mRNA and peptide levels in the DRG are actually **unchanged or even slightly decreased**, yet pain behaviours are prominent.
*   The key change is a dramatic upregulation of **RAMP1** in DRG neurons, which increases the surface expression of functional CGRP receptors. This renders nociceptors hyper‑responsive to ambient CGRP.
*   Additionally, calcium imaging reveals that CGRP‑induced calcium responses in DRG neurons are **enhanced** due to increased calcium mobilisation from intracellular stores, indicating sensitised receptor–effector coupling.
*   CGRP release from nociceptor terminals is also facilitated, independently of changes in peptide content. This combination—increased receptor availability and enhanced release—creates a potentiated CGRP axis that drives mechanical and cold hypersensitivity.
*   Paclitaxel similarly sensitises the CGRP system via TLR4‑mediated signalling, which upregulates RAMP1 and enhances CGRP‑dependent nociceptor excitability.

### 4. Supraspinal CGRP and Pain Affect

*   In the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA), CGRP receptor blockade produces sex‑dependent modulation of neuropathic pain behaviours. In female rodents, CGRP in the CeA appears to facilitate pain, whereas in males its role is less pronounced or even opposite, highlighting a complex, sex‑specific supraspinal regulation.
*   In the ACC and PAG, CGRP modulates descending pain pathways, potentially contributing to both pro‑ and anti‑nociceptive influences depending on the receptor subtype and neural circuit engaged.

### 5. CGRP in Diabetic Neuropathy and Post‑Surgical Pain

*   In diabetic peripheral neuropathy, CGRP expression and release are initially increased, contributing to early hyperalgesia. However, with prolonged diabetes, CGRP levels can fall due to neurodegeneration, coinciding with the transition to hypoalgesia or loss of protective sensation.
*   In post‑surgical pain models, CGRP is released from injured nerve endings and contributes to acute and persistent pain. Anti‑CGRP treatment during the peri‑operative period reduces the development of chronic post‑surgical pain in some preclinical studies.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

A wide range of approaches—from pharmacological antagonists and antibodies to genetic silencing—have been used to demonstrate the causal role of CGRP in neuropathic pain.

### 1. Anti‑CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies

*   **Galcanezumab, fremanezumab, eptinezumab**: Approved for migraine prevention, these antibodies have been tested in preclinical neuropathic pain models. Systemic or intrathecal administration reduces mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNI, diabetic neuropathy, and CIPN models.
*   The primary mechanism is the neutralisation of peripheral and central CGRP, thereby reducing neuroinflammation and central sensitization. Notably, these antibodies show better efficacy in models with a strong inflammatory component.

### 2. Small‑Molecule CGRP Receptor Antagonists (Gepants)

*   **Olcegepant (BIBN4096BS)** and **telcagepant (MK‑0974)** : First‑generation gepants. Intrathecal or systemic delivery attenuates neuropathic pain behaviours in rodent models by blocking spinal CGRP receptors.
*   **Ubrogepant, rimegepant, atogepant**: Second‑generation, orally bioavailable gepants approved for migraine. Preclinical data indicate that rimegepant, in particular, reduces mechanical allodynia in CIPN and CCI models. Clinical trials in neuropathic pain are being planned or are in early phases.
*   **Peripheral vs. central action**: Gepants with poor CNS penetration are still effective in some pain models, confirming that peripheral CGRP receptors on nociceptors and immune cells are key mediators. However, central penetration may be required for full efficacy in spinal‑driven pain.

### 3. Genetic Approaches

*   **α‑CGRP knockout mice**: *Calca*‑null mice show reduced inflammatory pain and mechanical hypersensitivity after nerve injury, but normal acute thermal sensitivity. Their phenotype is milder than expected, likely due to developmental compensation by β‑CGRP or other neuropeptides (e.g., substance P).
*   **RAMP1 knockout or knockdown**: Mice lacking RAMP1 display attenuated mechanical allodynia and reduced central sensitization after nerve injury, confirming the critical role of the CGRP receptor in pain.
*   **CLR conditional deletion**: Neuronal deletion of CLR in sensory neurons or spinal cord reduces pain behaviours and neuroinflammation, validating the receptor as a target.

### 4. Natural Compounds and Non‑Pharmacological Interventions

*   **Electroacupuncture**: Reduces CGRP expression in the DRG and spinal cord, contributing to its analgesic effects in neuropathic pain models.
*   **Curcumin and resveratrol**: Downregulate CGRP synthesis via inhibition of NF‑κB and MAPK pathways, with associated reductions in pain behaviours.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Anti‑CGRP therapies in migraine**: The clinical success of anti‑CGRP monoclonal antibodies and gepants for migraine demonstrates that systemic CGRP neutralisation or receptor blockade is safe and effective for a chronic pain condition. This provides a strong foundation for exploring their use in other pain disorders.
*   **Emerging evidence in neuropathic pain**:
    *   **Plasma CGRP as a biomarker**: Elevated plasma CGRP levels have been found in patients with polyneuropathy‑associated neuropathic pain, with a positive correlation to pain intensity. This suggests that circulating CGRP could serve as a biomarker for patient stratification.
    *   **Case reports and small series**: Some patients with refractory neuropathic pain (e.g., complex regional pain syndrome, post‑herpetic neuralgia) have shown improvement when treated with anti‑CGRP monoclonal antibodies, though controlled data are lacking.
    *   **Clinical trials**: A clinical trial (NCT05823909) is investigating galcanezumab for the treatment of chronic low back pain with a neuropathic component. Trials of rimegepant for painful diabetic neuropathy and post‑herpetic neuralgia are in planning stages.
*   **Potential advantages of CGRP‑targeted therapies**:
    *   **Non‑opioid and non‑addictive**: CGRP drugs have no known abuse potential.
    *   **Peripheral and central modulation**: They simultaneously target neurogenic inflammation and central sensitization.
    *   **Favourable safety profile**: Unlike NSAIDs or opioids, anti‑CGRP antibodies and gepants have a relatively benign side‑effect profile (mild injection‑site reactions, constipation for some gepants) and are not associated with major organ toxicity.
*   **Gepants for acute neuropathic pain flares**: Oral gepants, with their rapid onset, could be used for episodic exacerbations of neuropathic pain, similar to their use in acute migraine attacks.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Sex‑specific CGRP biology**: CGRP signalling and its modulation exhibit pronounced sex differences.
    *   In rodents, CGRP‑induced mechanical hypersensitivity is more prominent in females, and the expression of RAMP1 is higher in female spinal cord and trigeminal ganglia.
    *   In humans, women have higher circulating CGRP levels, and the efficacy of anti‑CGRP therapies in migraine appears slightly greater in women.
    *   In neuropathic pain, the CeA CGRP receptor blockade produces analgesia mainly in female mice, suggesting that CGRP‑targeted therapies may be more effective in females. This sex divergence must be considered in clinical trial design.
*   **Dual role in acute vs. chronic pain**: CGRP is critical for the initiation of inflammatory pain and for protective vasodilation. Chronic, complete CGRP inhibition could impair wound healing and tissue repair. Furthermore, in some contexts, CGRP can act as an **anti‑nociceptive** mediator: intracerebroventricular CGRP injection produces analgesia via descending inhibitory pathways, and intrathecal CGRP at low doses can be antinociceptive in certain models. The net effect depends on dose, site, and pain aetiology.
*   **Redundancy with amylin receptors**: At high pharmacological concentrations, CGRP can activate amylin receptors (CTR/RAMP1). Amylin is a satiety hormone, and chronic CGRP blockade could interfere with metabolic signalling, although this has not been a major clinical issue.
*   **Peripheral vs. central targets**: The degree to which CGRP‑targeted drugs need to cross the blood–brain barrier to treat neuropathic pain is unresolved. Gepants with central penetration may offer better spinal efficacy but could produce CNS side effects. Anti‑CGRP antibodies, being large proteins, have minimal CNS access; their peripheral action may be sufficient for some pain conditions but not for centrally driven pain.
*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**:
    *   **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: CGRP stimulates microglial BDNF release, driving disinhibition. Co‑targeting CGRP and TrkB may synergistically restore GABAergic tone.
    *   **Substance P/NK1**: CGRP and substance P are co‑released and synergise to amplify pain signalling. Dual inhibition may be more effective than either alone.
    *   **TRPV1**: CGRP release is triggered by TRPV1 activation. Conversely, CGRP sensitises TRPV1, forming a positive‑feedback loop. TRPV1 antagonists reduce CGRP release, and vice versa.
    *   **TLR4 and NF‑κB**: CGRP can activate NF‑κB via its G‑protein‑coupled receptor, contributing to the transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines. In turn, cytokines like TNF‑α enhance CGRP synthesis.
    *   **Wnt/β‑catenin**: CGRP may potentiate Wnt‑driven β‑catenin signalling in spinal neurons, linking neuropeptide signalling to long‑term plasticity.
    *   **RhoA/ROCK**: CGRP‑induced vasodilation involves endothelial RhoA/ROCK signalling, but in neurons, CGRP can modulate ROCK to affect growth cone dynamics and synaptic plasticity.
