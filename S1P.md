# Involvement of Sphingosine‑1‑phosphate (S1P) in Neuropathic Pain

Sphingosine‑1‑phosphate (S1P) is a bioactive lysophospholipid that functions as a pleiotropic signalling molecule in the nervous and immune systems. It is generated intracellularly by the phosphorylation of sphingosine, catalysed by sphingosine kinases (SphK1 and SphK2), and exerts most of its biological effects by acting on a family of five G‑protein‑coupled receptors (S1PR1–S1PR5). In the context of neuropathic pain, the S1P/S1PR axis is profoundly dysregulated at multiple levels of the nociceptive neuraxis—peripheral nerves, dorsal root ganglia (DRG), spinal cord, and brain—where it acts as a **bifunctional modulator**: the **S1PR1 subtype** primarily transduces anti‑inflammatory and barrier‑stabilising signals that can be neuroprotective, while **S1PR2 and S1PR3** drive pronociceptive neuroinflammation, glial activation, demyelination, and central sensitisation. After nerve injury, the balance of S1PR expression shifts towards a pathological profile, contributing to the development and maintenance of mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain. Pharmacological modulation of the S1P axis—most notably with **fingolimod (FTY720)** , a non‑selective S1PR modulator that induces functional antagonism of S1PR1—has demonstrated analgesic efficacy in multiple preclinical neuropathic pain models, including traumatic nerve injury, chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), and diabetic neuropathy. Clinically, S1PR modulators are already approved for multiple sclerosis (fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, ponesimod), where they reduce central neuroinflammation and have been associated with improvements in pain outcomes. However, the direct translation of S1PR‑targeted therapies to primary neuropathic pain conditions has been limited, and the complex receptor subtype‑specific roles, along with the cardiovascular and immunological side effects of S1PR modulators, demand highly selective and cell‑targeted strategies.

## 🧬 The S1P System: Synthesis, Receptors, and Normal Functions

*   **Synthesis and degradation**: S1P is not stored in vesicles but is produced on demand. Its synthesis is catalysed by two sphingosine kinase isoforms:
    *   **SphK1**: Primarily cytosolic; activated by growth factors, cytokines, and calcium signalling. Translocates to the plasma membrane, where it phosphorylates sphingosine to S1P. SphK1‑derived S1P is largely exported and acts in an autocrine/paracrine manner, promoting cell survival and proliferation.
    *   **SphK2**: Located in the nucleus, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum. It produces intracellular S1P that can directly modulate intracellular targets (e.g., HDACs, TRAF2) or be exported after conversion.
    *   S1P is irreversibly degraded by **S1P lyase** (SGPL1) or reversibly dephosphorylated back to sphingosine by **S1P phosphatases** (SGPP1/2). The balance between synthesis and degradation tightly controls local S1P concentrations.
*   **S1P chaperones and export**: Because S1P is amphipathic, it requires carrier proteins for transport in aqueous environments. In blood and lymph, **apolipoprotein M (ApoM)‑bound HDL** carries the largest pool of S1P, while albumin carries a smaller fraction. In tissues, **fatty acid‑binding proteins** and other transporters facilitate its release.
*   **S1P receptors (S1PR1–5)** : S1P signals through five high‑affinity GPCRs that couple to distinct heterotrimeric G‑proteins, leading to diverse and sometimes opposing cellular effects:
    *   **S1PR1**: Couples exclusively to **Gαi/o**. Mediates **anti‑inflammatory** signalling (e.g., through Rac activation), promotes endothelial barrier integrity, and is essential for lymphocyte egress from lymphoid organs. Highly expressed on T‑ and B‑cells, endothelial cells, and some neurons and astrocytes.
    *   **S1PR2**: Couples to **Gα12/13** (RhoA/ROCK activation), **Gαq/11** (PLC/Ca²⁺), and **Gαi**. Drives **pro‑inflammatory** responses, vascular permeability, demyelination, and astrogliosis. Expressed on oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, microglia, and some neurons.
    *   **S1PR3**: Couples to **Gαq/11**, **Gαi**, and **Gα12/13**. Promotes inflammation, fibrosis, and calcium mobilisation. Expressed on astrocytes, microglia, and some neurons.
    *   **S1PR4**: Expressed primarily on lymphoid and dendritic cells; its role in pain is less well defined.
    *   **S1PR5**: Predominantly expressed on oligodendrocytes in the CNS and some neuronal populations; involved in myelination.
*   **Tissue and cellular distribution in the nociceptive system**:
    *   **Primary sensory neurons (DRG)** : S1PR1, S1PR2, and S1PR3 are expressed on DRG neurons and satellite glial cells. S1PR2 is particularly enriched in small‑ and medium‑sized nociceptors, where it mediates pronociceptive calcium signalling.
    *   **Spinal dorsal horn**: S1PR1, S1PR2, and S1PR3 are expressed on dorsal horn neurons, microglia, and astrocytes. After nerve injury, S1PR2 and S1PR3 are upregulated in glial cells, contributing to central sensitisation.
    *   **Peripheral nerve**: Schwann cells express S1PR2 and S1PR3, which promote demyelination and inflammation after injury.
    *   **Supraspinal regions**: S1PRs are widely distributed in the brain, including the periaqueductal grey, thalamus, and cortex, where they modulate descending pain pathways and affective dimensions of pain.
*   **Physiological functions**: The S1P/S1PR axis is essential for vascular development, immune cell trafficking, endothelial barrier integrity, myelination, and neurogenesis. S1PR1, in particular, maintains the tightness of the blood‑brain barrier (BBB) and blood‑spinal cord barrier (BSCB). In the immune system, S1PR1 is required for lymphocytes to exit lymph nodes into the circulation; its blockade leads to lymphopenia, a mechanism exploited therapeutically.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of S1P in Neuropathic Pain

Following peripheral nerve injury, the S1P/S1PR axis is persistently dysregulated at multiple anatomical compartments, where it drives pain through receptor‑specific mechanisms that promote neuroinflammation, demyelination, glial activation, and central sensitisation.

### 1. Upregulation of S1P and Shift in Receptor Expression

*   **Increased S1P levels**: Sphingosine kinase 1 (SphK1) is activated and upregulated at the site of peripheral nerve injury and in the spinal cord, leading to a local increase in S1P production. Elevated S1P contributes to the pronociceptive signalling environment.
*   **Receptor expression shift**: The normal balance of S1PR subtypes is disrupted after nerve injury. **S1PR2 and S1PR3** are markedly upregulated in spinal microglia, astrocytes, and DRG neurons, while **S1PR1** may be downregulated or functionally antagonised. This pathological shift from predominantly anti‑inflammatory (S1PR1) to pro‑inflammatory (S1PR2/3) signalling is a critical determinant of pain chronicity.

### 2. S1PR1: The Double‑Edged Sword

S1PR1 plays a complex role in neuropathic pain that is highly context‑dependent.

*   **Anti‑inflammatory and barrier‑protective roles**: In endothelial cells, S1PR1 activation stabilises adherens junctions and tight junctions, maintaining the integrity of the BBB and BSCB. Loss of S1PR1 signalling (due to receptor internalisation or antagonism) increases vascular permeability, facilitating immune cell infiltration and the entry of pronociceptive mediators into the CNS. This is one reason why prolonged, complete S1PR1 blockade could theoretically exacerbate central inflammation, despite its immunosuppressive effects.
*   **Functional antagonism by fingolimod**: Fingolimod‑phosphate (the active metabolite of fingolimod) initially activates S1PR1 but then induces its sustained internalisation and degradation, creating a state of **functional antagonism**. This traps lymphocytes in lymph nodes, reducing neuroinflammation. In the CNS, chronic fingolimod‑phosphate can also modulate S1PR1 on astrocytes and microglia, generally reducing pro‑inflammatory cytokine production.
*   **Pro‑nociceptive role of S1PR1 in nociceptors**: Recent evidence indicates that S1PR1 is expressed on a subset of nociceptors, where its activation can directly excite these neurons and contribute to acute pain. S1PR1 on nociceptors may mediate the initial pain response to S1P. Chronic functional antagonism with fingolimod reduces this excitatory input.
*   **S1PR1 in neuropathic pain models**: Intrathecal or systemic administration of fingolimod has consistently been shown to reduce mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in CCI, SNI, and chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy models. The analgesic effects are associated with reduced spinal microglial and astrocytic activation, decreased pro‑inflammatory cytokine levels, and preservation of the BSCB.

### 3. S1PR2: A Major Pronociceptive Receptor

S1PR2 is emerging as the dominant pronociceptive S1P receptor subtype in the nervous system.

*   **Expression on nociceptors**: S1PR2 is highly expressed on small‑ and medium‑sized DRG neurons, where it colocalises with TRPV1 and Nav1.8. Activation of S1PR2 by S1P directly excites nociceptors via Gα12/13‑RhoA‑mediated signalling and Gαq‑mediated calcium mobilisation, increasing action potential firing.
*   **Demyelination and Schwann cell pathology**: S1PR2 on Schwann cells and oligodendrocytes promotes demyelination through the activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway and the induction of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Demyelination of primary afferent fibres is a key driver of mechanical allodynia in neuropathic pain, as discussed in the context of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) and Nogo‑A. S1PR2 is a central mediator of this process.
*   **Glial activation**: In spinal microglia and astrocytes, S1PR2 activation drives the production of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, and reactive oxygen species, amplifying neuroinflammation and central sensitisation. S1PR2‑selective antagonists have been shown to suppress microglial activation and alleviate pain in rodent models.
*   **Vascular permeability**: S1PR2 increases vascular permeability by disrupting endothelial tight junctions, counteracting the barrier‑stabilising effects of S1PR1. This facilitates the extravasation of immune cells and DAMPs into the spinal cord and DRG.

### 4. S1PR3: Astrogliosis and Neuroinflammation

S1PR3 is functionally similar to S1PR2 in many respects and is also pronociceptive.

*   **Astrocytic activation**: S1PR3 is upregulated on reactive astrocytes after nerve injury. Its activation stimulates astrogliosis, the release of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1), and the downregulation of glutamate transporters (GLT‑1), all of which sustain central sensitisation.
*   **Calcium signalling in neurons**: S1PR3 is Gαq‑coupled and can directly increase intracellular calcium in spinal dorsal horn neurons, enhancing excitability and contributing to wind‑up and long‑term potentiation.
*   **Redundancy with S1PR2**: S1PR2 and S1PR3 often co‑operate to drive neuroinflammation, and dual blockade may be required for maximal analgesia.

### 5. Fingolimod as a Broad‑Spectrum S1PR Modulator

Fingolimod (FTY720) is the most extensively studied S1P‑targeting drug in pain.

*   **Mechanism**: Fingolimod is phosphorylated *in vivo* by SphK2 to fingolimod‑phosphate, which acts as a non‑selective agonist at S1PR1, S1PR3, S1PR4, and S1PR5 (not S1PR2). It induces sustained internalisation and degradation of S1PR1, creating functional antagonism. Fingolimod‑phosphate also modulates S1PR3 and S1PR5.
*   **Preclinical analgesia**: Systemic or intrathecal administration of fingolimod consistently reduces mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and cold allodynia in models of:
    *   Chronic constriction injury (CCI)
    *   Spared nerve injury (SNI)
    *   Spinal nerve ligation (SNL)
    *   Paclitaxel‑ and vincristine‑induced CIPN
    *   Streptozotocin‑induced diabetic neuropathy
*   **Mechanisms of analgesia**: Fingolimod’s analgesic effects are multifactorial:
    *   **Reduced central neuroinflammation**: Fingolimod‑phosphate suppresses the activation of spinal microglia and astrocytes, reduces the expression of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6, iNOS, and COX‑2, and promotes a shift towards an M2‑like, anti‑inflammatory glial phenotype.
    *   **Preservation of the BSCB**: By stabilising S1PR1 (before internalisation) and reducing S1PR2/S1PR3‑mediated permeability, fingolimod helps maintain BSCB integrity, limiting the entry of circulating immune cells and pronociceptive molecules.
    *   **Reduction in peripheral immune cell infiltration**: The lymphopenic effect of fingolimod reduces the number of circulating T‑cells and monocytes available to infiltrate the injured nerve and DRG, dampening peripheral neuroinflammation.
    *   **Direct effects on nociceptors**: Fingolimod‑phosphate can directly inhibit nociceptor excitability, possibly through S1PR1 functional antagonism on DRG neurons.
    *   **Restoration of GABAergic inhibition**: By suppressing microglial BDNF release, fingolimod indirectly preserves KCC2 function, maintaining the inhibitory efficacy of GABA and glycine.

### 6. SphK1/SphK2 and S1P Lyase: Modulating the S1P Tone

*   **SphK1 inhibitors**: Systemic or local administration of SphK1 inhibitors reduces S1P production at the site of nerve injury, decreasing nociceptor sensitisation and inflammatory cell recruitment. The SphK1‑selective inhibitor **PF‑543** has shown analgesic effects in rodent models of inflammatory pain and some neuropathic pain paradigms.
*   **SphK2 inhibitors**: May have a more complex effect, as SphK2‑derived S1P is involved in intracellular signalling and can have both pro‑ and anti‑nociceptive roles depending on the context. In some models, SphK2 inhibition reduces pain; in others, it can exacerbate it.
*   **S1P lyase inhibitors**: By blocking S1P degradation, S1P lyase inhibitors increase S1P levels, which can paradoxically produce analgesia in certain pain models, likely through sustained S1PR1 activation. However, they can also raise S1P to toxic levels and are not yet clinically viable for pain.

### 7. S1P in Chemotherapy‑Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN)

*   Chemotherapeutic agents (paclitaxel, vincristine, bortezomib) upregulate SphK1 activity in the DRG and peripheral nerves, increasing local S1P production.
*   Fingolimod has been shown to prevent and reverse paclitaxel‑ and vincristine‑induced mechanical and cold allodynia in rodents. It reduces mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and the infiltration of macrophages into the DRG.
*   Selective S1PR2 antagonists are also effective in CIPN models, highlighting the pronociceptive role of this receptor subtype.

### 8. Central Neuropathic Pain and Spinal Cord Injury

*   After spinal cord injury (SCI), S1PR2 and S1PR3 are upregulated in the spinal cord, and S1P levels increase locally. Fingolimod reduces SCI‑induced mechanical allodynia, limits the spread of secondary injury, and improves functional recovery when administered early after injury.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### 1. Fingolimod (FTY720) and Other Non‑Selective S1PR Modulators

*   **Fingolimod**: As described above, consistently analgesic in a wide range of neuropathic pain models.
*   **Siponimod (BAF312)** : An S1PR1‑ and S1PR5‑selective modulator approved for secondary progressive MS. In preclinical models, siponimod reduces mechanical allodynia and neuroinflammation in EAE and some neuropathic pain models, with potentially fewer cardiac side effects than fingolimod.
*   **Ozanimod (RPC1063)** : An S1PR1‑ and S1PR5‑selective modulator approved for relapsing MS and ulcerative colitis. Preclinical pain data are limited but consistent with S1PR1‑mediated immunomodulation.
*   **Ponesimod**: Another S1PR1‑selective modulator approved for MS; limited preclinical pain data.

### 2. Subtype‑Selective S1PR Antagonists/Modulators

*   **S1PR2 antagonists** (e.g., **JTE‑013**, **compound 21**): These have been shown to reduce mechanical allodynia, neuroinflammation, and demyelination in CCI, SNI, and diabetic neuropathy models. They block S1PR2‑mediated calcium influx in DRG neurons and suppress the activation of the RhoA/ROCK pathway in Schwann cells and glial cells.
*   **S1PR3 antagonists** (e.g., **TY‑52156**, **CAY10444**): Reduce astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and pain in some models. Dual S1PR2/S1PR3 antagonism is being explored for synergistic analgesia.
*   **S1PR1 agonists that do not cause internalisation**: Are theoretically desirable for maintaining barrier integrity and anti‑inflammatory effects without lymphopenia. Research is ongoing.

### 3. Sphingosine Kinase Inhibitors

*   **SphK1 inhibitors** (e.g., **PF‑543**, **SKI‑II**): Reduce S1P production and have shown analgesic effects in inflammatory pain and some neuropathic pain models, but their development for pain is not as advanced as receptor modulators.
*   **Dual SphK1/SphK2 inhibitors**: May be more effective but carry greater risk of toxicity due to the essential roles of S1P in development and vascular homeostasis.

### 4. Genetic Models

*   **S1PR2 knockout mice**: Exhibit reduced mechanical allodynia and neuroinflammation after nerve injury and are protected from demyelination‑induced pain.
*   **S1PR3 knockout mice**: Show attenuated astrogliosis and reduced central sensitisation after nerve injury.
*   **S1PR1 conditional knockouts**: Lymphocyte‑specific S1PR1 deletion recapitulates the lymphopenic effect of fingolimod and reduces EAE severity, but pain‑specific studies are limited.
*   **SphK1 knockout mice**: Display reduced S1P levels and attenuated inflammatory hyperalgesia, but the effects on neuropathic pain are model‑dependent.

### 5. Natural Compounds

*   **Resveratrol** and **curcumin**: Have been shown to inhibit SphK1 activity and reduce S1P levels, contributing to their analgesic effects.
*   **Fingolimod** is a synthetic derivative of **myriocin**, a natural sphingolipid from the fungus *Isaria sinclairii*.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **S1PR modulators in multiple sclerosis and pain**: Fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, and ponesimod are approved for multiple sclerosis (MS), where they reduce relapse rates, MRI lesion activity, and brain volume loss. MS is frequently accompanied by central neuropathic pain, trigeminal neuralgia, and painful tonic spasms. Clinical trials and post‑hoc analyses have shown that **fingolimod reduces pain severity in patients with MS**, including those with neuropathic pain. This provides clinical proof of concept that S1PR modulation can reduce pain in a neuroinflammatory condition.
*   **Direct clinical trials in primary neuropathic pain**: Despite the strong preclinical rationale, there are **no published Phase II/III clinical trials** of S1PR modulators specifically for primary neuropathic pain conditions (e.g., painful diabetic neuropathy, post‑herpetic neuralgia, traumatic nerve injury). This represents a significant gap. The clinical development of fingolimod for pain has been hampered by its side‑effect profile and the perception that its immunosuppressive effects are too broad for non‑life‑threatening conditions.
*   **Fingolimod for other neuropathic pain types**:
    *   A small clinical study reported that fingolimod reduced pain and opioid use in patients with **complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)** , but controlled trials are lacking.
    *   A clinical trial of fingolimod for **chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN)** has been proposed but not yet reported.
    *   Case reports have described pain relief with fingolimod in **trigeminal neuralgia** and **central post‑stroke pain**.
*   **Subtype‑selective S1PR modulators**: The development of **S1PR2‑selective antagonists** or **S1PR3‑selective antagonists** that do not cause lymphopenia (which is S1PR1‑dependent) could provide analgesia without immunosuppression. Similarly, **S1PR1 agonists that do not induce internalisation** could maintain barrier integrity and anti‑inflammatory effects without lymphocyte trapping. These are active areas of preclinical research.
*   **S1P lyase inhibitors**: While still in early stages, increasing S1P levels with lyase inhibitors could theoretically enhance protective S1PR1 signalling. However, the risk of S1P‑mediated toxicity (pulmonary edema, vascular leak) is a major concern.
*   **Biomarker potential**: S1P levels in cerebrospinal fluid or plasma are elevated in patients with MS and some chronic pain conditions. S1P and the S1PR expression pattern on peripheral blood mononuclear cells could serve as biomarkers for patient stratification.
*   **Combination strategies**:
    *   **S1PR modulator + KCC2 enhancer**: Fingolimod reduces BDNF‑driven KCC2 downregulation, so combining with a direct KCC2 activator could synergistically restore inhibition.
    *   **S1PR modulator + anti‑TNF or anti‑IL‑6 biologics**: S1PR modulation reduces, but does not eliminate, neuroinflammation; co‑therapy could provide broader cytokine suppression.
    *   **S1PR2 antagonist + Nav channel blocker**: Combining reduced nociceptor excitability (S1PR2 blockade) with sodium channel inhibition could achieve multimodal analgesia.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **Lymphopenia and immunosuppression**: The clinical hallmark of fingolimod and other S1PR1 modulators is a dose‑dependent reduction in circulating lymphocytes (to ~20–30% of baseline) due to their trapping in lymph nodes. This increases the risk of infections, particularly viral infections (e.g., herpes zoster, influenza), and requires vaccination and monitoring. The immunosuppressive effect makes chronic use for non‑life‑threatening pain conditions problematic. S1PR2/3‑selective antagonists that spare S1PR1 would avoid lymphopenia.
*   **First‑dose bradycardia**: Fingolimod causes a transient, dose‑dependent decrease in heart rate (nadir ~4–5 hours post‑dose) due to S1PR1 activation on atrial myocytes. This requires first‑dose monitoring and is a barrier to widespread use. Second‑generation S1PR1 modulators (siponimod, ozanimod) have a slower onset and reduced cardiac effects, but still require dose titration.
*   **Macular edema**: Fingolimod and other S1PR modulators can cause macular edema, particularly in patients with diabetes or uveitis, requiring ophthalmologic monitoring.
*   **Receptor subtype complexity**: S1PR1 can be either pronociceptive or antinociceptive depending on its location (immune cells, endothelial cells, nociceptors). S1PR2 and S1PR3 are generally pronociceptive but may have protective functions in certain contexts. The net effect of global S1PR modulation is difficult to predict and depends on the specific compound, dose, timing, and pain aetiology.
*   **S1P and cancer**: S1P promotes cell proliferation, survival, and angiogenesis, and elevated S1P levels are associated with tumorigenesis. Fingolimod is contraindicated in patients with active malignancies. The long‑term risk of S1P modulation in patients without cancer is unknown.
*   **Central vs. peripheral effects**: Fingolimod crosses the BBB and has central effects, which may be necessary for its analgesic efficacy in central neuropathic pain. Peripherally restricted S1PR modulators might be safer but may not fully address spinal and supraspinal pain mechanisms.
*   **Sex differences**: Preclinical studies have used both male and female animals, but sex‑specific analyses of S1PR modulator efficacy in pain models are limited. Hormonal influences on S1P metabolism and receptor expression have been reported and could affect therapeutic responses.
*   **Interactions with the wider pain network**: The S1P system is integrated with many of the pathways discussed in this series:
    *   **TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6**: S1PR2/3 activation induces these cytokines, and they in turn upregulate SphK1, creating a positive‑feedback loop. Fingolimod breaks this cycle.
    *   **BDNF‑TrkB‑KCC2**: S1P‑induced glial activation promotes BDNF release, leading to KCC2 downregulation. Fingolimod indirectly preserves KCC2.
    *   **RhoA/ROCK**: S1PR2 signals through Gα12/13 to activate RhoA/ROCK, which drives demyelination, cytoskeletal changes, and MMP activation—directly linking S1P to this central pronociceptive pathway.
    *   **MMP‑9 and perineuronal nets**: S1PR2‑RhoA‑mediated MMP‑9 release degrades perineuronal nets, contributing to PV interneuron dysfunction and disinhibition.
    *   **LPA**: The S1P and LPA systems are closely related bioactive lipids, often with overlapping and synergistic pronociceptive effects. LPA can transactivate S1P receptors in some contexts.
    *   **Autophagy and mTOR**: S1P can modulate mTORC1 activity, and S1PR modulators may indirectly influence autophagy and mitochondrial quality control.
    *   **TRPV1**: S1P, via S1PR2, sensitises TRPV1 through PKC‑mediated phosphorylation, enhancing thermal hyperalgesia.
    *   **Blood‑brain/Blood‑spinal cord barrier**: S1PR1 maintains barrier integrity; S1PR2 disrupts it. The balance between these receptors determines vascular permeability and the degree of central immune cell infiltration.
