# Involvement of TRPV1 in Neuropathic Pain

The transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channel is a non‑selective cation channel that functions as a polymodal detector of noxious heat, acidic pH, and endogenous lipid mediators such as anandamide. It is predominantly expressed on primary nociceptive sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), trigeminal ganglia, and vagal ganglia, where it plays a pivotal role in the transduction and transmission of nociceptive signals.[reference:0] In the context of neuropathic pain, TRPV1 exhibits a striking dual role—acting both as a driver of pain through its **sensitisation** and as a mediator of analgesia through its **desensitisation** and defunctionalisation.[reference:1] Nerve injury or metabolic stress leads to the post‑translational sensitisation of TRPV1 via phosphorylation by protein kinase A (PKA), protein kinase C (PKC), and Ca²⁺/calmodulin‑dependent kinase II (CaMKII), which lowers its activation threshold and enhances its activity at body temperature.[reference:2][reference:3] Sensitised TRPV1 facilitates spontaneous nociceptor activity, amplifies neurotransmitter release (e.g., glutamate, CGRP) in the spinal dorsal horn, and promotes central sensitisation.[reference:4] Conversely, prolonged or high‑intensity activation of TRPV1 by agonists such as capsaicin leads to a state of profound channel desensitisation, characterised by reduced calcium influx, depletion of neuropeptides, and in some cases reversible nerve terminal degeneration—a phenomenon exploited therapeutically with topical capsaicin patches.[reference:5][reference:6]

## 🧬 TRPV1: Structure, Localisation, and Physiological Functions

*   **Channel structure**: TRPV1 is a homotetrameric, six‑transmembrane domain channel with a pore‑loop between S5 and S6. It contains an N‑terminal ankyrin repeat domain and a C‑terminal calmodulin‑binding and PIP₂‑interacting region.
*   **Ion selectivity**: TRPV1 is a non‑selective cation channel with a high relative permeability to Ca²⁺ (PCa/PNa ≈ 10). Calcium entry through TRPV1 is critical both for the acute excitation of nociceptors and for the calcium‑dependent desensitisation that limits sustained channel activity.[reference:7][reference:8]
*   **Endogenous activators**:
    *   Noxious heat (threshold ≈ 43 °C)
    *   Extracellular protons (pH < 6)
    *   Endovanilloids (anandamide, N‑arachidonoyl‑dopamine, 12‑ and 15‑HPETE)
    *   Depolarisation‑induced activation
*   **Tissue distribution**: TRPV1 is expressed in approximately 60–70% of small‑ and medium‑diameter DRG neurons (C‑fibre and Aδ‑fibre nociceptors). It is also present in non‑neuronal cells including epidermal keratinocytes, vascular smooth muscle, and immune cells, where it modulates inflammation and vascular tone.
*   **Physiological role**: TRPV1 is essential for thermal nociception and the development of thermal hyperalgesia during inflammation. It also contributes to nociceptor development and the maintenance of nociceptive phenotype.

## 🔬 Mechanisms of TRPV1 Dysregulation in Neuropathic Pain

TRPV1 undergoes profound changes in expression, trafficking, and post‑translational modification after peripheral nerve injury, which collectively shift the channel into a sensitised state that promotes spontaneous pain and stimulus‑evoked hypersensitivity.

### 1. Sensitisation by Phosphorylation and Inflammatory Mediators

The primary mechanism by which TRPV1 promotes neuropathic pain is through its sensitisation—a reduction in the activation threshold and an increase in channel open probability at a given stimulus intensity.

*   **PKA‑mediated phosphorylation**: Pro‑inflammatory mediators (e.g., prostaglandin E₂, bradykinin) act on G‑protein‑coupled receptors, elevating cAMP and activating PKA. PKA phosphorylates TRPV1 at Ser<sup>116</sup> and Thr<sup>370</sup>, reducing the heat threshold and enhancing the response to capsaicin and protons.[reference:9]
*   **PKC‑mediated phosphorylation**: PKCε, activated downstream of bradykinin and nerve growth factor (NGF) receptors, phosphorylates TRPV1 at Ser<sup>800</sup>. This sensitises the channel, particularly in the context of inflammation and nerve injury, and is crucial for the development of thermal and mechanical hypersensitivity.[reference:10][reference:11]
*   **CaMKII‑mediated phosphorylation**: Elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ activates CaMKII, which phosphorylates TRPV1 and contributes to activity‑dependent sensitisation.
*   **AKAP79 scaffolding**: The scaffolding protein AKAP79 (A‑kinase anchoring protein 79) organises PKA, PKC, and protein phosphatase 2B (calcineurin) into a signalling complex with TRPV1, enabling rapid and localised modulation of channel function.[reference:12]
*   **NGF‑TrkA signalling**: NGF, released from damaged tissue and Schwann cells, binds to TrkA receptors on nociceptors, initiating PI3K‑ and MAPK‑dependent signalling that increases TRPV1 trafficking to the plasma membrane and enhances its transcription.

### 2. Upregulation of TRPV1 Expression and Epigenetic Regulation

*   **Increased mRNA and protein levels**: TRPV1 mRNA and protein are upregulated in DRG neurons and the spinal dorsal horn following peripheral nerve injury (e.g., chronic constriction injury, spinal nerve ligation), as well as in models of diabetic neuropathy (DPN) and chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN).[reference:13]
*   **Epigenetic mechanisms**:
    *   **Tbx5‑dependent transcription**: Nerve injury induces the transcription factor Tbx5 in the spinal dorsal horn. Tbx5 promotes TRPV1 gene transcription via epigenetic remodelling (increased H3K9 acetylation at the *Trpv1* promoter) and simultaneously degrades the ubiquitin ligase Fbxl2, which otherwise targets TRPV1 for proteasomal degradation. This dual mechanism leads to sustained TRPV1 upregulation in central pain pathways and contributes to mechanical allodynia.[reference:14]
    *   **G9a‑mediated bivalent histone marks**: The histone methyltransferase G9a deposits H3K9me2 marks at the *Trpv1* and *Trpa1* gene loci in DRG neurons. Inflammatory stimuli alter this bivalent chromatin state, favouring active transcription and sustained upregulation of TRPV1.[reference:15]
    *   **DNA methylation**: The *Trpv1* promoter is subject to DNA methylation (CpG methylation) which is dynamically regulated after nerve injury, affecting transcription factor access and gene expression.[reference:16]

### 3. Role in Specific Neuropathic Pain Conditions

*   **Chemotherapy‑induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN)** : TRPV1 is critically implicated in oxaliplatin‑, paclitaxel‑, and bortezomib‑induced neuropathy. Paclitaxel directly sensitises TRPV1 on human and rodent sensory neurons through a TLR4‑dependent mechanism, independently of changes in channel expression.[reference:17][reference:18] Oxaliplatin enhances TRPV1 membrane trafficking and sensitises the channel through ROS‑dependent pathways.[reference:19]
*   **Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN)** : Hyperglycaemia‑induced oxidative stress, advanced glycation end‑products, and inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β) sensitise TRPV1 in DRG neurons and contribute to mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia.[reference:20] Methylglyoxal, a reactive dicarbonyl elevated in diabetes, directly activates and sensitises TRPV1.
*   **Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN)** and **HIV‑associated neuropathy**: TRPV1 expression is increased in affected DRG and skin nerve fibres, correlating with pain severity. Capsaicin‑induced defunctionalisation is clinically effective in these conditions.

### 4. Mitochondrial Dysfunction and TRPV1‑Mediated Excitotoxicity

TRPV1‑mediated Ca²⁺ influx has a privileged crosstalk with the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) and the mitochondrial Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCLX).[reference:21] Under physiological conditions, mitochondria buffer TRPV1‑induced Ca²⁺ loads, supporting ATP synthesis and regulating Ca²⁺‑dependent desensitisation of the channel.[reference:22]

In the setting of neuropathic pain, sustained or excessive TRPV1 activation overwhelms mitochondrial Ca²⁺ handling:
*   TRPV1‑mediated Ca²⁺ entry drives mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload, causing opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS).[reference:23][reference:24]
*   Mitochondrial ROS, in turn, further sensitise TRPV1 (oxidative sensitisation), creating a self‑amplifying positive‑feedback loop that drives nociceptor hyperexcitability, excitotoxicity, and neuronal apoptosis.[reference:25]
*   A genome‑wide CRISPRi screen revealed that reduced expression of mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) components protects against TRPV1‑mediated excitotoxicity, indicating that mitochondrial metabolism tunes the resilience of nociceptors to TRPV1‑induced damage.[reference:26] Intriguingly, TRPV1‑positive sensory neurons maintain lower baseline ETC expression, which may be an intrinsic protective mechanism.[reference:27]
*   Schwann cell dysfunction (e.g., via LRP1 deletion) disrupts neuron–glia metabolic coupling, impairs mitochondrial function in DRG neurons, and sensitises TRPV1 and TRPA1 channels, providing a non‑cell‑autonomous mechanism linking glial pathology to TRPV1 hyperactivity.[reference:28]

### 5. Interaction with TRPA1 and Other Channels

TRPV1 is co‑expressed with TRPA1 in a subpopulation of nociceptors, and the two channels physically interact.[reference:29] This TRPV1–TRPA1 complex allows for cross‑sensitisation: TRPV1 activation can potentiate TRPA1 responses, and vice versa.[reference:30][reference:31]

*   Reactive oxygen species (ROS) simultaneously activate and sensitise both TRPV1 and TRPA1 in the spinal dorsal horn, enhancing excitatory synaptic transmission and contributing to central sensitisation.[reference:32]
*   Dual TRPV1/TRPA1 antagonists (e.g., SZV 1287) have demonstrated superior efficacy compared to selective antagonists in some neuropathic pain models.[reference:33]
*   TRPV1 also functionally interacts with voltage‑gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8), N‑type calcium channels, and the ATP‑gated P2X3 receptor, forming a nociceptive signalosome.

### 6. Spinal and Supraspinal TRPV1 in Central Sensitisation

*   **Spinal dorsal horn**: TRPV1 is expressed presynaptically on the central terminals of primary afferent nociceptors, where it enhances glutamate and CGRP release upon activation. Capsaicin‑induced TRPV1 activation in the spinal cord triggers exaggerated glutamate release, which is subsequently downregulated during desensitisation.[reference:34] TRPV1 expression in spinal neurons and glial cells is upregulated after nerve injury and contributes to the development of mechanical allodynia through both presynaptic and postsynaptic mechanisms.[reference:35]
*   **Periaqueductal grey (PAG)** : TRPV1 is expressed in the ventrolateral PAG, a key node in descending pain modulation. Activation of TRPV1 in the PAG by the paracetamol metabolite AM404 produces central analgesia through the engagement of OFF‑cell activity and suppression of ON‑cell firing in the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM).[reference:36] This central TRPV1‑mediated analgesia is a distinct mechanism from peripheral nociceptor sensitisation.

### 7. Cannabinoid Modulation of TRPV1

Endocannabinoids (e.g., anandamide) and phytocannabinoids (e.g., cannabidiol) directly activate or modulate TRPV1 independently of cannabinoid CB1/CB2 receptors.[reference:37]

*   Anandamide is a full agonist of TRPV1, and its activation of the channel on nociceptors contributes to both pronociceptive (acute pain) and antinociceptive (desensitisation) effects.
*   Cannabidiol (CBD) indirectly modulates TRPV1 through the activation of 5‑HT₁A receptors and PPARγ, as well as via direct low‑affinity agonism. This provides an alternative pathway for cannabinoid‑based analgesia.

## 🔬 Preclinical Evidence of Modulation

### TRPV1 Agonists (Desensitisation Strategy)

*   **Capsaicin**: Acute application of capsaicin excites nociceptors (causing initial burning pain), but repeated or high‑dose application leads to a long‑lasting, reversible defunctionalisation of TRPV1‑expressing nerve terminals. This is the basis for the clinical use of capsaicin 8% patches.[reference:38][reference:39]
    *   Capsaicin‑induced desensitisation involves: calcium‑dependent calmodulin binding to the TRPV1 C‑terminus; activation of the phosphatase calcineurin, which dephosphorylates TRPV1; depletion of neuropeptides (substance P, CGRP); and, at high concentrations, reversible nerve terminal degeneration (defunctionalisation).[reference:40]
    *   Presynaptic capsaicin desensitisation in the spinal dorsal horn downregulates both synchronous glutamate release and postsynaptic responses, transiently increasing the pain threshold.[reference:41]

*   **Resiniferatoxin (RTX)** : An ultrapotent capsaicin analogue that produces a more profound and longer‑lasting defunctionalisation of TRPV1‑expressing neurons. RTX is being explored for intrathecal administration in cancer pain and severe neuropathic pain, providing sustained analgesia lasting weeks to months from a single injection.[reference:42]

### TRPV1 Antagonists

*   **AS1928370**: A novel TRPV1 antagonist that prevents ligand‑induced (capsaicin) activation but spares proton‑induced activation. Intrathecal AS1928370 attenuates mechanical allodynia in rodent models of neuropathic pain *without* producing the hyperthermia characteristic of earlier‑generation antagonists. This modality‑selective antagonism represents a strategy to achieve analgesia while preserving the physiological thermoregulatory function of TRPV1.[reference:43][reference:44]
*   **AMG 517**: A highly selective, systemic TRPV1 antagonist that produced marked, reversible hyperthermia (up to 40.2 °C) in Phase I clinical trials, illustrating the critical role of TRPV1 in human thermoregulation and the challenge of developing systemic TRPV1 blockers.[reference:45]
*   **Other antagonists**: Numerous compounds (SB‑705498, AZD‑1386, JNJ‑39439335/mavatrep, XEN‑D0501) have entered clinical trials. Most were discontinued due to hyperthermia, impaired heat sensation (risk of burns), or insufficient efficacy in non‑heat‑related pain endpoints.
*   **siRNA targeting TRPV1**: Small interfering RNA that selectively downregulates TRPV1 expression in DRG neurons has shown efficacy in preclinical CIPN models, reducing mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity without affecting baseline nociception. This approach avoids the hyperthermia associated with pharmacological antagonists because it does not block the channel's tonic thermoregulatory function acutely but rather reduces channel density over days to weeks.[reference:46][reference:47]

### Genetic Approaches

*   **TRPV1 global knockout mice**: *Trpv1*‑/‑ mice exhibit normal baseline mechanical and thermal sensitivity but fail to develop thermal hyperalgesia after inflammation. They show attenuated mechanical allodynia in some neuropathic pain models, although the dependence on TRPV1 varies by injury type and modality.
*   **TRPV1 peptide aptamer**: AAV‑encoded TRPV1‑specific peptide aptamers delivered to DRG neurons interfere with TRPV1 signalling and attenuate neuropathic pain.[reference:48]

### Electroacupuncture and Complementary Approaches

*   Electroacupuncture alleviates neuropathic pain in part by inhibiting TRPV1 expression and function in DRG neurons and the spinal dorsal horn, reducing CGRP release and central sensitisation.

## 🩺 Clinical Implications and Therapeutic Opportunities

*   **Capsaicin 8% patch (Qutenza)** :
    *   FDA‑approved for neuropathic pain associated with postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) and diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) of the feet.[reference:49]
    *   A single 30‑ to 60‑minute application produces analgesia lasting up to 12 weeks. The capsaicin patch works by reversibly defunctionalising TRPV1‑expressing nociceptors, depleting substance P and CGRP, and inducing a long‑lasting refractory state.
    *   Clinical trials demonstrate a ~30% reduction in average pain scores compared to low‑dose capsaicin control patches. The most common adverse effects are transient application‑site reactions (erythema, pain, pruritus).[reference:50]
    *   Capsaicin patches are also being investigated for spinal cord injury‑associated focal neuropathic pain, HIV‑associated neuropathy, and chemotherapy‑induced neuropathy.

*   **ACD440 (topical TRPV1 antagonist)** :
    *   A first‑in‑class, topical TRPV1 antagonist (gel formulation) in Phase II clinical development for chronic peripheral neuropathic pain.[reference:51][reference:52]
    *   Phase IIa data demonstrated that ACD440 reduces temperature‑evoked pain by approximately 50%, a clinically relevant reduction. The topical route limits systemic exposure, maintaining high local drug concentrations while avoiding hyperthermia.[reference:53][reference:54]
    *   ACD440 received orphan drug designation from the FDA for erythromelalgia, a rare condition caused by gain‑of‑function mutations in *SCN9A* (Nav1.7) that produces severe burning pain and heat sensitivity—a phenotype directly linked to TRPV1 activation.[reference:55]
    *   Post‑hoc analyses indicate that ACD440 is particularly effective in patients whose pain has a strong heat‑evoked component, supporting a precision‑medicine approach that matches TRPV1‑targeted therapy to the patient's sensory phenotype.[reference:56]

*   **Qutenza for diabetic peripheral neuropathy**: Represents the most recent FDA expansion (2020) of a TRPV1‑targeted therapy, validating TRPV1 desensitisation as an effective, non‑opioid, non‑systemic approach to neuropathic pain management.[reference:57]

*   **Future directions in TRPV1‑targeted analgesics**:
    *   **Modality‑selective antagonists**: Compounds that block capsaicin‑ and heat‑induced activation but spare proton‑induced activation (e.g., AS1928370) represent a strategy to achieve analgesia without hyperthermia.[reference:58]
    *   **Peripheral‑restricted antagonists**: Topical or peripherally restricted (non‑CNS‑penetrant) TRPV1 antagonists like ACD440 avoid both hyperthermia and central side effects.[reference:59]
    *   **Dual TRPV1/TRPA1 antagonists**: Given the extensive crosstalk between these channels, dual antagonists (e.g., SZV 1287) may provide broader efficacy.[reference:60]
    *   **siRNA and gene‑silencing approaches**: Sustained downregulation of TRPV1 expression using intrathecal siRNA or AAV‑delivered microRNA may provide long‑lasting analgesia without the acute hyperthermia of pharmacological antagonists.
    *   **Cannabinoid‑based TRPV1 modulators**: Non‑pungent cannabinoids that target TRPV1 may offer an alternative to both capsaicin and traditional analgesics.
    *   **Endovanilloid modulation**: Inhibiting the degradation of endovanilloids (e.g., FAAH inhibitors) may enhance endogenous TRPV1‑mediated analgesia.

## ⚠️ A Note of Caution and Complexity

*   **The hyperthermia problem**: Systemic TRPV1 antagonists consistently produce dose‑dependent hyperthermia (increases of 1–2 °C) in humans due to the blockade of tonic TRPV1 signalling in the preoptic area of the hypothalamus, which is essential for thermoregulation.[reference:61][reference:62] This has been the single greatest barrier to clinical development of systemic TRPV1 antagonists. Strategies to circumvent hyperthermia—topical application, peripheral restriction, modality‑selective blockade—are critical to the viability of TRPV1‑targeted analgesics.

*   **Impaired noxious heat sensation**: Systemic TRPV1 antagonism elevates the heat‑pain threshold, increasing the risk of burns and thermal injury. Patients may not perceive dangerously hot surfaces or liquids, leading to accidental tissue damage. This is a serious safety concern for chronic, systemic TRPV1 inhibition.

*   **The dual role of TRPV1: sensitisation vs. desensitisation**: The same channel that drives pain when sensitised can produce analgesia when desensitised. This functional duality means that the therapeutic approach—agonist (desensitisation) or antagonist (blockade)—must be carefully matched to the pain aetiology and the desired outcome. Agonist‑induced desensitisation (capsaicin) is effective for peripheral neuropathic pain, while antagonists may be more suitable for conditions where heat‑evoked pain is a dominant feature.

*   **TRPV1 expression on non‑neuronal cells**: TRPV1 is expressed on keratinocytes, vascular smooth muscle, bladder urothelium, and immune cells. These peripheral sites may contribute to both the therapeutic and adverse effects of TRPV1 modulators. For example, TRPV1 in the bladder regulates micturition reflex, and antagonists may cause urinary retention.

*   **Developmental and compensatory changes**: Long‑term TRPV1 blockade or genetic deletion can lead to compensatory upregulation of other TRP channels (e.g., TRPA1, TRPM3) and altered nociceptor development, potentially limiting the durability of TRPV1‑targeted monotherapy.

*   **Sex differences**:
    *   TRPV1 and TRPA1 upregulation in CIPN models shows no significant sex difference, whereas TRPM8 upregulation is more pronounced in females.[reference:63]
    *   However, a macrophage–IL‑23/IL‑17A–TRPV1 axis has been identified as a female‑specific generator of mechanical allodynia, suggesting that TRPV1 may be differentially engaged in pain processing between the sexes.[reference:64]
    *   Sex hormones (particularly oestrogen) modulate TRPV1 expression and function, potentially contributing to the higher prevalence of chronic pain in women and to sex‑specific responses to TRPV1‑targeted therapies.[reference:65]
    *   A TRPV1 polymorphism (Met315Ile) has been associated with neuropathic pain susceptibility specifically in females.[reference:66]

*   **Interactions with other pain pathways**:
    *   **BDNF–TrkB–KCC2**: TRPV1 activation in nociceptors stimulates BDNF release from primary afferent terminals. This BDNF can act on TrkB receptors on spinal lamina I neurons, downregulating KCC2 and contributing to disinhibition.[reference:67][reference:68] Schwann cell‑derived BDNF also sensitises TRPV1 via PKCε.[reference:69]
    *   **P2X4–microglia–BDNF**: The P2X4–BDNF–TrkB–KCC2 cascade in the spinal cord is a distinct, parallel mechanism of disinhibition, but TRPV1‑positive nociceptors can release ATP upon activation, potentially fuelling the P2X4‑dependent pathway.
    *   **TLR4**: Paclitaxel sensitises TRPV1 through a TLR4‑dependent mechanism, linking innate immune signalling in nociceptors to TRPV1 hyperexcitability.
    *   **TNF‑α and IL‑1β**: Pro‑inflammatory cytokines sensitise TRPV1 through both transcriptional upregulation and post‑translational phosphorylation. In turn, TRPV1‑mediated neurogenic inflammation promotes further cytokine release.
    *   **RhoA/ROCK**: TRPV1 activation can engage RhoA signalling to regulate cytoskeletal dynamics and synaptic plasticity, linking TRPV1 to the RhoA/ROCK pathway.
    *   **HDAC6 and microtubule stability**: HDAC6 inhibition promotes α‑tubulin acetylation and microtubule stability, which may indirectly affect TRPV1 trafficking. TRPV1 is also subject to epigenetic regulation by histone deacetylases and methyltransferases.[reference:70][reference:71]
    *   **TRPA1**: Extensive physical and functional interaction; both channels are co‑regulated and co‑sensitised in neuropathic pain. Dual targeting may be synergistic.
    *   **Nav1.7 and Nav1.8**: PKC‑mediated phosphorylation of TRPV1 and Nav1.8 occurs downstream of common inflammatory mediators, and TRPV1 activity can modulate the voltage dependence of sodium channels, amplifying nociceptor excitability.
